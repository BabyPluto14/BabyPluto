# PART I: CASE – The Impact of Changes in Electricity Prices (10p)

**Context:** A country's main power station permanently breaks down, causing a strong rise in electricity prices. This is a classic **adverse (negative) supply shock**.

---

## Question 1.1 — Initial Long-Run Equilibrium (0.75p)

**Diagram description (label as Time 0):**

```
Price Level (P)
     ↑
     |          LRAS₀
     |            |
 P₀  |----E₀------+
     |   /        |
     |  / SRAS₀   |
     | /           \
     |/     AD₀    \
     +---------------→ Real Output (Y)
                  Y₀=Yₙ
```

**Key elements to draw:**
- **Y-axis:** Price Level (P)
- **X-axis:** Real Output / Real GDP (Y)
- **AD₀:** Downward-sloping Aggregate Demand curve
- **SRAS₀:** Upward-sloping Short-Run Aggregate Supply curve
- **LRAS₀:** Vertical Long-Run Aggregate Supply curve at natural output Yₙ
- **E₀ (Time 0):** All three curves intersect at the same point → long-run equilibrium at (Yₙ, P₀)

**Explanation:** In long-run equilibrium, the economy operates at its **natural rate of output** (Yₙ). Actual and expected prices are equal, unemployment is at its natural rate, and all factors of production are fully utilized. SRAS intersects LRAS and AD at the same point.

---

## Question 1.2 — Short-Run Impact of Permanent Breakdown (1.5p)

**Diagram addition (label as Time 1):**
- **SRAS₀ shifts LEFT/UPWARD → SRAS₁**
- **LRAS₀ also shifts LEFT → LRAS₁** (permanent shock)
- New short-run equilibrium E₁: intersection of AD₀ and SRAS₁ → **lower output Y₁ < Y₀, higher price P₁ > P₀**

**WHY it affects the economy:**

Electricity is a critical **input (natural resource/capital)** used in virtually all production processes. When the main power station breaks down permanently:
1. The **cost of production rises** for firms across the entire economy (higher electricity prices = higher marginal costs)
2. Since this is a **permanent** breakdown, it also reduces the economy's long-run productive capacity → LRAS shifts left to LRAS₁

**HOW it affects the economy (mechanism):**

- Higher production costs mean firms are willing to produce **less output at any given price level** → SRAS shifts **left/upward**
- In the new short-run equilibrium (E₁):
  - **Real output falls** (Y₁ < Yₙ): firms cut production due to higher costs → recession
  - **Price level rises** (P₁ > P₀): higher costs are passed on to consumers via higher prices → inflation
- This simultaneous fall in output and rise in prices is called **stagflation**
- The economy is now operating below the new natural output level

## Question 1.4 — Short-Run Impact on the Phillips Curve (1.5p)

**Diagram description:**
```
Inflation Rate (π)
     ↑
     |     LRPC₀  LRPC₁
     |       |       |
 π₁  |---E₁---+       |
     |  / SRPC₁       |
     | /               |
 π₀  +-E₀--+---SRPC₀  |
     |                  |
     +------------------+---→ Unemployment Rate (u)
        uₙ₀             uₙ₁
```

**Key elements:**
- **X-axis:** Unemployment Rate (u)
- **Y-axis:** Inflation Rate (π)
- **SRPC₀:** Initial downward-sloping Short-Run Phillips Curve (before breakdown)
- **LRPC₀:** Initial vertical Long-Run Phillips Curve at natural rate uₙ₀
- **SRPC₁:** Shifts **upward/left** (adverse supply shock worsens the inflation-unemployment tradeoff)
- **LRPC₁:** Shifts **rightward** to uₙ₁ (if the permanent loss of capital raises structural unemployment)
- **E₀ (Time 0):** On SRPC₀ and LRPC₀ at (uₙ₀, π₀)
- **E₁ (Time 1):** On SRPC₁ — **both inflation AND unemployment rise simultaneously**

**Explanation:**

An adverse supply shock (higher electricity/energy costs) causes **stagflation**:
- **Inflation rises** (higher production costs → higher prices charged by firms)
- **Unemployment rises** (lower output → firms need fewer workers)

This represents an **upward shift of the SRPC**: the short-run tradeoff worsens because at any given unemployment rate, inflation is now higher (and vice versa). The economy does NOT simply move along the existing Phillips curve — the curve itself shifts.

Since the breakdown is **permanent**, the natural rate of unemployment may also increase (structural unemployment rises due to destroyed productive capacity), shifting the **LRPC rightward** to uₙ₁.

## Question 1.5 — Labour Market with Binding Minimum Wage (2p)

**Context:** The minimum wage (W_min) is **above** the equilibrium wage → there is already structural unemployment before the breakdown.

**Diagram description:**
```
Wage (W)
    ↑
    |   LS₀
W_min |-----+-----+--------  ← Minimum wage (binding)
    |    |     |
W*  |    |  *  |          (equilibrium below W_min, not reached)
    |    |     |
    |    |     |  LD₀
    |    |     LD₁
    +----+--+--+--+-------→ Quantity of Labour (L)
          Ld₁ Ld₀   Ls
```

**Key elements:**
- **Y-axis:** Nominal Wage (W)
- **X-axis:** Quantity of Labour (L)
- **LS:** Upward-sloping labour supply curve
- **LD₀:** Downward-sloping initial labour demand
- **W_min:** Horizontal line above market equilibrium (binding minimum wage)
- **Time 0:** At W_min → employed = Ld₀, labour supply = Ls → **unemployment₀ = Ls − Ld₀**
- **Time 1:** LD shifts LEFT to **LD₁** (breakdown reduces labour demand)
- **Unemployment₁ = Ls − Ld₁ > Ls − Ld₀** → **unemployment increases**

**Explanation:**

**Why labour demand shifts left:**
The breakdown raises production costs and reduces output. When firms produce less, they need **fewer workers** at any given wage → labour demand falls (LD₀ → LD₁).

**Why the minimum wage prevents market adjustment:**
In a free market, the fall in labour demand would push the equilibrium wage down. However, the **legal minimum wage is fixed** at W_min and cannot fall. As a result:
- Firms employ fewer workers (Ld₁) at the unchanged W_min
- Labour supply does not fall (or may even rise slightly as real wages fall due to inflation and workers seek income)
- The **gap between supply and demand at W_min widens** → unemployment rises from (Ls − Ld₀) to (Ls − Ld₁)

The minimum wage thus **amplifies** the unemployment effect of the supply shock, since the wage cannot adjust downward to partially clear the labour market.

## Question 1.6 — Government Policy Response

### 1.6.1 — Type of Policy (0.75p)

The government will conduct **expansionary fiscal policy** (also called demand-side or demand-stimulating policy).

**Specifically:** This means **increasing government spending (G)** and/or **reducing taxes (T)** to boost aggregate demand.

**Reasoning:**
- The breakdown caused a negative supply shock → output fell below potential → deflationary/recessionary gap relative to the original equilibrium
- With elections approaching, the government wants to maintain high economic activity
- Since it cannot directly fix the supply side in the short run, it stimulates **aggregate demand** to push output back up
- This is a **counter-cyclical fiscal policy** response to the supply shock

---

**Add (in a different colour, label as Time 2):**
- **AD₀ shifts RIGHT → AD₂** (due to expansionary fiscal policy: ↑G or ↓T)
- New short-run equilibrium **E₂**: intersection of AD₂ with SRAS₁
- E₂ shows **higher output than E₁** (Y₂ > Y₁) but **even higher price level** (P₂ > P₁)

### 1.6.3 — Effectiveness of Fiscal Policy: IS-LM Model (2p)

**Answer: Fiscal policy is MORE effective when the interest sensitivity of investment is LOW.**

**Using the IS-LM model (i on Y-axis, Y on X-axis):**

**Mechanism of fiscal policy in IS-LM:**
1. Government increases spending (G↑) → IS curve shifts **rightward** (IS₀ → IS₁)
2. Higher Y → increased demand for money → interest rate (i) rises
3. Higher i → investment falls (**crowding-out effect**)
4. The more investment responds to i, the larger the crowding out, and the smaller the net gain in Y

---

**Diagram 1: Interest Sensitivity of Investment is HIGH → IS curve is FLAT**

```
Interest Rate (i)
    ↑
    |       LM
i₁  |------/--E₁
i₀  |----/-E₀
    |   /    IS₁ (flat)
    |  /   IS₀ (flat)
    | /
    +--------→ Y
       Y₀ Y₁
```

- IS is **flat** (investment responds strongly to i)
- IS shifts right by the same amount (due to ΔG)
- BUT: the rise in i from LM causes a **large fall in investment**
- The crowding-out effect is **strong**
- Net increase in Y is **small** → fiscal policy LESS effective

---

**Diagram 2: Interest Sensitivity of Investment is LOW → IS curve is STEEP**

```
Interest Rate (i)
    ↑
    |    LM
i₁  |---/--E₁
i₀  |-/-E₀
    |/  IS₁ (steep)
    |  IS₀ (steep)
    +--------→ Y
       Y₀    Y₁
```

- IS is **steep** (investment barely responds to i)
- IS shifts right by the same amount (due to ΔG)
- The rise in i causes only a **small fall in investment**
- The crowding-out effect is **weak**
- Net increase in Y is **large** → fiscal policy MORE effective

**Conclusion:** Fiscal policy is most effective when the **interest sensitivity of investment is LOW** (steep IS curve), because the crowding-out effect is minimal and most of the initial demand stimulus translates into higher output.

## Question 1.7 — Long-Run Outcome (0.75p) [add to diagram 1.1, label Time 3]

**Since the breakdown is PERMANENT, the LRAS has permanently shifted left to LRAS₁.**

**In the long run, the economy must settle at the new natural rate of output Yₙ₁ (< Yₙ₀).**

**What happens:**
- In the short run after the government's AD expansion (Time 2): output is at Y₂ on SRAS₁ and AD₂, above the new natural rate Yₙ₁
- This creates an **inflationary gap** (Y₂ > Yₙ₁)
- Over time, workers adjust their **wage expectations upward** (actual inflation is above expected inflation)
- Unions bargain for higher nominal wages → production costs rise further → SRAS₁ shifts **further left/upward → SRAS₃**
- The economy moves to **E₃ (Time 3)**: intersection of AD₂, SRAS₃, and LRAS₁

**Long-run result:**
- **Output = Yₙ₁** (new, permanently lower natural rate of output — the power capacity is gone)
- **Price level = P₃ > P₂ > P₁ > P₀** (highest price level yet, due to both supply shock AND expansionary policy)
- The government's fiscal expansion only caused **more inflation** in the long run without permanently raising output

**Key insight:** In the long run, the economy cannot exceed its productive capacity (LRAS₁). The government stimulus (AD shift) only leads to a higher price level, not permanently higher output. Real output is ultimately determined by the supply side.

## Question 2.1 — Money Market Diagram ✅ **Answer: A — Both statements are correct**

**Money market setup (Value of money = 1/P on vertical axis, Quantity of money on horizontal axis):**
- Money Supply (Ms): **vertical** line (controlled by central bank)
- Money Demand (Md): **downward sloping** (higher value of money → lower price level → more goods per euro → less money needed)

**Statement 1: QE shifts equilibrium from A to D → CORRECT**

Quantitative easing (QE) = central bank purchases long-term government and corporate bonds, injecting money into the banking system → **money supply increases** → Ms shifts **right**.

In the money market (value of money vs. quantity):
- More money in circulation → the value of each euro falls (lower 1/P = higher price level)
- Equilibrium shifts to a point with **lower value of money** → consistent with moving from A (higher value) to D (lower value) ✓

**Statement 2: Movement from D to C results from increased inclination to hold cash instead of deposits → CORRECT**

When people prefer to hold **cash (currency)** over **demand deposits**:
- Banks receive **fewer deposits** → banks hold fewer reserves → the money multiplier decreases
- The effective money supply **contracts** (supply curve shifts left)
- This moves the equilibrium from D (post-QE, low value of money) back up toward C (higher value of money)

Alternatively: holding more cash = increased demand for base money (Md shifts right), both raising the value of money. Either interpretation supports D → C. ✓

## Question 2.3 — Before-Tax Nominal Interest Rate ✅ **Answer: C — 15% < x ≤ 20%**

**Given:**
- After-tax **real** interest rate = 12%
- Inflation rate = 2%
- Tax rate = 12.5%

**Step 1: Find the after-tax nominal interest rate**

Using: Real interest rate = Nominal interest rate − Inflation rate

```
After-tax real rate = After-tax nominal rate − Inflation rate
12% = After-tax nominal rate − 2%
After-tax nominal rate = 14%
```

**Step 2: Find the before-tax nominal interest rate (x)**

Taxes are applied to the **nominal** interest earnings:
```
After-tax nominal rate = x × (1 − tax rate)
14% = x × (1 − 0.125)
14% = x × 0.875
x = 14% / 0.875
x = 16%
```

**Verification:**
- Before-tax nominal rate: 16%
- Tax paid: 16% × 12.5% = 2%
- After-tax nominal rate: 16% − 2% = **14%** ✓
- After-tax real rate: 14% − 2% (inflation) = **12%** ✓

**x = 16%, which falls in the range 15% < x ≤ 20% → Answer C**

## Question 2.4 — Trading Partner's Economic Growth ✅ **Answer: C — Only statement 2 is correct**

**Setup:** Country X is initially in long-run equilibrium. Its main trading partner experiences a large increase in economic growth.

**Effect on Country X:** Trading partner grows → demands more imports from X → X's **exports increase** → NX↑ → **AD shifts RIGHT** for Country X.

---

**Statement 1: "Assume I=f(i,Y). The short-run effect is theoretically ambiguous." → INCORRECT**

With I=f(i,Y), investment responds both to interest rates (negatively) and income (positively — accelerator effect).

When AD shifts right due to higher NX:
- Higher Y raises investment via the Y-channel (positive)
- Higher i (due to increased money demand at higher Y via LM) lowers investment (negative)
- So the **net effect on I is ambiguous**

However, the **overall short-run effect on GDP (Y) is NOT ambiguous** — the positive demand shock from higher NX unambiguously **increases Y** in the short run (the IS curve shifts right; the intersection with LM is at a higher Y and higher i). The economy clearly produces more output. Statement 1 is **incorrect**.

---

**Statement 2: "Price level rises more without automatic stabilizers than with them." → CORRECT**

In the **long run**, after the positive demand shock:
- LRAS is vertical → output returns to natural rate regardless
- The **price level** is determined by where AD intersects LRAS

**Without automatic stabilizers:**
- Full multiplier applies → AD shifts **more** to the right
- Larger AD shift → new long-run equilibrium has **higher price level**

**With automatic stabilizers** (e.g., progressive taxation, unemployment benefits):
- As Y rises, taxes automatically increase and transfers automatically decrease → **dampens the AD shift**
- Smaller AD shift → new long-run equilibrium has **lower price level**

Therefore, without automatic stabilizers → more inflation in the long run. Statement 2 is **correct**. ✓

## Question 2.5 — GDP Investment Component (Eurozone) ✅ **Answer: C**

**Recall:** Investment (I) in GDP = business spending on capital equipment + new structures (including new housing) + **changes in inventories**. Crucially, GDP includes only **newly produced** goods within the defined territory.

---

**A) A British firm builds a new factory in London → Does NOT affect Eurozone GDP**

The UK is **not in the Eurozone** (UK left the EU, and even prior to Brexit was not in the Eurozone). A factory built in London is part of **UK GDP**, not Eurozone GDP. Irrelevant to Eurozone investment. ❌

---

**B) A Belgian buys a German car produced in year t → Affects Consumption (C), not Investment (I)**

Both Belgium and Germany are Eurozone members. Within the Eurozone, this is an internal transaction. The car is a **consumer durable** purchased by a private individual → counted as **Consumption (C)**, not investment. ❌

*(Note: From Belgium's perspective alone, this is a C↑ and Import↑, offsetting in NX. For Germany: an Export↑ offset by C↑ elsewhere in Eurozone. Net for full Eurozone: C↑, which is not I.)*

---

**C) A Dutch textile company sells a carpet produced in year t−1 → AFFECTS Investment (I) in year t** ✓

Timeline analysis:
- **Year t−1:** Carpet is **produced** → added to firm's inventory → counted as **positive inventory investment** in year t−1 GDP
- **Year t:** Carpet is **sold** → inventory is drawn down → counted as **negative inventory investment** (inventory decumulation) in year t GDP

The investment component (I) of Eurozone GDP in year t is **reduced** by this transaction (inventory decreases). This DOES affect I in year t. ✓

---

**D) A Greek resident buys an old house → Does NOT affect Eurozone GDP**

GDP only counts **newly produced** goods and services. An existing (old) house was already counted in GDP when it was built. The resale of an old house is a **transfer of an existing asset** — it is NOT part of GDP (no new production). Only **new housing construction** counts as investment (I). ❌

*(Note: Real estate agent fees for the transaction would be counted in GDP as a service, but the house value itself is not.)*
