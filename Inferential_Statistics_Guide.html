The Story: The Case of the Sinking GPA
One narrative that quietly introduces every idea in the chapter before a single formula appears.

Professor Devlin has taught the same first-year course for twenty years. For as long as anyone can remember, the average final grade — the GPA — has sat at 4.4. It's practically folklore. It's printed in the course brochure. Nobody questions it.

This year, walking out of the exam hall, she has a nagging feeling. The class seemed… off. Distracted. She suspects the true average has slipped below 4.4. But here's her problem, and it's the problem behind this entire chapter:

She cannot grade every student who will ever take this course. The "population" she cares about — the true, long-run average ability of students like these — is invisible. She can only ever hold a sample in her hands: this year's 100 exam papers.

Chapter 1 of her investigation: make a best guess

She averages this year's 100 papers and gets 4.16. That single number is her point estimate — her one best guess for the invisible true average. But Devlin has been burned before. She knows that if she'd happened to grab a different 100 students, she'd have gotten a slightly different number — maybe 4.09, maybe 4.21. The number she computes is a bit of a lottery. So the honest question isn't just "what's my guess?" but "how much does my guess bounce around from sample to sample?" That bounce has a name — the standard error — and it's the quiet hero of the whole chapter. (This is Part 1.)

Chapter 2: turn the guess into an honest range

A single number pretending to be the truth is dishonest — it hides the bounce. So instead of reporting "4.16, take it or leave it," Devlin reports a range: "I'm 95% confident the true average lies between 4.05 and 4.27." That's a confidence interval — her guess, wrapped in error bars sized exactly to how noisy her sample is. Wide range = "I'm not sure." Narrow range = "I've pinned it down." (This is Part 2.)

Chapter 3: put the old claim on trial

Now she has to make a decision, and this is where it gets tense. The brochure says 4.4. Devlin suspects lower. She can't just announce "it dropped!" on a hunch — that's an accusation, and accusations need evidence. So she runs it like a court case:

The old claim, μ = 4.4, is the defendant, presumed innocent (this is H₀, the null hypothesis). Devlin, the prosecutor, must prove the alternative — that the average really fell (H₁: μ < 4.4). She doesn't get to convict on a feeling. She only convicts if the evidence would be absurdly unlikely for an innocent defendant.

Her sample average of 4.16 sits well below 4.4. She asks the key question of the entire chapter: "If the true average really were still 4.4, how often would pure luck hand me a sample this low?" The answer — the p-value — comes back as roughly 0.0003. Three in ten thousand. That's not bad luck; that's a signal. She rejects the old claim. The GPA really has sunk. (This is Part 3.)

Chapter 4: where's the line, and how sharp are her eyes?

Two loose ends remain. First: what exactly was the threshold — how low did the sample average have to be before she was allowed to "convict"? That cut-off is the critical value. Second, and deeper: suppose the true average really did drop, but only a little. Would her test even notice? A test that can't detect a real drop is a smoke detector with a dead battery. The probability that her test correctly catches a real effect is its power. (This is Part 4.)

Hold onto this. Every formula ahead is just Devlin being careful. Point estimate = her best guess. Standard error = how shaky the guess is. Confidence interval = the guess plus honest error bars. Hypothesis test = putting the old claim on trial. Critical value = the conviction threshold. Power = how sharp her eyesight is. Same investigation, four lenses.

The One Big Idea
If you remember nothing else, remember this diagram.

All of inferential statistics answers a single question: “I can only see a sample. What can I honestly say about the population I can’t see?” The bridge from sample to population is always the same object — the sampling distribution of X̄, which the Central Limit Theorem hands us for free.

Part 1 · Point estimate One number: “my best guess is X̄.”
Part 2 · Confidence interval A range: “μ is plausibly in here.”
Part 3 · Hypothesis test A verdict: “reject the old claim, or not.”
Part 4 · Critical value & power The threshold, and the quality of the test.
The single thread: the standard error SE = σ/√n appears in every single part. It is the measure of "how much does my sample guess wobble." Master it once here and Parts 2, 3, and 4 are mostly bookkeeping.
Part 1
Point Estimators
Your single best guess for something you can’t measure — and how much that guess wobbles.

1.1 The Big Picture — Why We Need This

In the real world you never observe the whole population. You take a sample, compute something from it, and use that as your best guess for the unknown population value. That's Devlin's exact situation: she can't grade every student who will ever exist, only this year's 100.

Population (unknown)
All students, ever. You want the true average GPA μ. You can never measure everyone. μ is a fixed number, hidden behind a curtain.
Sample (what you have)
100 randomly chosen students. Their average is x̄ = 4.16. This is your best guess for μ — the only thing you actually get to hold.

The process of using a sample value to estimate a population value is called point estimation. "Point" because it's a single point on the number line — one number, not a range (the range comes in Part 2).

1.2 Four Terms You Must Not Confuse EXAM

TermSymbolWhat it isKnown?
Population parameterμ, σ, pThe true value in the whole population — the thing you actually want❌ Unknown
Point estimatorX̄, S, p̂A rule / formula for turning a sample into an estimate — decided before you see data✓ You choose it
Point estimatex̄, s, p̂The actual number you get after plugging your sample in✓ You compute it
Sampling distributionX̄ ~ N(μ, σ/√n)The distribution of the estimator across all possible samples of size n✓ From the CLT
Estimator vs estimate — the distinction the exam loves:
X̄ (capital) is the estimator — a random variable, a recipe. It hasn't met your data yet, so it could still turn out to be many values. It varies from sample to sample.
x̄ (lowercase) is the estimate — the one specific number from your one sample. It's frozen. It's just 4.16.
Intuition: the estimator is the dice; the estimate is the number you rolled. Statistics is the study of the dice (X̄) so you can judge how much to trust the roll (x̄).

1.3 Common Point Estimators

Population parameterSymbolEstimatorFormula
Population meanμSample meanX̄ = (X₁+…+Xₙ)/n
Population proportionpSample proportionp̂ = (number with trait) / n
Population varianceσ²Sample varianceS² = Σ(Xᵢ−X̄)² / (n−1)
Population SDσSample SDS = √S²
Why n−1, not n, for S²? Dividing by n slightly under-estimates the spread (the data always hugs its own mean a bit too tightly). The n−1 correction fixes that bias exactly. More on "bias" in §1.6.

1.4 The Sampling Distribution — The Heart of Everything KEY

Here is the crucial mental leap. If you took a different sample, you'd get a different x̄. So the estimator X̄ is itself a random variable with its own distribution. That distribution — the pattern of all the x̄'s you'd get across every possible sample — is the sampling distribution.

This is exactly Devlin's worry from the story: "if I'd grabbed a different 100 students, I'd have gotten 4.09 or 4.21 instead of 4.16." The sampling distribution describes that wobble.

The thought experiment (GPA dataset, n = 100)
1
Draw sample 1 (100 students) → x̄₁ = 4.09
2
Draw sample 2 (100 students) → x̄₂ = 4.21
3
Draw sample 3 (100 students) → x̄₃ = 4.14
4
Repeat 1000 times. Collect all 1000 x̄'s. Plot them as a histogram.

Result: the histogram of those 1000 sample means is a smooth bell curve centred at the true μ — even though individual GPAs are not bell-shaped at all. That miracle is the Central Limit Theorem.

Sampling distribution of X̄ (from the CLT)
X̄ ~ N( μ , σ/√n )

Centre = μ  → on average X̄ lands on the true mean (unbiased).
Spread = σ/√n  → the standard error; shrinks as n grows.
Shape = normal  → from the CLT, for large enough n, whatever the raw data looks like.

1.5 The Standard Error — The Wobble, Quantified KEY

The standard deviation of the sampling distribution gets its own name: the standard error (SE). It is the single most important quantity in the chapter — it literally measures how much your best guess bounces around.

Standard Error of the Sample Mean
SE(X̄) = σ / √n

Small SE → your estimate is reliable (guesses cluster tightly). Large SE → unreliable.

σ vs SE — do not confuse these (this is the #1 mistake in the whole chapter):
σ (or s) = spread of individual observations. How different one student's GPA is from another's.
SE = σ/√n = spread of the sample mean. How different one sample's average is from another sample's average.

SE is always smaller than σ by a factor of √n. Averages are always steadier than the individuals they average over. Ask 1 person their height and you might be way off; average 100 people and you'll be close every time.
Why √n and not n? Noise partly cancels when you average, but it doesn't cancel perfectly — random ups only partly offset random downs. The math works out to √n. Practical consequence you'll use in Part 2: to make your estimate twice as precise, you need four times the data.

1.6 What Makes an Estimator "Good"? Three Properties

Not every formula for guessing μ is equally trustworthy. Three properties separate a good estimator from a bad one — and X̄ has all three.

1. Unbiasedness — it aims at the right target

An estimator is unbiased if its expected value equals the true parameter: E(X̄) = μ. Meaning: average over many samples and you land exactly on μ — no systematic over- or under-shooting.

X̄ is unbiased for μ. ✓ Picture a dartboard: an unbiased estimator's darts scatter, but their centre of mass is the bullseye.

2. Efficiency — its darts scatter the least

Among all unbiased estimators, the most efficient one has the smallest variance — its darts cluster tightest around the bullseye. Two estimators can both aim true, but you want the one that's also precise.

X̄ is the most efficient unbiased estimator of μ for i.i.d. normal data. Nothing unbiased beats it.

3. Consistency — more data zeroes in on the truth

An estimator is consistent if it converges to the true value as n → ∞. Since SE = σ/√n → 0 as n grows, the whole sampling distribution collapses onto μ.

X̄ is consistent. ✓ With infinite data you'd know μ exactly — which is just the promise that "collect enough evidence and the truth comes out."

1.7 Worked Example — GPA Estimation EXAM

GPA dataset (n = 100, population σ ≈ 0.538)
1
Identify the estimator & its distribution.
X̄ estimates μ. By the CLT with n = 100:
X̄ ~ N(μ, σ/√n) = N(4.16, 0.538/√100) = N(4.16, 0.0538)
2
Interpret the standard error.
SE = 0.0538. Across many samples of 100, the sample means typically vary by about ±0.054 around the true mean. That's tiny — this is a trustworthy estimate.
3
Confirm the CLT held.
The histogram of 1000 sample means is bell-shaped and its QQ-plot is roughly straight → the sampling distribution really is normal, so everything downstream (Parts 2–4) is valid.
Answer: X̄ ~ N(4.16, 0.0538) — very tight, because n = 100 makes SE small.
The mistake to burn into memory: if a question asks about a single observation X, use σ. If it asks about a sample mean X̄, use SE = σ/√n. Swapping them is the most common error in this chapter — and it silently poisons every confidence interval and test that follows.
Part 2
Confidence Intervals
A single guess hides its own uncertainty. A confidence interval reports the guess and the error bars.

2.1 Why a Confidence Interval?

A point estimate gives one number for μ. But we saw in Part 1 that X̄ wobbles — the true μ could be a bit higher or lower; we just got this particular sample. A confidence interval (CI) is a range of plausible values that honestly builds the wobble in.

Point estimate
X̄ = 105.3
“My best guess is 105.3 — but I've told you nothing about how wrong I might be.”
Confidence interval
[99.42, 111.18]
“I'm 95% confident the true mean lies in this range.” The width is the honesty.

This is Devlin upgrading "the average is 4.16" into "the average is somewhere in [4.05, 4.27] with 95% confidence." Same guess — now with its uncertainty made visible.

2.2 The Three Ingredients of Width

Before the formula, feel what makes a CI wide or narrow:

🎯
Confidence level (e.g. 95%)More certainty demanded → wider interval. A 99% CI is wider than a 95% CI — the price of a stronger guarantee.
📊
Standard error (SE = σ/√n)More wobble in your estimator → wider interval. (There's our Part-1 hero again.)
🔢
Sample size (n)Larger n → smaller SE → narrower interval. More data buys precision.
Intuition check: want to be 99.9999% sure you've captured μ? You'd need an enormous interval — nearly [−∞, +∞], which says nothing useful. That's the eternal trade-off: higher confidence costs precision.

2.3 The Formula KEY

When σ is known and the CLT conditions hold (normal data, or n ≥ 30):

Confidence interval for μ (σ known)
CI = [ X̄ − z*·(σ/√n) , X̄ + z*·(σ/√n) ]  =  X̄ ± z*·σ/√n
SymbolNameWhat it is
X̄Sample meanYour point estimate — the centre of the CI
z*Critical valueHow many SEs wide to reach — set by the confidence level
σ/√nStandard errorThe wobble, from Part 1
z*·σ/√nMargin of error (ME)Half the total width — the "± part"

Critical values z* by confidence level

Confidenceα = 1−CLα/2z*Excel
90%0.100.051.645=NORM.INV(0.95;0;1)
95%0.050.0251.96=NORM.INV(0.975;0;1)
99%0.010.0052.576=NORM.INV(0.995;0;1)
Where does z* come from? For 95% we want P(−z* ≤ Z ≤ z*) = 0.95, so 2.5% sits in each tail, so P(Z ≤ z*) = 0.975, so z* = NORM.INV(0.975) = 1.96. Remember these three numbers — 1.645 / 1.96 / 2.576 — they return in Parts 3 and 4.

2.4 Where the Formula Comes From (worth seeing once)

The formula isn't magic — it's the sampling distribution rearranged. From Part 1, the CLT gives X̄ ~ N(μ, σ/√n), so standardising:

Standardised
Z = (X̄ − μ)/(σ/√n) ~ N(0,1)

For 95% we know P(−1.96 ≤ Z ≤ 1.96) = 0.95. Substitute Z and rearrange to trap μ in the middle:

P(−1.96 ≤ (X̄−μ)/(σ/√n) ≤ 1.96) = 0.95

P(X̄ − 1.96·σ/√n ≤ μ ≤ X̄ + 1.96·σ/√n) = 0.95

That's the CI. We took a statement about how X̄ scatters around μ and algebraically flipped it into a statement about where μ plausibly sits relative to our observed X̄.

2.5 Worked Example 1 — IQ at KU Leuven EXAM

X = IQ scores, σ = 15. A sample of n = 25 students gives X̄ = 105.3. Find a 95% CI for μ.

1
Knowns. X̄ = 105.3 | σ = 15 | n = 25 | 95% → z* = 1.96
2
Standard error. SE = σ/√n = 15/√25 = 15/5 = 3
3
Margin of error. ME = z*·SE = 1.96 × 3 = 5.88
4
Build the interval. 105.3 ∓ 5.88 → 95% CI: [99.42, 111.18]
Excel: =105.3 - 1.96*(15/SQRT(25)) → 99.42  ·  =105.3 + 1.96*(15/SQRT(25)) → 111.18

2.6 How to Interpret a CI — The #1 Exam Trap EXAM

WRONG (never write this): “There is a 95% probability that μ lies in [99.42, 111.18].”

Why it's wrong: μ is a fixed, unknown constant, not a random variable. Once the interval is computed, μ either is inside it or it isn't — there's no probability left to speak of. The randomness was in which sample you drew, not in μ.
CORRECT: “For 95% of all possible samples of size 25, the interval constructed this way will contain the true μ.” The confidence is a property of the method, not of this one interval.

Picture repeating the study 100 times: about 95 of the intervals capture μ, and about 5 miss by bad luck. You just don't know which kind yours is.

2.7 Worked Example 2 — Required Sample Size EXAM

Same IQ problem (σ = 15, 95%). How many students do we need so the margin of error is ME ≤ 2?

Start from ME = z*·σ/√n and solve for n:

1
Set up. 2 = 1.96 × 15/√n
2
Solve for √n. √n = 1.96 × 15 / 2 = 14.7
3
Square. n = 14.7² = 216.09
4
Round UP (always). n = 217 students — rounding down would leave ME > 2, breaking the requirement.
General sample-size formula
n = ( z*·σ / ME )²  → always round UP

2.8 One-Sided Confidence Bounds EXAM

Sometimes you only care about one direction — a ceiling or a floor, not both. These are one-sided bounds, and they're the conceptual bridge to hypothesis testing (Part 3), where tests are also one- or two-sided.

Upper bound — "μ is at most ___"

UB = X̄ + z·σ/√n

95% upper: z = NORM.INV(0.95) = 1.645
Lower bound — "μ is at least ___"

LB = X̄ − z·σ/√n

95% lower: z = NORM.INV(0.95) = 1.645
Key difference: a two-sided 95% CI uses z* = 1.96 (2.5% in each tail). A one-sided 95% bound uses z = 1.645 (all 5% in one tail). Same total α = 5%, but pointed entirely in one direction — so you can reach less far and the bound is tighter.

Part 3
Hypothesis Testing
From "here's my estimate" to "here's my decision." Putting a claim on trial with sample evidence.

3.1 The Big Picture — What Is a Hypothesis Test?

A hypothesis test is a formal procedure for deciding whether sample data provide enough evidence to reject a claim about the population. This is Devlin's Chapter 3: the brochure says μ = 4.4; she suspects it dropped; she needs to decide, defensibly.

The logic is four steps, every single time:

  1. State the hypotheses — what claim is on trial?
  2. Compute a test statistic — how far is the sample from the claim, in standard errors?
  3. Find the p-value — how surprising is that, if the claim were true?
  4. Conclude — reject the claim, or not?
Intuition (the discrimination example from the slides): 58.3% of women vs 87.5% of men were promoted — a 29.2% gap. Could that happen by luck even with no real discrimination? If a gap that big would occur randomly only ~2% of the time (p ≈ 0.02), we start to doubt the "no discrimination" story. That doubt-when-the-data-is-too-surprising is hypothesis testing.

3.2 The Court Case Analogy KEY CONCEPT

The whole logic maps onto a criminal trial — the single best intuition in the chapter, and the backbone of Devlin's story.

Court of law
  • H₀: accused is innocent
  • H₁: accused is guilty
  • Start by presuming innocence
  • Need strong evidence to convict
  • "Guilty" = strong conclusion
  • "Not guilty" = weak conclusion (not "proven innocent" — just not enough to convict)
Hypothesis test
  • H₀: the conservative/status-quo claim
  • H₁: what you want to show
  • Start by assuming H₀ is true
  • Need strong evidence (low p-value) to reject
  • "Reject H₀" = strong conclusion
  • "Do not reject H₀" = weak conclusion (not "H₀ proven true")
Never say "accept H₀". Say "do not reject H₀" / "fail to reject H₀". Failing to convict a defendant doesn't prove innocence; it means the evidence fell short. Identically, not rejecting H₀ is not proof it's true.

3.3 Formulating the Hypotheses EXAM

Test typeH₀H₁When to use
Two-sidedμ = μ₀μ ≠ μ₀You expect a change, direction unknown
One-sided rightμ = μ₀μ > μ₀You specifically expect an increase
One-sided leftμ = μ₀μ < μ₀You specifically expect a decrease

Rules for setting up H₀ and H₁

  • H₀ is always the equality / "no change" claim: H₀: μ = μ₀.
  • H₁ is what you want to prove — the research question, the accusation.
  • H₁ sets the direction (one- vs two-sided).
  • The burden of proof is on H₁ — you stay with H₀ until evidence is strong.
GPA example: historical average = 4.4; Devlin believes it fell. → H₀: μ = 4.4   H₁: μ < 4.4  (one-sided left).
Why does the thing we want to prove go in H₁? Because "reject H₀" is the strong, evidence-backed conclusion. Put your claim in H₁ and the data must actively overturn the status quo to support you — demanding and credible. Put it in H₀ and you'd "prove" it just by failing to find evidence against it, which is far too easy. Science makes you earn your claims.

3.4 The Test Statistic KEY

For testing μ with σ known:

Z-test statistic
Z = (X̄ − μ₀) / (σ/√n)

Read it literally: how many standard errors is our sample mean away from the claimed mean μ₀? It's just the Part-1 standard error doing the measuring. Under H₀, Z ~ N(0,1), so a Z near 0 is unremarkable and a Z far from 0 is evidence against H₀.

GPA example: X̄ = 4.16, μ₀ = 4.4, σ = 0.538, n = 100.
SE = 0.538/√100 = 0.0538 → Z = (4.16 − 4.4)/0.0538 = −4.46.
(The slides show −3.42 using a slightly different sample mean — same procedure, same conclusion.)
Notice the reuse: this is the exact same standardisation as the CI in §2.4 — except there we plugged in the unknown μ and solved for it, and here we plug in the hypothesised μ₀ and see how extreme the result looks. One machine, two jobs.

3.5 The P-Value EXAM

The p-value is the probability of observing a test statistic as extreme as yours (or more), assuming H₀ is true. It answers Devlin's central question: "if the average really were still 4.4, how often would luck alone hand me a sample this extreme?" Small p-value = "this would be a bizarre fluke under H₀" = evidence against H₀.

H₁Test typeP-valueExcel
μ < μ₀LeftP(Z ≤ z_obs)=NORM.DIST(z;0;1;TRUE)
μ > μ₀RightP(Z ≥ z_obs)=1-NORM.DIST(z;0;1;TRUE)
μ ≠ μ₀Two-sided2 × P(Z ≥ |z_obs|)=2*(1-NORM.DIST(ABS(z);0;1;TRUE))
GPA example: H₁: μ < 4.4, z_obs = −3.42 → p = P(Z ≤ −3.42) = 0.000313 (0.031%). Extremely small — very strong evidence against H₀.
What the p-value is NOT: it is not "the probability that H₀ is true." H₀ isn't random — it's either true or false. The p-value is the probability of the data (or more extreme) given H₀. Reversing that conditional is the most common misconception in all of statistics.

3.6 Making the Conclusion EXAM

Compare the p-value to the significance level α (usually 0.05) — the bar you set in advance for how surprising is surprising enough.

p ≤ α → Reject H₀

Result is statistically significant.
"The sample provides sufficient evidence to reject H₀ at the 5% level."
p > α → Do NOT reject H₀

Result is not statistically significant.
"The sample does not provide sufficient evidence to reject H₀ at the 5% level."
GPA conclusion: p = 0.000313 < 0.05 → reject H₀. "There is sufficient statistical evidence that the mean GPA has decreased below 4.4." Devlin's suspicion is vindicated — with evidence, not a hunch.

3.7 Decision Errors — Type I & Type II KEY CONCEPT

Any verdict can be wrong in exactly two ways — and this table is the seed of all of Part 4.

H₀ True (reality)
H₁ True (reality)
Do NOT reject H₀
✓ Correct
✗ Type II error (β)
Reject H₀
✗ Type I error (α)
✓ Correct
Type I error (α)
Reject H₀ when H₀ is actually true.
"False alarm" — convict an innocent person.

P(Type I) = α = the significance level you set (usually 0.05).
Type II error (β)
Fail to reject H₀ when H₁ is actually true.
"Miss" — let a guilty person walk.

P(Type II) = β, harder to control (depends on the true μ, n, σ).
The trade-off: keeping α small (0.05) means rarely convicting the innocent — but it makes you cautious, so you'll more often miss a real effect (larger β). That's a deliberate choice: society, like the courts, treats convicting the innocent (Type I) as worse than freeing the guilty (Type II), so we hold α down and accept the larger β. Part 4 is all about measuring and shrinking that β.

3.8 Complete Worked Example — the GPA Test EXAM

n = 100 students. Historical average GPA = 4.4, σ = 0.538. Sample gives X̄ = 4.16. Teacher believes GPA fell. Test at α = 0.05.

1
Hypotheses. H₀: μ = 4.4   H₁: μ < 4.4 (one-sided left).
2
Assumptions. Random sample ✓ · σ known ✓ · n = 100 ≥ 30 so CLT gives X̄ ~ N(μ, σ/√n) ✓.
3
Test statistic. SE = 0.538/10 = 0.0538 → Z = (4.16 − 4.4)/0.0538 = −4.46 (slides: −3.42, same procedure).
4
P-value. Left test → p = P(Z ≤ −3.42) = NORM.DIST(−3.42;0;1;TRUE) = 0.000313.
5
Conclusion. 0.000313 < 0.05 → Reject H₀. "Strong evidence that mean GPA has decreased below 4.4."

3.9 The Two-Sided P-Value — Watch Out! EXAM

For a two-sided test (H₁: μ ≠ μ₀), extreme values in either direction count against H₀:

Two-sided p-value
p = 2 × P(Z ≥ |z_obs|)
Practical rule: compute the one-sided p-value and double it. If z_obs = −3.42, two-sided p = 2 × 0.000313 = 0.000626.
Slides warn — "scale might be misleading!" When you sketch the bell curve the tails look tiny, but the p-value can still be decisively significant. Always compute; never eyeball.

Part 4
Critical Values & Power
The conviction threshold — and how sharp your test's eyesight really is.

4.1 Two Equivalent Ways to Decide

Part 3 decided via the p-value. There's a second, always-equivalent route: the critical value approach. This is Devlin asking "how low did the sample average have to be before I was allowed to convict?"

P-value approach

Compute Z_obs → compute p-value.
Reject H₀ if p ≤ α.

“Is this result surprising enough?”
Critical value approach

Compute Z_obs → compare to z_c.
Reject H₀ if Z is past the threshold.

“Is this result past the line?”
Same verdict, always. Both methods give identical reject/don't-reject decisions. The critical-value route is often faster on exams where α is fixed in advance.

4.2 Critical Values — The Threshold KEY

The critical region is the set of Z values that trigger rejection; its boundary is the critical value z_c. For α = 0.05:

H₁Test typez_cReject H₀ if
μ < μ₀Left−1.645Z_obs ≤ −1.645
μ > μ₀Right+1.645Z_obs ≥ +1.645
μ ≠ μ₀Two-sided±1.96|Z_obs| ≥ 1.96
Recognise these numbers? They're the exact z* from the confidence intervals in Part 2 — no coincidence: both come from the same N(0,1) and the same α. A two-sided test at α is literally "is μ₀ outside the (1−α) confidence interval?"

4.3 The Critical Region for X̄ (not just Z) EXAM

You can express the threshold on the sample-mean scale directly — useful when a question asks "what value of X̄ would trigger rejection?" Set Z_obs = z_c in Z = (X̄−μ₀)/SE and solve for X̄:

Critical value for X̄ (left, H₁: μ < μ₀)
x̄_c = μ₀ − 1.645 · σ/√n
Critical value for X̄ (right, H₁: μ > μ₀)
x̄_c = μ₀ + 1.645 · σ/√n
Critical values for X̄ (two-sided)
x̄_c = μ₀ ± 1.96 · σ/√n
GPA example (H₁: μ < 4.4, σ = 0.538, n = 100):
SE = 0.0538 → x̄_c = 4.4 − 1.645 × 0.0538 = 4.4 − 0.0885 = 4.3115.
Reject H₀ if X̄ ≤ 4.3115. Since X̄ = 4.16 < 4.3115 → reject. ✓ This is the literal "line in the sand" from Devlin's Chapter 4.

4.4 Type II Error — β KEY

The Type II error is failing to reject H₀ when H₁ is actually true — letting the guilty walk. Its probability is β. To compute it you need a specific alternative μ₁: the actual true mean if H₀ is wrong. (You can't measure "how often do I miss the effect?" without saying how big the effect really is.)

Type II error probability (left test)
β(μ₁) = P(X̄ > x̄_c | μ = μ₁) = P(Z > (x̄_c − μ₁)/(σ/√n))

In words: if the truth is μ₁, what's the chance X̄ still lands in the "don't reject" zone and we miss it?

Reading it: blue = X̄'s distribution if H₀ is true; green = if H₁ (μ₁) is true. Red (left of x̄_c under blue) = α. Orange (right of x̄_c under green) = β: the truth is μ₁, but X̄ drifted into the "don't reject" zone and we missed it. The more the two bells overlap, the bigger β.

4.5 Computing β — Step by Step EXAM

GPA: H₀: μ = 4.4, H₁: μ < 4.4, σ = 0.538, n = 100, α = 0.05. Suppose the true mean is μ₁ = 4.2. Find β.

1
Critical value (from H₀). x̄_c = 4.4 − 1.645 × 0.0538 = 4.3115. Reject if X̄ ≤ 4.3115.
2
β = P(don't reject | μ = μ₁). = P(X̄ > 4.3115 | μ = 4.2).
3
Standardise using μ₁ = 4.2 (not μ₀!). Z = (4.3115 − 4.2)/0.0538 = 2.073.
4
Probability. β = P(Z > 2.073) = 1 − NORM.DIST(2.073;0;1;TRUE) = 1 − 0.9809 = 0.0191 ≈ 1.9%.
Interpretation: if the true mean really is 4.2, there's only a 1.9% chance the test fails to catch it. Sharp eyes.
The critical distinction: the test statistic standardises with μ₀; β standardises with μ₁, the true alternative. Mixing them up is the classic β mistake.

4.6 Power of the Test KEY

Power for alternative μ₁
Power(μ₁) = 1 − β(μ₁)

Power is the probability of correctly rejecting H₀ when H₁ is true at μ₁ — the chance your test actually catches a real effect. It's the smoke detector with a live battery. High power = sensitive test.

PowerMeaningVerdict
0.990% chance of detecting a real effectGood
0.5Coin flipPoor
0.2Misses 4 times in 5Very poor
GPA continued: β(4.2) = 0.019 → Power(4.2) = 1 − 0.019 = 0.981 = 98.1%. If the true mean is 0.2 below the null, the test catches it 98% of the time. Excellent.

What raises power?

FactorPowerWhy
Larger n↑Smaller SE → the two bells overlap less → easier to tell apart
Larger |μ₁ − μ₀|↑Bigger true effect → more obvious departure
Smaller σ↑Less noise → the signal stands out
Larger α↑Wider rejection region → easier to reject (but more Type I errors)
The α–β trade-off (again): shrinking α raises β and lowers power — there's no free lunch. The only way to shrink both error types at once is to collect more data (raise n). That single lever, n, quietly runs through all four parts: it shrinks SE (P1), narrows CIs (P2), and now sharpens power (P4).

4.7 The Power Curve

Compute Power(μ₁) for every possible true value μ₁ and plot it:

  • μ₁ far from μ₀ (big true effect): Power ≈ 1 — almost certain to catch it.
  • μ₁ close to μ₀: Power ≈ α — a tiny effect is nearly invisible.
  • μ₁ = μ₀ exactly: Power = α — that's just the Type I rate (there's nothing real to detect).

The one-sentence summary of the whole chapter.

You can only ever see a sample, so you use the sampling distribution X̄ ~ N(μ, σ/√n) to (1) make a best guess, (2) wrap it in honest error bars, (3) decide whether a claim survives the evidence, and (4) check how good that decision procedure actually is. And the quantity σ/√n — the standard error, the wobble — is the thread running through all four.
