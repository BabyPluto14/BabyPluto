The Big Picture: Why Risk & Return?

Before diving into formulas, understand the two-part journey of corporate finance.

Part 1 · Value

NPV

Discount future cash flows at the opportunity cost of capital (hurdle rate). Higher risk → lower value, all else equal.

Part 2 · Risk

CAPM

Estimate what that hurdle rate should be. Risk drives required return — we need a way to measure and price it.

For Shareholders

COE

Cost of Equity — the return required by an individual equity investor.

For the Company

WACC

Weighted Average Cost of Capital — the overall hurdle rate for investment projects.

"Higher Risk = Higher Expected Return" — there are no free lunches in finance.
📈
Historical proof: From 1802 to 2021, equities (high risk) returned far more than bonds (medium risk), which returned more than cash (low risk). Investors who accepted more risk were compensated with higher long-run returns.

CH7 · Measuring Risk & Return of a Single Security

Before building portfolios, we must be able to describe any single investment with two numbers: its expected return and its risk.

📊 Expected Return

The expected return is the probability-weighted average of all possible outcomes. Think of it as what you "expect on average" if you could repeat the investment many times.

Formula: Expected Return
E(R) = Σ Pᵢ × Rᵢ
Pᵢ = probability of scenario i occurring
Rᵢ = return in scenario i
(Σ means "sum over all scenarios")

Using historical data: E(R) = (R₁ + R₂ + … + Rₙ) / n
Worked Example — Securities A & B (both priced at €100)
EconomyProbabilityPrice AReturn APrice BReturn B
Boom25%€130+30%€120+20%
Normal50%€110+10%€110+10%
Recession25%€90–10%€95–5%
E(R)+10%+8.75%

E(Rₐ) = 0.25×30% + 0.50×10% + 0.25×(–10%) = 7.5% + 5% – 2.5% = 10%

📉 Risk = Standard Deviation

Risk is the spread or deviation around the expected return. The wider the spread of possible outcomes, the riskier the investment.

Variance & Standard Deviation
σ²(R) = Σ Pᵢ × [Rᵢ − E(R)]²
σ(R) = √σ²(R)
σ² = variance (risk squared — harder to interpret directly)
σ = standard deviation (same units as return — easy to interpret)

Using historical data: σ = √[ Σ(Rₜ − E(R))² / (n−1) ]
💡
Intuition: Think of expected return as the centre of a bell curve and standard deviation as how wide that bell curve is. A narrow bell = predictable returns = low risk. A wide bell = unpredictable returns = high risk.
Returns of a risky asset follow a normal distribution. A wider spread = higher σ = higher risk.

CH7 · Portfolio Return & Risk

When you combine assets into a portfolio, the maths changes — and something magical happens to risk.

📦 Portfolio Expected Return

Good news: portfolio return is simply the weighted average of the individual returns.

Portfolio Expected Return
E(Rₚ) = Σ wᵢ × E(Rᵢ)
wᵢ = fraction of total portfolio value invested in asset i
E(Rᵢ) = expected return of asset i
(weights must sum to 1: Σwᵢ = 1)
🔑
Example: You invest 60% in Stock A (E(R) = 12%) and 40% in Stock B (E(R) = 8%). E(Rₚ) = 0.60 × 12% + 0.40 × 8% = 7.2% + 3.2% = 10.4%

🔀 Portfolio Risk — The Correlation Effect

Portfolio risk is NOT the weighted average of individual risks. Thanks to correlation, it can be significantly lower — this is the essence of diversification.

Portfolio Variance (2-Asset Case)
σ²ₚ = w₁²σ²₁ + w₂²σ²₂ + 2·w₁·w₂·σ₁·σ₂·ρ₁₂
σ₁, σ₂ = standard deviations of assets 1 and 2
w₁, w₂ = portfolio weights
ρ₁₂ = correlation coefficient between the two assets (ranges from −1 to +1)
σₚ = √σ²ₚ (portfolio standard deviation)

The crucial insight is what the correlation ρ does:

Correlation (ρ)MeaningEffect on Portfolio Risk
ρ = +1 Assets move perfectly together No diversification benefit — σₚ = weighted average σ
0 < ρ < 1 Assets move somewhat together Some diversification — σₚ < weighted average σ
ρ = 0 Assets move independently Good diversification — significant risk reduction
ρ = −1 Assets move perfectly opposite Perfect hedge — σₚ can reach zero!
⚡
Real-world insight: This is why diversifying across different asset classes (stocks + bonds + real estate + commodities) reduces portfolio risk — they don't all move together. During the 2008 financial crisis, correlations between asset classes spiked toward +1, which is why "diversification failed" exactly when people needed it most.

CH7 · Diversification, Beta & The Sharpe Ratio

Not all risk is equal. Some risk can be eliminated for free — the rest must be compensated.

🌀 Two Types of Risk

Systematic Risk
(Market Risk)
Total Risk
Idiosyncratic Risk
(Unique / Diversifiable)

Idiosyncratic / Unique Risk

Risk from factors specific to one company: bad management, product recall, scandal. Can be diversified away — rational investors never get compensated for it.

Systematic / Market Risk

Economy-wide risk: recessions, interest rate changes, pandemics. Cannot be diversified away — you MUST be compensated for bearing it. Measured by Beta (β).

As you add more securities to a portfolio, unique risk disappears. Market risk remains regardless.

β Beta — Measuring Market Risk

Beta measures how sensitive a stock is to movements in the overall market. It is the slope of a regression line when you plot the stock's returns against market returns.

Beta Definition
β = Cov(Rᵢ, Rₘ) / Var(Rₘ)
Rᵢ = return on security i
Rₘ = return on the market portfolio (e.g., S&P 500, MSCI World)
β = 1 means the stock moves exactly with the market
β < 1
Defensive Stock
e.g., utilities, pharma
β = 1
Moves with market
e.g., market index ETF
β > 1
Aggressive Stock
e.g., tech, startups
β < 0
Counter-cyclical
e.g., gold, inverse ETF
💡
Portfolio Beta = weighted average of individual betas. If you hold 60% in a β=1.5 stock and 40% in a β=0.5 stock: βₚ = 0.6×1.5 + 0.4×0.5 = 1.1

⚖️ Sharpe Ratio — Return per Unit of Risk

Instead of looking at return and risk separately, the Sharpe Ratio combines them: how much excess return do you get per unit of risk taken?

Sharpe Ratio
Sharpe = (R − Rƒ) / σ
R = actual return of the security or portfolio
Rƒ = risk-free rate (e.g., government bond yield)
σ = standard deviation of the security or portfolio
Higher Sharpe = better risk-adjusted performance
📌
Example: Fund A returns 12% with σ = 10%. Fund B returns 15% with σ = 20%. Risk-free = 3%.

Sharpe A = (12%−3%)/10% = 0.90
Sharpe B = (15%−3%)/20% = 0.60

Fund A gives more return per unit of risk — it's the better investment on a risk-adjusted basis!

CH8 · Markowitz Portfolio Theory & The Efficient Frontier

Harry Markowitz (1952, Nobel Prize 1990) asked a simple question: what is the optimal way to combine assets?

🏆 The Efficient Frontier

The key assumptions of Markowitz portfolio theory:

  • Returns are normally distributed with known mean and standard deviation.
  • Investors only care about risk and return — more return is better, less risk is better.
  • All investors are rational and risk-averse.
1
Combine 2 securities

By varying the weights between two assets, you trace a curved line (a "bullet shape") in risk-return space. The curvature comes from their imperfect correlation.

2
Add more & more securities (N → ∞)

Each additional asset adds more combinations. The set of all feasible portfolios forms a filled "egg" shape. The upper-left edge of that shape is the Efficient Frontier.

3
Introduce a risk-free asset

You can now lend/borrow at the risk-free rate Rƒ. This transforms the efficient frontier into a straight line — the Capital Market Line (CML).

The Efficient Frontier is the upper-left edge of all feasible portfolios. M is the market portfolio — where the CML is tangent to the frontier.
🎯
Key insight: Any rational investor should hold a portfolio ON the efficient frontier. A portfolio below or inside the frontier means you're accepting unnecessary risk for your return level — that's irrational. The tangency point M (the market portfolio) is special because it has the highest Sharpe ratio of all possible portfolios.

CH8 · CAPM — The Capital Asset Pricing Model

CAPM gives us a precise formula linking risk (measured by beta) to the expected return of any security.

📐 Two Key Lines

Capital Market Line (CML)

For portfolios. Uses total risk (σ) on the x-axis. Describes the best possible risk/return trade-off achievable by mixing the risk-free asset with portfolio M.

Security Market Line (SML)

For individual securities. Uses beta on the x-axis. This is the formula we actually use in practice to price individual assets. This is CAPM.

CML — Capital Market Line (for Portfolios)
E(Rₚ) = Rƒ + [(E(Rₘ) − Rƒ) / σₘ] × σₚ
Rƒ = risk-free rate
E(Rₘ) = expected market return
σₘ = standard deviation of the market portfolio
σₚ = standard deviation of your portfolio
(E(Rₘ)−Rƒ)/σₘ = Sharpe ratio of the market
SML — Security Market Line (CAPM) — for Individual Securities
E(Rᵢ) = Rƒ + βᵢ × (E(Rₘ) − Rƒ)
E(Rᵢ) = expected return on security i (the "fair" return given its risk)
Rƒ = risk-free rate
βᵢ = beta of security i
E(Rₘ) − Rƒ = market risk premium (typically ~5–7% historically)
The SML: every security should plot ON this line in equilibrium. Securities above are underpriced (good buys), securities below are overpriced.
CAPM Worked Example

Given: Rƒ = 3.5%, E(Rₘ) = 10%, β = 1.2 (company shares)

E(R) = 3.5% + 1.2 × (10% − 3.5%)
= 3.5% + 1.2 × 6.5%
= 3.5% + 7.8% = 11.3%

This 11.3% is the minimum return shareholders require for bearing this level of market risk.

⚠️
Does CAPM hold perfectly? Studies show high-beta portfolios do earn more than low-beta ones, but the difference is smaller than CAPM predicts. This led to the development of multi-factor models.

CH8 · APT & Fama-French 3-Factor Model

CAPM uses one factor (market beta). What if other risk factors matter too?

🔬 Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT)

Stephen Ross (1976) proposed that multiple macroeconomic factors drive returns, not just market risk:

APT Formula
E(Rᵢ) = Rƒ + β₁·RP₁ + β₂·RP₂ + … + βₙ·RPₙ
βₙ = sensitivity of the security to factor n
RPₙ = risk premium for factor n (e.g., GDP growth, inflation, interest rates)

3️⃣ Fama-French 3-Factor Model

Eugene Fama & Kenneth French (1992) identified three specific risk factors that empirically explain stock returns better than CAPM alone:

Factor 1 · Market Risk

Rₘ − Rƒ

The classic market risk premium. Still the most important factor.

Factor 2 · Size (SMB)

Small – Big

Small-cap stocks historically outperform large-cap stocks. Bearing size risk earns an extra premium.

Factor 3 · Value (HML)

High – Low B/M

Value stocks (high book-to-market ratio) historically outperform growth stocks.

Fama-French 3-Factor Formula
E(Rᵢ) = Rƒ + β₁(Rₘ−Rƒ) + β₂·SMB + β₃·HML
SMB = Small Minus Big (size premium)
HML = High Minus Low book-to-market (value premium)
Each β measures the sensitivity of the stock to that specific factor
💡
Why does value outperform growth? Value companies (old, cheap stocks trading below book value) are riskier businesses facing financial distress — investors demand a higher return to own them. It's not a "free lunch" but compensation for a hidden risk.

CH9 · Cost of Equity (COE)

The Cost of Equity is the return that shareholders require for investing in the company's stock. It is estimated using the SML (CAPM).

💰 Calculating COE with CAPM

Cost of Equity
COE = Rƒ + β × (E(Rₘ) − Rƒ)
Rƒ = risk-free rate → use 10-year government bond yield
β = beta of the company's stock (from regression)
E(Rₘ) = expected market return
E(Rₘ) − Rƒ = market risk premium (typically 5–7%)
COE Example — Standard CAPM
10-year government bond (Rƒ)3.5%
Expected market return E(Rₘ)10.0%
Market risk premium6.5%
Beta of company (β)1.2
Security risk premium = 1.2 × 6.5%7.8%
Required COE = 3.5% + 7.8%11.3%
COE with Small-Firm Premium (F&F)
Market risk premium = 1.2 × 6.5%7.8%
Additional size premium (small firm)1.0%
Required COE = 3.5% + 7.8% + 1.0%12.3%

If this is a small company, investors demand an extra 1% for the size risk, raising the required return to 12.3%.

🌍
Damodaran's 2022 global data: Median Cost of Equity across all industries globally = 7.12%. Varies hugely — from ~5% for utilities to 12%+ for emerging market tech. You can look up sector-specific COE estimates on Damodaran's NYU website.

CH9 · WACC — Weighted Average Cost of Capital

A company is funded by both equity holders and debt holders. WACC blends their required returns into a single company-wide hurdle rate.

🏗️ WACC Formula

WACC Formula
WACC = (E/V)·rₑ + (D/V)·r_d·(1−t)
E = market value of equity
D = market value of debt
V = E + D = total firm value
rₑ = cost of equity (from CAPM/SML)
r_d = cost of debt (yield on company's bonds or from financial statements)
t = corporate tax rate
(1−t) = tax shield on debt — interest is tax-deductible, reducing real cost of debt
🏦
Why is debt multiplied by (1−t)? Interest payments on debt are tax-deductible. If you pay 5% interest but your tax rate is 25%, the government effectively subsidizes 1.25% of it — your real after-tax cost of debt is only 3.75%. This tax shield makes debt cheaper than equity, which is why companies use leverage.
💡
Extended WACC — if the company also has mezzanine/hybrid financing (M), just add another term:

WACC = (E/V)·rₑ + (D/V)·r_d·(1−t) + (M/V)·r_m

🔽 Managing & Minimizing WACC

A lower WACC directly increases company value (higher NPV for the same projects). Management can influence WACC:

LeverHow It Reduces WACCCaution
Add Leverage Debt is cheaper than equity (after-tax). Shifting the capital structure toward debt reduces average cost. Up to a point — too much debt raises financial distress risk, increasing both r_d and rₑ.
Reduce Equity Risk Lower beta → lower COE → lower WACC. Achieved through stable cash flows, diversification, hedging. Limited control — beta is partly driven by industry.
Renegotiate Debt Refinance at lower rates during low interest rate environments. Highly actionable when rates fall.
🎯
WACC is the hurdle rate. If a project returns more than WACC, it creates value. If it returns less, it destroys value. This is the direct link between risk measurement and investment decision-making — the whole purpose of Part 2 of corporate finance.
🌐
Damodaran's 2022 global data: Median WACC across all industries globally = 6.33% (slightly below the median COE of 7.12%, as expected since debt is cheaper). WACC for high-risk industries can exceed 10–12%.

Full Worked Example · AB InBev WACC

Let's apply everything step by step using real-world data for the world's largest brewer.

📋 Given Information

AB InBev — Input Data
Risk-free rate (Rƒ)3.0%
Beta (β)1.32
Expected market return E(Rₘ)8.0%
Market risk premium E(Rₘ) − Rƒ5.0%
Debt / (Debt + Equity) — D/(D+E)42%
Equity / (Debt + Equity) — E/(D+E)58%
Cost of Debt (r_d)4.65%
Marginal Tax Rate (t)25%
1
Calculate Cost of Equity (COE) using CAPM
COE = 3.0% + 1.32 × 5.0% = 3.0% + 6.6% = 9.6%
2
Calculate After-Tax Cost of Debt
r_d × (1−t) = 4.65% × (1−0.25) = 4.65% × 0.75 = 3.4875%
3
Calculate WACC
WACC = 0.58 × 9.6% + 0.42 × 3.4875%
= 5.568% + 1.4648%
≈ 7.03%
4
Apply the Results

Use 9.6% as the discount rate when valuing AB InBev shares (e.g., in a DDM model — equity investor perspective).

Use 7.03% as the hurdle rate when evaluating AB InBev's own investment projects (e.g., building a new brewery — whole company perspective).
