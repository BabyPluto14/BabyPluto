📖 The Story: A Data Detective Agency

The Agency Opens Imagine you run a detective agency. A client walks in with a massive file of raw numbers — exam scores, salaries, temperatures. Your job is not to make predictions about other people (that's inferential statistics). Your job is to describe exactly what is in that file. You are a Descriptive Data Detective.
Chapter D1 — Sorting the Evidence First, you sort the evidence into types. Hair colour? Qualitative — you can't average it. Heights? Quantitative — you can. You group clues into neat frequency tables and draw charts: needle graphs for countable things, histograms for measurable things.
Chapter D2 — Profiling a Suspect (One Variable) You pick one variable — say, exam scores — and profile it completely. Where is it centred? (Mean, median, mode.) How spread out is it? (Standard deviation, IQR.) What shape does it take? (Skewed, symmetric, bimodal?) You build a boxplot to spot outliers. You also learn that outliers are suspects, not convicts — they need investigation, not automatic deletion.
Chapter D3 — Two Suspects Together A new question: do two variables move together? You set up a contingency table, plot a scatterplot, and compute correlation. If r is close to ±1, the suspects are linked. If r ≈ 0, they act independently. Linear combinations let you build new variables from old ones — like a weighted score sheet.
D0 — What Is Descriptive Statistics?

KEY IDEA Descriptive statistics summarises the data you actually have. It makes no claims about any wider population.

The Golden Rule Stay inside your sample. If you have test scores for 30 students, you can say "the average in this class is 72." You cannot say "the average for all students everywhere is 72." That step requires inferential statistics.

Descriptive

Summarises data you have.
"The mean salary in this company is €45k."

Inferential

Generalises to a wider population.
"We estimate the national mean salary is €47k ± 2k."

Two Types of Summary

Numerical Summaries

Single numbers: mean, median, standard deviation, quartiles, correlation…

Graphical Summaries

Pictures: bar chart, histogram, boxplot, scatterplot, frequency polygon…
D1 — Variable Types & Frequency Tables

Variable Taxonomy

Qualitative (Categorical)

  • Nominal — no natural order. Eye colour, blood type, hair colour. You can count categories; you cannot rank them.
  • Ordinal — categories have a meaningful order but distances are unclear. Satisfaction (1 = unhappy, 5 = delighted).

Quantitative (Numerical)

  • Discrete — countable, gaps between values. Number of children: 0, 1, 2, …
  • Continuous — any value in an interval. Weight, time, income.
Warning: Don't Average Colours A common trap: you code hair colour as 1 = blond, 2 = brown, 3 = black, then compute an average of 2.1. This is meaningless. The codes are arbitrary labels, not measurements. Nominal data must only be counted, never averaged.

Quantitative — Continuous: The Histogram

Group observations into bins [aᵢ, bᵢ) of width bᵢ = aᵢ₊₁ − aᵢ. Draw bars — but what height?

Count histogram: bar height = nᵢ
Misleading when bins have unequal widths!
Density histogram: bar height = hᵢ = p̂ᵢ / bᵢ
Area = probability. Always correct.
Density histogram bar height
hᵢ = p̂ᵢ / bᵢ = (nᵢ/n) / bᵢ
Area of bar i = hᵢ × bᵢ = p̂ᵢ → total area = Σp̂ᵢ = 1
Unequal Bin Widths — Use Density, Not Count Suppose you have bins of width 5 for low incomes but width 20 for high incomes. A count histogram gives the wide bins artificially tall-looking bars, implying more data density than really exists. Dividing by bin width corrects this. When bins are equal, both graphs are equivalent in shape.

Shape Vocabulary

"The Statistician Who Drowned" Analogy A statistician drowned crossing a river of average depth 30 cm. A single outlier — one very deep spot — pulled the mean up, but the median would have been 20 cm and saved her. Right-skewed distributions: a few very large values drag the mean far above the median. This is why salaries and house prices are always reported with the median, not the mean.
Skewness Rule of Thumb Right-skewed: mode < median < mean. Left-skewed: mean < median < mode. Symmetric: mean ≈ median ≈ mode.
D2.2 — Measures of Centre and Spread

Measures of Centre

Mean
x̄ = (1/n) Σxᵢ
Balance point. Sensitive to outliers.
Median
Middle value
Sort data; robust to outliers.
Mode
Most frequent
Only centre for qualitative data.
Weighted mean (from frequency table) When you have relative frequencies p̂ᵢ and midpoints mᵢ: x̄ = Σ p̂ᵢ · mᵢ — each midpoint weighted by its proportion.

Measures of Spread

Sample Variance
s² = (1/(n−1)) Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²
Divide by (n−1), not n. Units are squared. Always ≥ 0.
Sample Standard Deviation
s = √s²
Same units as the data. Typical distance from the mean.
Why divide by n−1 and not n? We compute deviations from x̄ (the sample mean). Because those deviations must sum to zero — Σ(xᵢ − x̄) = 0 — only n−1 of the n deviations are free to vary. The last one is fixed once the others are known. Dividing by n−1 gives an unbiased estimator of the population variance σ². Dividing by n would systematically underestimate it.
Coefficient of Variation (CV)
CV = s / |x̄|
Relative spread; dimensionless. Compare variability across different scales.

D2.3 — EDF, Percentiles, Quartiles & Boxplots

Empirical Distribution Function (EDF)

The EDF tells you: "what fraction of observations are ≤ x?"

EDF Definition
F̂ₙ(x) = #{xᵢ ≤ x} / n
Step function: starts at 0, jumps by 1/n at each data point, ends at 1.

The EDF is the raw, empirical version of a theoretical CDF. It jumps at each observed value and stays flat in between.

Percentiles (SPSS Method)

The k-th percentile Pₖ is the value below which k% of the data falls.

1
Sort data in ascending order: x₍₁₎ ≤ x₍₂₎ ≤ … ≤ x₍ₙ₎
2
Compute position: L = (n+1) · k/100 = i.j
3
Interpolate: Pₖ = x₍ᵢ₎ + j · (x₍ᵢ₊₁₎ − x₍ᵢ₎)
✓
If L is an integer (j = 0), Pₖ = x₍ᵢ₎ exactly.
Worked Example n = 9 scores: 12, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25, 27, 30, 35 (already sorted).
Find P₂₅: L = 10 · 0.25 = 2.5, so i = 2, j = 0.5.
P₂₅ = x₍₂₎ + 0.5(x₍₃₎ − x₍₂₎) = 15 + 0.5(18 − 15) = 15 + 1.5 = 16.5

Quartiles & IQR

The Boxplot (Box-and-Whisker Plot)

How to draw a boxplot
1
Draw a box from Q1 to Q3 (the IQR).
2
Mark the median (Q2) with a vertical line inside the box.
3
Compute fences: lower = Q1 − 1.5·IQR; upper = Q3 + 1.5·IQR.
4
Whiskers extend to the most extreme data points within the fences.
5
Any point beyond a whisker is plotted individually as a suspected outlier.
Outlier = Suspect, Not Convict The 1.5·IQR rule flags unusual points for investigation. You do not automatically delete them. Ask: Is it a data entry error? A genuine extreme value? A different sub-population? Report what you find; never silently drop data.
D2.4 — Transformations and Z-Scores

Linear Transformations: g(x) = a + b·x

When you add a constant, multiply by a constant, or both:

OperationEffect on MeanEffect on Median/ModeEffect on s & IQREffect on s²
Shift: y = x + ax̄ + a+aunchangedunchanged
Scale: y = b·xb · x̄× b× |b|× b²
Both: y = a + b·xa + b·x̄transformed same way× |b|× b²
Intuition Shifting moves the whole distribution along the number line without changing its shape. Scaling stretches or compresses it. Adding 10 to every exam score shifts the mean up by 10; it doesn't change the variation (everyone shifted equally). Multiplying all temperatures by 2 (F → rough C conversion) doubles the spread.
Logarithmic Transformation: g(x) = ln(x) For heavily right-skewed data (incomes, company sizes), log-transforming compresses the long right tail and often produces a more symmetric distribution. The log of a product is a sum, so this also helps with multiplicative relationships.

Z-Scores (Standardisation)

Z-Score
z = (x − x̄) / s
After standardising: mean = 0, standard deviation = 1. Units disappear.
The Nephews Example Two nephews: Tom scored 68 on a maths exam (class mean 60, s = 8) and Sara scored 45 on a science exam (class mean 40, s = 4). Who did better relative to their class?
Tom: z = (68−60)/8 = +1.00 — one SD above the mean.
Sara: z = (45−40)/4 = +1.25 — 1.25 SDs above the mean.
Sara did better relative to her class, even though her raw score was lower.
Z-score properties Negative z: below the mean. z = 0: exactly at the mean. z = +2: two SDs above the mean (unusual). Z-scores let you compare values from completely different scales — apples and oranges become comparable.
D2.5 — Binned Data

When data arrive pre-grouped into bins (e.g., from a survey or published table), the individual observations are lost. You can still compute summaries — with a key assumption:

Uniformity Assumption Within each bin, assume observations are uniformly distributed. This means you approximate each observation by the bin midpoint mᵢ = (aᵢ + bᵢ)/2.

Summary Statistics from Binned Data

Approximate Mean
x̄ ≈ (1/n) Σ nᵢ·mᵢ = Σ fᵢ·mᵢ
Treat all observations in bin i as equal to the midpoint mᵢ.
Approximate Variance
s² ≈ (1/(n−1)) Σ nᵢ·(mᵢ − x̄)²
Or equivalently: Σ fᵢ·mᵢ² − x̄² (computing shortcut).
Mean = Balance Point On a density histogram, the mean is exactly the point where the histogram would balance if placed on a see-saw. A right-skewed histogram has a long right tail pulling the balance point (mean) to the right of the peak (mode).

EDF and Percentiles from Binned Data

Inside a bin, use linear interpolation. For a value x in bin [aᵢ, bᵢ):

Interpolated EDF
F̂ₙ(x) ≈ Fᵢ₋₁ + fᵢ · (x − aᵢ) / bᵢ
Fᵢ₋₁ is the cumulative freq up to the start of bin i.
Percentile from bins (invert)
Pₖ ≈ aᵢ + bᵢ · (k/100 − Fᵢ₋₁) / fᵢ
Find the bin that contains the k-th percentile (where Fᵢ₋₁ ≤ k/100 ≤ Fᵢ), then interpolate.
Worked Example — Median from bins Using the table above, find the median (P₅₀, i.e. k = 0.50).
F₁ = 0.20 < 0.50 ≤ F₂ = 0.70 → median is in bin [20, 40).
P₅₀ ≈ 20 + 20 · (0.50 − 0.20)/0.50 = 20 + 20 · 0.60 = 20 + 12 = 32

Side-by-Side Boxplots (Qual × Quant)

Draw one boxplot per category of the qualitative variable, all on the same scale. This lets you compare centres, spreads, and outliers across groups simultaneously.

From the plot above: Group B has a higher median and is more spread out than Group A. Group B also has one suspected outlier (the open circle). This pattern would not be visible in separate histograms unless you aligned them carefully.

Scatterplots (Quant × Quant)

Plot ordered pairs (xᵢ, yᵢ). Each person/object is one dot. You observe form (linear/curved?), direction (positive/negative?), strength (tight cluster or wide scatter?), and outliers.

The red dashed line is the trend. Most points follow it (positive linear association). One orange dot is far from the trend — a candidate outlier worth investigating.

Covariance and Correlation

Sample Covariance
sₓᵧ = (1/(n−1)) Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ)
Positive: x and y move together. Negative: they move in opposite directions. Units: x-units × y-units.
Why covariance is hard to interpret If you measure height in cm and weight in kg, the covariance is in "cm·kg" — a meaningless unit. Double all heights (now in half-cm) and the covariance doubles too. We need a scale-free measure.
Pearson Correlation Coefficient
r = sₓᵧ / (sₓ · sᵧ)
Always in [−1, 1]. Dimensionless. Equal to average product of z-scores.
Correlation ≠ Causation r measures linear association only. A high correlation doesn't imply that x causes y. Ice cream sales and drowning rates are positively correlated — both are driven by hot weather (a confounding variable). Also: r can be zero even when x and y have a strong nonlinear relationship (e.g., y = x²).
Correlation and linear transformations Correlation is invariant to linear transformations of x or y. If you convert heights from cm to inches and weights from kg to lbs, r stays the same. This is a direct consequence of r being a ratio of the covariance to standard deviations.

Linear Combinations of Variables

You often need to build a new variable Y as a weighted sum of existing variables X₁, X₂, …, Xₖ:

Linear Combination
Y = a₀ + a₁X₁ + a₂X₂ + … + aₖXₖ
Each aᵢ is a fixed constant (weight).

Effect on Mean

Mean of Y
ȳ = a₀ + a₁x̄₁ + a₂x̄₂ + … + aₖx̄ₖ
Linearity of expectation — weights carry straight through.

Effect on Variance

Variance of Y (two variables)
s²ᵧ = a₁²s²ₓ₁ + a₂²s²ₓ₂ + 2a₁a₂sₓ₁ₓ₂
sₓ₁ₓ₂ is the covariance between X₁ and X₂.
Why the covariance term matters If X₁ and X₂ move together (positive covariance), combining them amplifies variance. If they move in opposite directions (negative covariance), combining them reduces variance — this is the logic behind portfolio diversification in finance. Two perfectly negatively correlated assets (r = −1) cancel each other's fluctuations entirely.
Worked Example — Weighted Exam Score Final grade = 0.4·Midterm + 0.6·Final.
Suppose x̄_mid = 65, x̄_fin = 70, s_mid = 8, s_fin = 10, r = 0.5, so s_mid,fin = 0.5 × 8 × 10 = 40.
Mean: ȳ = 0.4(65) + 0.6(70) = 26 + 42 = 68
Variance: s²ᵧ = 0.16(64) + 0.36(100) + 2(0.4)(0.6)(40) = 10.24 + 36 + 19.2 = 65.44
SD: sᵧ = √65.44 ≈ 8.09
Special case: Y = X₁ − X₂
s²ᵧ = s²ₓ₁ + s²ₓ₂ − 2sₓ₁ₓ₂
Differences can have larger or smaller variance than either component, depending on the sign of covariance.

Common exam traps

Dividing variance by n instead of n−1 Always n−1 for the sample variance.
Using count histogram with unequal bins Always use density (hᵢ) when bin widths differ.
Confusing covariance sign with correlation sign They always have the same sign, but covariance isn't bounded.
Claiming causation from correlation r describes association, never causation.
Deleting outliers automatically Flag, investigate, report — never silently delete.
