Adjusting Entries & Changes in Inventories

Year-end housekeeping: shifting revenues and expenses into the period they truly belong to, and correcting the books for stock still on the shelf. This is where the books finally tell the truth.

Core idea: the matching principle The 2×2 accrue/defer matrix Textbook pp. 123–141 interactive
◆ The one question behind the whole chapter

"Does this euro of expense or revenue belong to this accounting year — regardless of when the cash actually moved?" If cash and period don't line up, you make an adjusting entry. If purchases and sales left stock behind, you book a change in inventory. Both are end-of-period entries on 31/12.

01

Where this fits — and defer vs. accrue

Adjusting entries are made at 31/12, after the first trial balance and before the financial statements — corrections that align the books with the correct period.

In the accounting cycle, after all transactions are recorded and the inventory (stock-take) is drawn up, you make end-of-period entries. This chapter covers two kinds; others (depreciation, provisions) come later.

Transactions recorded→1st trial balance→Inventory→End-of-period entries→2nd trial balance→Financial statements

The two verbs at the heart of it

◀ DEFER — push into the future

Move an expense/revenue to a future accounting period. Used when cash moved now but part of it belongs to next year. You're removing the too-early part from this year.

▶ ACCRUE — pull into now

Take an expense/revenue from a future period into the current one. Used when the economic event happened now but cash will move next year. You're adding the not-yet-recorded part to this year.

⚠ Two rules you must never forget
  • VAT is never an expense or a revenue — adjusting entries carry no VAT. They only touch a 6/7 account and a 490–493 account.
  • Accruals & deferrals ignore the timing of actual payment. The question is only which period the economic benefit belongs to. Cash timing is what creates the mismatch you're fixing.

◆ How to read the colours

Blue = asset (490, 491): something owed to you — a prepayment not yet consumed, or income earned but not yet collected. Debited on 31/12.
Rust = liability (492, 493): something owed by you — an expense used but unpaid, or income banked but not yet earned. Credited on 31/12.

✎ Fills the verbal gap — the reversal on 01/01

Every adjusting entry is reversed ("booked off") on the first day of the next year. Why? So that when the cash finally moves (you pay the interest, receive the rent), the ordinary transaction can be booked normally and the amounts land in the right year automatically — the reversal cancels the double-count. Every example below shows this three-step rhythm: book cash → adjust at 31/12 → reverse at 01/01.

Deferred expenses · OMEGA KAPPA plc

You paid the whole expense up front, but part of the benefit runs into next year. Push that part out to account 490.

On 01/08/20X0, OMEGA KAPPA pays a fire-insurance premium of 1 800,00/year in advance. Cover runs 01/08/20X0 → 31/07/20X1. No VAT. Year-end is 31/12/20X0.

Split: 5/12 × 1 800 = 750,00 belongs to 20X0; 7/12 × 1 800 = 1 050,00 belongs to 20X1 → deferred.

StepEntryDC
1 · 01/08 pay premium61 Services and other goods D / 5500 Bank C1 800,001 800,00

◆ Result

Expense hitting the 20X0 income statement = 1 800 − 1 050 = 750,00. In 20X1 the reversal puts 1 050,00 into that year. Account 490 sits on the 20X0 balance sheet as a current asset (a prepayment) until it's booked off.

✎ Fills the verbal gap — two separate 31/12 jobs

Don't conflate them. Reclassification (step 2) answers "how much of this long-term debt is now due within 12 months?" → move 50 000 from 173 to 423 so the balance sheet shows short-term debt correctly. Accrual (step 3) answers "how much interest belongs to this year?" → 11 250 into the income statement. Different questions, different accounts. Interest in 20X1 = 15 000 paid − 11 250 reversed = 3 750,00.

08

Changes in inventories — the problem

During the year, purchases go to expense and sales to revenue, and the inventory account never moves. That only tells the truth if you sold exactly what you bought.

The set-up (from Chapter 5): all purchases → 604 (expense), all sales → 70x (revenue), and the balance of 340 Inventory of goods for resale stays frozen all year. So the books show:

▤ Accounting result vs. actual result

Accounting result = 70x sales − 604 purchases
Actual result     = sales price of goods sold − cost of goods SOLD

These match only when purchases = cost of goods sold, i.e. closing inventory = opening inventory. Any leftover (or run-down) stock breaks the equality.

VIVA VELO plc — a wholesaler of mountain bikes (cost 500, sell 800)

In 20X1 the books understate profit by 500 000 (purchases include 1 000 unsold bikes). In 20X2 they overstate it by 375 000 (9 750 sold but only 9 000 bought — 750 came from opening stock). The fix aligns them.

◆ The two directions (memorise this)
  • Inventory INCREASES (closing > opening): 340 D / 6094 C → operating expenses ↓. You bought more than you sold, so some "purchases" aren't really this year's cost.
  • Inventory DECREASES (closing < opening): 6094 D / 340 C → operating expenses ↑. You sold from stock, so cost of goods sold exceeds this year's purchases.

In the income statement this shows on line 609 "Inventories: decrease (increase)" inside operating expenses group 60. Cost of goods sold = purchases − change in inventory.

◆ Where to read & practise

Textbook A Practical Guide to Financial Accounting, Chapter 6 (pp. 123–141). Practise with Ex. 1: DINGO (p. 142) and Ex. 2: FIGARITA (p. 143). Use the Module 7 & 9 builders to self-check.

⚠ Top traps for this chapter
  • Assets vs. liabilities in the 2×2: 490 & 491 are assets (debited 31/12); 492 & 493 are liabilities (credited 31/12).
  • Advance vs. arrear decides defer vs. accrue: cash-now → defer the future slice; cash-later → accrue the current slice.
  • Never put VAT in an adjusting entry.
  • Reversal on 01/01 is part of the answer — don't stop at the 31/12 entry.
  • Inventory direction: stock up → expenses down (340 D / 6094 C); stock down → expenses up (6094 D / 340 C).
  • Reclassification ≠ accrual: LT→ST (173→423) is a separate 31/12 job from accruing interest.
