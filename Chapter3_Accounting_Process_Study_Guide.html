01

The accounting cycle — the map before the details

Everything in this chapter is one step of a single, repeating annual loop. Learn the loop first; then each topic has a home.

A company doesn't record transactions at random. Belgian accounting runs on a fixed annual cycle. The slides show it as a vertical flow — here it is with the "why" of each step filled in (the part usually explained out loud in class).

1
Opening balance sheet
Start of the year. Every balance-sheet item is opened as an account in the journal and general ledger, carrying last year's closing figures as opening balances (OB).
2
Record all transactions
Throughout the year, every dated source document (invoice, bank extract…) is journalised and posted to the ledger. This is where 90% of daily bookkeeping lives.
3
1st provisional trial balance
An interim check that total debits = total credits. Catches arithmetic/posting errors before year-end work. Not legally required — it's your safety net.
4
Inventory
A physical/real-world stock-take: what do the assets, debts and claims actually amount to on 31/12? Reality vs. the books.
5
End-of-period entries
Adjust the books to match reality: depreciation, stock changes, accruals, provisions, corrections. (Detail in later chapters — here we only close a simple case.)
6
2nd provisional trial balance
Re-run after adjustments. Now it reveals the result before tax (profit or loss).
7
Final trial balance
After tax and profit appropriation are booked.
8
Financial statements
Balance sheet + income statement + notes — the public output of the whole machine.
9
Taxes & profit distribution
Corporate tax on the result; the after-tax profit is then appropriated (reserves / carried forward / dividends).

02

The balance sheet — a snapshot of resources

A photograph of the company's financial position at one instant: what it uses, and where that came from.

Imagine someone with the itch to open a sandwich shop. Two questions drive the balance sheet, and they map onto its two sides:

◧ What do you need? → ASSETS

Operating resources. The building, refrigerated counter, oven, van (long-term), plus stock of bread, a bank account and cash (short-term). These are the use of funds.

◨ How do you finance it? → EQUITY + LIABILITIES

Financing resources. Own savings put in (equity/contributions), a bank loan, deferred payment from suppliers. These are the origin of funds.

The two ordering principles (a classic exam gap)

The template we use is not ordered alphabetically or by size. Each side has its own logic — memorise both directions:

SideOrdered by increasing…Top of the listBottom of the list
ASSETSliquidity (how fast it turns into cash)Fixed assets (building) — least liquidCash — most liquid
EQUITY + LIAB.callability (how soon it must be repaid)Equity — never "called back" (permanent)Short-term payables — due soonest
✎ Fills the verbal gap

Liquidity = nearness to cash. Callability = nearness to repayment. Assets flow from "hard to sell" down to "already cash"; financing flows from "permanent" (your own money) down to "pay it back next month." Equity sits at the top of the right side precisely because owners are the last to be repaid.

◆ The invariant

Because every asset was financed by something, the two totals are always equal: TOTAL ASSETS = TOTAL EQUITY + LIABILITIES. In the sandwich example both sides come to 214 250,00. A balance sheet that doesn't balance contains an error — full stop.

Every transaction keeps the equation balanced

There are exactly four ways a transaction can move the balance sheet, and all four preserve A = E + L:

PatternAssetsEquity + Liab.Example
Both sides up▲ A ↑▲ (E+L) ↑Buy furniture on credit
Both sides down▼ A ↓▼ (E+L) ↓Pay a supplier from the bank
Swap within assets▲↓ A = A—Customer pays: debtors ↓, bank ↑
Swap within financing—▲↓ (E+L) = (E+L)Convert a supplier debt into a bank loan

Formally: Δ Assets = Δ Equity + Δ Liabilities. Keep this in mind and no transaction can ever surprise you.

Balance-sheet accounts & the golden rules

You can't re-draw the whole balance sheet after every transaction. Instead, each line gets its own running tally: a T-account.

A T-account represents one balance-sheet item. Its shape gives it the name: a horizontal line with a vertical drop, forming a T. The left side is DEBIT, the right side is CREDIT. The collection of all T-accounts is the general ledger.

◆ The golden rule — memorise the "home side"

An item's opening balance sits on its "home" side, and increases go on the same side, decreases on the opposite side:

  • Assets are "debit-natured": home = debit. Increase → debit. Decrease → credit. They carry a debit balance (D > C).
  • Equity & Liabilities are "credit-natured": home = credit. Increase → credit. Decrease → debit. They carry a credit balance (C > D).

And the iron law behind every entry: total DEBIT = total CREDIT. This is why it's called double-entry.

The 4-step decision procedure

For any transaction, march through these four questions in order. This is the single most useful habit in the whole chapter — the trainer below drills exactly this.

1 · Which source document?→ 2 · Which account(s)? (asset / liability)→ 3 · Increase or decrease?→ 4 · Debit or credit?

Closing (balancing) an account

Closing an account = balancing it. The balance is the difference between the two column totals:

  • Total debit > total credit → debit balance (DB). You write the DB on the credit side to make both columns total the same, then carry it forward.
  • Total credit > total debit → credit balance (CB), written on the debit side.

Consistent with the golden rule: asset accounts close with a debit balance; equity/liability accounts close with a credit balance. Those closing balances become the figures on the closing balance sheet — assets (DB) on the left, equity + liabilities (CB) on the right.

Self-check: The "Suppliers" account has OB 30 250, credits of 4 235 + 7 623, and a debit of 4 235. What is its balance and on which side?

Credit side (a liability): OB 30 250 + 4 235 + 7 623 = 42 108 credit. Debit side: 4 235. Balance = 42 108 − 4 235 = 37 873 credit balance (CB). It appears under EQUITY + LIABILITIES on the balance sheet. (This is exactly TELECONNECT's Suppliers position on 28/02.)

04

VAT — the two-way street

Value Added Tax passes through the company. The trick is knowing when it's an asset and when it's a debt.

VAT (21% standard rate in Belgium) is collected on behalf of the tax authority. The company is a middleman — but the direction flips depending on whether it's buying or selling.

◧ On PURCHASES → "VAT to reclaim" (asset)

You pay VAT to your supplier on each purchase. The tax office will refund it to you. Until refunded, you hold a claim / receivable against the VAT administration.

Account 411 · Asset · increases on the DEBIT side.

◨ On SALES → "VAT due" (liability)

Your customer pays VAT to you on each sale. You must forward it to the tax office. Until paid, you owe a debt to the VAT administration.

Account 451 · Liability · increases on the CREDIT side.

✎ Fills the verbal gap — why two accounts?

Students often ask "isn't it all just VAT?" No — the company periodically nets them and settles the difference with the tax office. If VAT due (on sales) > VAT to reclaim (on purchases), you pay the state; if reclaim > due, the state owes you. Keeping them in separate accounts (an asset and a liability) is what makes that netting possible at period end.

The arithmetic you'll repeat constantly

Invoices are quoted excluding VAT. You add 21%:

Net (excl. VAT)× 21% = VAT amount+ net = Gross (total invoice)

E.g. 10 000,00 × 21% = 2 100,00 VAT → gross 12 100,00. The net hits the asset/expense account, the VAT hits 411 or 451, and the gross hits Suppliers or Trade debtors.

05

Income-statement accounts

The balance sheet is a snapshot of position; the income statement is a movie of performance over the year — how the result was earned.

Some transactions don't just swap resources around — they make the company richer or poorer. Selling smartphones brings in revenue; paying the phone bill is an expense. These are recorded in income-statement accounts, and their net difference is the result.

▤ Result of the period

Revenues > Expenses → PROFIT  ·  Revenues < Expenses → LOSS

Golden rules for income-statement accounts

Same logic as the balance sheet, extended. Think of it this way: expenses behave like assets (a "use" — debit-natured), and revenues behave like equity (a source of enrichment — credit-natured).

◆ One rule to bind them

Debit = Assets & Expenses increase / Equity, Liabilities & Revenues decrease.
Credit = Equity, Liabilities & Revenues increase / Assets & Expenses decrease.

Mnemonic: DEAD CLIC — Debits: Expenses, Assets, Drawings up · Credits: Liabilities, Income, Capital up.

✎ Fills the verbal gap — the extended decision step

From Module 3 the decision procedure gains a branch at step 2: an account might now be an asset/liability OR an expense/revenue. Once you've classified it, steps 3–4 are unchanged. That's the only new idea here.

Structure of the income statement

Revenues and expenses are split two ways: operating vs. financial (the nature of the activity) and recurring vs. non-recurring (whether it's part of normal business or a one-off, like a gain on disposal or fire damage).

Self-check: TELECONNECT buys 30 smartphones for resale at 140,00/unit excl. 21% VAT (entry 4). What are the three accounts, their types, and entries?

30 × 140 = 4 200,00 net; VAT 882,00; gross 5 082,00.

  • Purchases of goods for resale — expense (class 6) — increase → Debit 4 200,00
  • VAT to reclaim — asset — increase → Debit 882,00
  • Suppliers — liability — increase → Credit 5 082,00

Note: "purchases of goods for resale" is an expense, not an asset — a subtle but heavily-tested point.

The chart of accounts

A structured, numbered plan so that everyone books the same thing in the same place.

A chart of accounts is a structured plan in which all accounts are judiciously placed. It's a decimal system: the more digits, the more detail. Its two objectives are unity in terminology and easier auditing & comparison.

LevelDigitsExample
Class1 digit2 — Fixed assets
Group2 digits23 — Plant, machinery & equipment
Account3 digits232 — Equipment

The ten classes — the map you must know cold

ClassContentsStatement
1Equity, provisions, amounts payable > 1 yearBalance sheet (credit)
2Fixed assets & amounts receivable > 1 yearBalance sheet (debit)
3Inventories & contracts in progressBalance sheet (debit)
4Receivables & payables ≤ 1 year (incl. VAT, suppliers, debtors)Balance sheet (both)
5Current investments & cash at bank and in handBalance sheet (debit)
6Expenses (operating 60/64, financial 65, non-recurring 66, taxes 67, appropriation 69)Income statement

◆ Fast heuristic

Classes 1–5 = balance sheet; 6 = expenses, 7 = revenues. See a 6 → something cost money. See a 7 → something earned money.

Two refinements to watch (verbal-gap territory)

① Specific last digit

Within a tangible-asset account, the 4th digit encodes the nature of the amount:

  • …0 = cost of acquisition
  • …8 = revaluation surpluses
  • …9 = depreciation

So "cost of acquisition of machinery" (group 23, account 231) = 2310.

② Specific subaccount

Where one account covers several real-world items, subaccounts split them out — e.g. account 550 "current accounts at different banks":

  • 5500 = C/A at bank A
  • 5510 = C/A at bank B

A deeper level can even track cheques written / amounts written off per bank.

The 5-question booking plan

The chart of accounts slots into your procedure. For each account in a transaction ask:

Class?→Group?→Account number?→Increase / decrease?→Debit / credit?

07

The general ledger & the journal

Two books, two viewpoints on the same transactions: one organised by account, one organised by date.

▥ General ledger — organised by account

The book that holds all T-accounts. Documents are sorted and logically recorded per account. Each account gets a double page: left = debit amounts, right = credit amounts.

Question it answers: "What's the current state of this account?"

▤ Journal — organised by date

The accounting diary: entrepreneurs enter all transactions without delay, faithfully, completely and in time order. Every entry has a standardised form.

Question it answers: "What happened, and in what sequence?"

Anatomy of a journal entry

The standardised form has six parts. The convention: debited account(s) first, then @ (or "at") the credited account(s), with a description of the source document.

◆ Same data, two shapes

Journalising and posting to the ledger are two records of one event. In an exam you may be asked for either — a journal entry (debit lines then credit lines, columns equal) or the T-accounts (post each amount to the correct side).

08

The trial balance

An interim control that catches errors — not required by law, but indispensable in practice.

A trial balance lists every account with its totals. It comes in two flavours, and the difference between them is worth nailing down.

① Trial balance with FOOTINGS

For every account, take the total of the debit side and the total of the credit side (the "footings") and list both.

Check: Σ debit totals = Σ credit totals.

TELECONNECT: 182 372,25 = 182 372,25 ✓

② Trial balance with BALANCES

For every account, take the difference between debit and credit sides (i.e. close/balance it) and list that single balance in the debit or credit column.

Check: Σ debit balances = Σ credit balances.

TELECONNECT: 148 603,00 = 148 603,00 ✓

✎ Fills the verbal gap — footings vs. balances

Footings = raw column totals (both sides survive). Balances = the net (each account collapses to one figure). Footings prove your posting arithmetic is internally consistent; balances give you the numbers that actually flow into the financial statements. Note both checks can pass while a wrong account was used — a trial balance catches arithmetic errors, not every classification error.

The actual vs. accounting situation

At year-end the ledger's "accounting situation" is compared against the inventory's "actual situation." Where they differ, end-of-period entries bring the books into line (Module 9). The trial balance is where you stand just before that reconciliation.

Self-check: Your trial balance with footings does not balance. Name two likely causes.
  • A transaction was posted with debit ≠ credit (one side entered wrong / omitted).
  • A posting or addition error — an amount posted to the wrong side, or a column mis-totalled.

What it will not catch: booking to the wrong (but valid) account, or omitting a whole transaction — both sides stay equal.

09

From opening to closing balance sheet

The year-end finale: inventory, adjustments, determining the result, and the beautiful link between the two statements.

Inventory & end-of-period entries

The inventory is a detailed statement of all assets, receivables, debts and other liabilities reflecting the company's actual situation. Where the books disagree with reality, end-of-period entries correct them. Typical adjustments (detailed in later chapters):

  • Correcting errors
  • Recording correct year-end values: decreases in value (depreciation), increases in value, stock changes
  • Provisions; rescheduling of debts & receivables; closure of VAT accounts; negative bank balance
  • Accruals & deferrals (income-statement timing)

Determining the result — and the two views that must agree

Here is the conceptual payoff of the whole chapter. The profit (or loss) can be computed two independent ways, and they must give the same number:

View A · via the income statement

Σ revenue balances (class 7)  10 200,00
− Σ expense balances (class 6) −7 818,75
= Result              = 2 381,25

View B · via the balance sheet

Σ asset balances (DB)  140 784,25
− Σ equity+liab. balances (CB) −138 403,00
= Result               = 2 381,25

◆ Why they must match — the link

Both statements draw from the same ledger. Every transaction that created a profit also left more assets (or fewer liabilities) behind. So the "gap" that makes a raw closing balance sheet not balance (assets 140 784,25 vs. E+L 138 403,00) is exactly the profit sitting in the income-statement accounts. Move the result into equity and the balance sheet balances again. This is how a "balanced" balance sheet is prepared at year-end — an exam objective stated in those very words.

Appropriation of the result

The result is transferred out of the class-6/7 accounts into the balance sheet via an appropriation account (group 69 for profit, group 79 for loss). It can then be:

If PROFIT (69-group)If LOSS (79-group)
Added to equity (reserves)Withdrawn from equity (contributions ↓)
Carried forward to next year (profit carried forward)Carried forward to next year (loss carried forward)
Distributed to partners (creates a debt — dividend)Covered by partners (creates a receivable)

⚠ The five traps students fall into
  • "Purchases of goods for resale" is an expense (class 6), not an asset. Buying stock for resale is booked as an expense in this system.
  • VAT direction: reclaim (asset, on purchases) vs. due (liability, on sales). Get the side wrong and the whole entry unbalances.
  • Invoices are excl. VAT. The gross (net + 21%) is what the supplier/customer account carries; the net goes to the asset/expense line.
  • Equity sits on the credit side and increases with a credit — it feels backwards until DEAD CLIC is automatic.
  • Private-use split (e.g. the 80/20 phone bill): only the business share is an expense; the private share becomes a receivable from the manager (C/A manager), and VAT is split accordingly.
