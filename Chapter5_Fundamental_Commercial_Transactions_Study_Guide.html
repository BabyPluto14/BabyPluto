01

What counts as a commercial transaction

Buying and selling goods and services for the trading or production process — the core operating activity of the business.

A commercial transaction is buying goods/services to be used in the trading or production process, or selling goods/services. The critical exclusion:

⚠ Not a commercial transaction

Buying a fixed asset (a building, machine, vehicle) is an investment, not a commercial transaction. It's capitalised on the balance sheet, not booked as a purchase expense. Keep this line clear — it's a classic trap.

◧ What can be bought?
  • Manufacturers: raw materials & consumables
  • Traders: goods for resale
  • Both: services & other goods (rent, insurance, telephone…)
◨ What can be sold?
  • Manufacturers: finished goods
  • Traders: goods for resale
  • Service companies: services
✎ Fills the verbal gap

Purchases land in the income statement as an expense straight away — not as inventory. The stock you haven't sold yet is corrected only at year-end via a change-in-inventory end-of-period entry (Chapter 6). So during the year, "purchases" ≠ "what's still on the shelf."

03

How VAT really works

An indirect consumer tax collected in stages along the business chain — but borne entirely by the final consumer.

VAT is a tax on value added at each step. Companies collect it on behalf of the tax authorities under a system of netting: each business pays the state only the VAT on the value it added.

▤ The golden statement

VAT is neither an expense nor a revenue for the company. It flows through. VAT to reclaim (411) is an asset (paid to suppliers, reclaimable). VAT due (451) is a liability (collected from customers, owed to the state).

The three-stage flat-screen TV, worked out

A TV travels manufacturer → wholesaler → retailer → consumer. Watch how each firm remits VAT only on its margin, yet the state collects VAT on the full final price.

04

The VAT declaration — settling up by netting

Monthly or quarterly, the two VAT accounts are compared and the difference is settled with the tax office.

Periodically the company files a VAT declaration. The procedure is mechanical:

  1. Compare the balances of 411 (VAT to reclaim) and 451 (VAT due).
  2. Close the account with the smaller balance and transfer it into the larger one.
  3. The remaining balance is the net VAT due to (if 451 wins) or receivable from (if 411 wins) the state — then settled through the bank.

Worked example (the wholesaler)

411 shows 210,00 debit (VAT on the 1 000,00 purchase); 451 shows 315,00 credit (VAT on the 1 500,00 sale). 451 is larger → close 411 into 451.

◆ Three things this scheme quietly teaches
  • Commercial discount is inside the VAT basis (subtracted before NET), so it reduces the VAT. Additional charges too (added before NET) — they increase it.
  • Financial discount also reduces the VAT basis — but it's only booked when quick payment actually happens (accounts 757/657 hit at payment, not at invoicing).
  • The supplier/debtor line (440/400) carries the total as if no quick payment; VAT, however, is computed on the reduced (quick-payment) basis. That mismatch is the source of most mistakes.

06

Commercial discount

A trade discount on the goods themselves — part of normal operations, so it lands in the operating result.

A commercial (trade) discount is granted to boost sales, reward bulk orders, promote a product, compensate for damage, or favour an important customer. Because it's tied to the normal operating activity, it's classified under operating results:

  • Purchase invoice → operating expenses ↓ → 608 Discounts received (−), credit.
  • Sales invoice → operating revenues ↓ → 708 Discounts granted (−), debit.
✎ Two equally-correct entry methods

The slides show both. Gross method: book the full price to 604/70x and the discount separately to 608/708. Net method (alternative): book only the net (price − discount) straight to 604/70x and skip 608/708. Same NET, same VAT, same total — pick whichever the exercise asks for. The gross method keeps the discount visible; the net method is shorter.

07

Additional charges

Direct buying/selling costs added onto the invoice — transport, insurance, packaging.

Additional charges are direct costs of buying or selling: transport, loading/unloading, lost packaging, insurance. They move the operating result the opposite way to a discount:

  • Purchase invoice → operating expenses ↑ (folded into 604).
  • Sales invoice → operating revenues ↑ (folded into 70x).
◆ They're inside the VAT basis

Charges are added before the NET line, so VAT is charged on them too. In the slides they're simply merged into the 604/70x net figure rather than given their own account.

08

Financial discount (cash discount)

A reward for paying quickly. Because it's about timing of payment, it belongs to the financial result — and it's booked only when payment actually arrives.

A financial discount (discount for cash / quick payment) is offered if payment is made within a time limit. Paying early is a financing decision, so this discount is classified under financial results:

  • Purchase invoice → financial revenues ↑ → 757 Financial discounts received, credit.
  • Sales invoice → financial expenses ↑ → 657 Financial discounts granted, debit.
⚠ The three things everyone gets wrong
  1. VAT is computed on the reduced basis (after the financial discount), assuming the customer will pay quickly — even at invoicing time, before you know if they will.
  2. At invoicing, 757/657 are NOT booked. The supplier/debtor account carries the full "no quick payment" total; the discount only appears at the payment entry, and only if paid on time.
  3. If payment is late, there is no VAT correction. The buyer simply pays the full total (440 D / 5500 C for the whole amount); the reduced VAT stands.

Credit notes

A credit note is an invoice in reverse — a proportional correction for returns or after-the-fact discounts.

◧ Incoming credit note (ICN)

Drawn up by your supplier — you get money/credit back. Triggers: you returned purchased goods, or got an extra discount afterwards. Reverses a purchase invoice.

◨ Outgoing credit note (OCN)

Drawn up by you, as seller — you give credit back to a customer. Triggers: goods returned to you, or an extra discount you grant afterwards. Reverses a sales invoice.

◆ Just flip the sides

Every account that was debited on the original invoice is now credited, and vice-versa — scaled to the returned/discounted portion. Nothing new to learn; it's the invoice scheme run backwards.

Modes of payment

Bank, cash, cheque, and moving money between your own accounts. Each has its own document and account.

ModeDocumentCollecting a salePaying a purchase
Bank accountCurrent account extract5500 D ↑5500 C ↓

Cheque written outCheque—5501 C then cashed → 5500
✎ Why cheques take two entries

A cheque isn't instant cash — there's a gap between writing/receiving it and the bank actually moving the money. So a bridge account holds the amount in the meantime: 540 for cheques you've received, 5501 for cheques you've written. When the bank confirms via the account extract, you clear the bridge into 5500. Same idea powers internal transfers.

Internal transfers of funds — account 58

Moving cash between your own accounts (e.g. depositing cash at the bank) also spans two documents on two dates: the cash document and, later, the bank extract. Account 58 Internal transfers of funds bridges the timing gap and nets to zero once both sides post.

Exam readiness

The lecturer's objectives as a checklist. Tick each when you can do it unaided.

  • ✓Describe in my own words how VAT works (indirect tax, netting, borne by the final consumer).

  • ✓Record purchase/sales invoices with commercial discounts (gross & net methods).
  • ✓Record invoices with additional charges.
  • ✓Record invoices with financial discounts — VAT on reduced basis, discount booked at payment, no correction if late.
  • ✓Record incoming & outgoing credit notes as proportional reversals.
  • ✓Record payments by bank, cash and cheque (incl. the bridge accounts 540 / 5501).
  • ✓Record internal transfers of funds using account 58.
  • ✓Explain the impact of these transactions on the balance sheet and income statement.
◆ Where to read & practise

Textbook A Practical Guide to Financial Accounting, Chapter 5 (pp. 85–118). Practise with Ex. 1: AMERICANO (p. 119), Ex. 2: BLUE NOTE (p. 120), Ex. 3: GRASSHOPPER (p. 121). Tip: plug each exercise's numbers into the Module 5 calculator to self-check.

⚠ Top exam traps for this chapter
  • Commercial vs. financial discount: commercial → operating (608/708); financial → financial (757/657). Different accounts, different part of the income statement.
  • Financial discount timing: not booked at invoicing; VAT already on the reduced basis; no VAT correction if paid late.
  • 60 vs. 61: resale goods/materials (60) vs. services & other goods like rent/phone (61).
  • Fixed-asset purchase is not a commercial transaction — don't route it through 604.
  • Cheques & transfers need two entries via a bridge account (540 / 5501 / 58).
