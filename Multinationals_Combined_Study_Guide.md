## Four Theoretical Lenses the Course Keeps Returning To

1. **Transaction cost theory** — internalize an activity when market contracting is too costly or risky (basis of the internalization advantage in OLI).
2. **Resource-based view (RBV)** — success comes from valuable, rare, inimitable, organized internal resources (VRIO).
3. **Institution-based view** — success depends on fit with the host country's formal and informal institutions.
4. **Internationalization process models** — firms expand in stages, learning and committing gradually as uncertainty falls.

# 1. Globalization & Deglobalization
*Syllabus topic: Globalization · Textbook Chapter 1*

> **🟪 MOCK EXAM SIGNAL — Sels MC Q1 / Slangen open Q1a**
> MNE definition appeared in both mock exams. Know: all MNEs do IB, but not all IB firms are MNEs. Statement "all internationally-active firms are MNEs" is **WRONG** — MNE requires FDI (= managerial control), not just cross-border trade or portfolio investment.

---

## 1.1 Core Economic Indicators

**GDP (Gross Domestic Product)** — the sum of value added by all resident firms, households and government within a country, regardless of ownership. *Location-based: includes output by foreign firms in the country (e.g., Toyota in UK = UK GDP).*

**GNP (Gross National Product)** — the value added by all national (resident) firms, including those operating abroad. GNP = GDP + net income from abroad. *Nationality-based: captures output by a country's citizens regardless of location.*

**GNI (Gross National Income)** — GDP plus income received from non-resident sources such as remittances and dividends.

**PPP (Purchasing Power Parity)** — an adjustment using real rather than nominal values to control for inflation and differences in purchasing power between countries. Allows meaningful cross-country comparisons (€1 buys more in Nairobi than Oslo).

**TNI (Transnationality Index, UNCTAD)** — the mean of three ratios: foreign assets/total assets, foreign sales/total sales, and foreign employees/total employees, each weighted one-third. Average TI of leading MNEs ≈ 62%. Higher = more globally dispersed.

**GFCF (Gross Fixed Capital Formation)** — total investment in fixed assets: GFCF = foreign direct investment + domestic investment.

---

## 1.2 Defining Globalization

Globalization is the increasing economic, political and social interconnection of countries. Commonly described through five features:
1. **Technological acceleration** — rapid ICT and transport advances shrink distance.
2. **Rise of multinationals** — large MNE CEOs wield influence comparable to heads of state; may widen inequality.
3. **Job competition** — lower-skilled domestic workers face pressure from cheaper labour abroad.
4. **Cultural convergence** — similar brands and tastes appear across global cities.
5. **Inter-dependence** — crises spread instantly (e.g., 1998 Thai devaluation, 2008 subprime crisis).

---

## 1.3 Three Views on the Extent of Globalization

| Viewpoint | Proponent | Core idea | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flat world | Thomas Friedman (2005) | Globalization is inevitable and broadly uniform across the world. | Trade liberalization, digital communication, fall of political barriers. |
| Spiky world | Richard Florida (2005) | Globalization clusters in dense urban hubs; rural areas lag behind. | Innovation, VC, talent cluster in few peaks (NY, London, Shanghai, Tokyo). |
| Semi-globalization | Pankaj Ghemawat (2007) | Globalization is only partial — roughly 10% of activity is genuinely trans-national. | Cross-border flows typically represent only 10–25% of totals (immigration, calls, FDI, web traffic). |

Globalization moves in **waves** driven by technology (advances) and politics (setbacks). The 19th-century wave peaked ~1913 (FDI/GDP = 9%), collapsed with World Wars and protectionism; the current wave accelerated from the 1990s (world output grew 23%, trade expanded 80%, FDI flow increased fivefold). Since ~2008, a *'slowbalization'* debate has emerged.

---

## 1.4 The Big Question: Why Do Firms Succeed or Fail Globally?

**Unified Framework — two parallel lenses:**

1. **Institution-Based View** — Focuses on formal (laws, regulations) and informal (culture, norms) rules that shape the business environment. Two core propositions: (1) managers and firms pursue their interests rationally within given institutional constraints; (2) when formal constraints are unclear or fail, informal constraints play a larger role in reducing uncertainty.

2. **Resource-Based View** — Emphasizes a firm's internal resources and capabilities that can offset disadvantages of being an outsider.

> *"Institutions are the formal and informal rules of the game that explain firms' performance worldwide."*

**Liability of Foreignness / Outsidership** — The inherent disadvantage outsiders experience in a new environment due to lack of familiarity with local contexts, networks, and legitimacy. Three sources: (1) distant origins, (2) lack of local experience, (3) lack of nearby experience. Companies like Coca-Cola and Microsoft overcome this through firm-specific resources.

---

## 1.5 Measuring Internationalization

**Multinational Enterprise (MNE)** — a firm that engages in FDI and operates value-added activities in 2+ countries. Requires FDI and managerial control — not just exports or licensing.

**FDI vs. portfolio investment:** FDI gives direct control and management of foreign operations (≥10% voting shares); portfolio investment is passive ownership of foreign equities with no managerial control.

| Term | Definition | Key Distinction |
|---|---|---|
| International Business (IB) | Cross-border economic activities, or the action of doing business abroad | Covers both the international environment (I) and business operations (B) |
| Global Business | Business around the globe, including domestic activities affected by international forces | Broader than IB — includes domestic firms impacted by globalization |
| MNE | Firm that engages in FDI and operates value-added activities in 2+ countries | Requires FDI and managerial control, not just exports or licensing |
| FDI | Investment in controlling and managing value-added activities in other countries | Distinguished from FPI by element of managerial control |
| FPI (Foreign Portfolio Investment) | Investing in foreign securities for financial return only | No managerial control — purely financial. FDI ≠ FPI is a key exam distinction |

---

## 1.6 Deglobalization — Drivers & Trend Assessment

**Slow globalization** — the continuation of cross-border trade and investment at a reduced pace, without a systemic reversal — the trend most evidence currently supports.

**Seven drivers discouraging globalization (2016–2023):**
- Brexit — reduced EU–UK trade and investment.
- COVID-19 — supply-chain disruption, "just-in-case" inventories, protectionist stimulus.
- US tariffs — the "Trump-era" trade war, especially with China since 2018.
- WTO appellate body crisis — a shortage of dispute-resolution judges.
- Industrial policy — e.g., the CHIPS Act and semiconductor export restrictions.
- EU export controls on China — targeted technology curbs.
- Russia–Ukraine war — geopolitical decoupling in energy and defence.

**Why full deglobalization has not occurred:** Core global value chains (semiconductors, pharmaceuticals) remain deeply interdependent. FDI capital flows continue, though with heightened risk assessment. Trade volume has not collapsed — only its growth rate has decelerated. *The verdict: slow globalization, not reversal.*

---

## 1.7 Policy Tools & Strategic Sectors

**CHIPS Act (2022)** — US legislation allocating roughly $52 billion to domestic semiconductor R&D and manufacturing (≈¾ to manufacturing incentives, ¼ to R&D and workforce development), aimed at curbing technology transfer to China.

**Dual-use goods** — civilian items that can also serve military purposes; subject to export controls when the destination is judged a security risk.

**Excessive international specialization** — when a region relies heavily on a single external source for an essential input, so geopolitical conflict can cause severe supply disruption.

**Base of the Pyramid (BoP)** — The roughly 4 billion people earning less than €1,500/year who sit at the bottom of the global economic pyramid. C.K. Prahalad argued MNEs can serve the poorest profitably while addressing poverty — but only by fundamentally rethinking products, pricing, distribution, and business models (e.g., Unilever's single-use sachets, micro-finance, affordable mobile services).

*Case link:* Unilever's Indian subsidiary developed low-cost shampoo sachets for BoP consumers; the innovation was later adopted globally — an example of reverse knowledge transfer (see Section 7).

# 2. Formal Institutions & the Institution-Based View
*Syllabus topic: Formal and informal institutions · Textbook Chapter 2*

> **🟪 MOCK EXAM SIGNAL — Sels MC Q2 / Slangen MC Q2 (tested in BOTH mock exams)**
> CME vs LME, civil law vs common law is THE most tested distinction in the course. Key rule: **CME = civil law** (statutes/codes); **LME = common law** (precedents/case law). Exam trap: do NOT confuse civil law (statutes) with common law (case law) — options (a) and (b) in the mock Q reversed these definitions.

---

## 2.1 What Institutions Do

**Institution** — a set of formal rules (laws, regulations) and informal norms (culture, values) that shape the behaviour of economic agents and help to reduce uncertainty.

**Formal institutions** — codified rules — constitutions, commercial codes, property rights — forming the regulatory pillar.

**Informal institutions** — unwritten practices, cultural attitudes and ethical standards — the normative and cognitive pillars (covered in Section 3).

**Institutions reduce uncertainty:** predictable political and economic environments lower the cost of planning and investment, and knowledge of a host country's legal system lets a firm anticipate how disputes will be resolved. When institutions are strong, transaction costs are lower, trust is higher, and business operates more efficiently.

**Two core propositions of the institution-based view:**
- *Proposition 1* — Managers and firms rationally pursue their interests and make choices within the formal and informal institutional constraints in a given institutional framework.
- *Proposition 2* — In situations where formal constraints are unclear or fail, informal constraints will play a larger role in reducing uncertainty, guiding behaviour, and providing constancy. (Critical for understanding why culture matters so much — links to Section 3.)

---

## 2.2 ⬛ INSERT: Scott's Three Pillars of Institutions

> *Content from Exam Handbook Ch.2 — not in Study Guide. Appears in Exam Focus summary for Ch.2.*

**Three Pillars of Institutions (W. Richard Scott)**

| Pillar | Basis | Mechanisms | Logic | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Regulatory** | Laws, regulations, rules | Coercive (fines, sanctions, enforcement) | Expedience — what is legally required | EU competition law, GDPR, tax codes |
| **Normative** | Social norms, values, expectations | Normative pressure (shame, legitimacy, social approval) | Appropriateness — what is socially expected | CSR norms, professional standards |
| **Cognitive** | Taken-for-granted beliefs, shared mental models | Mimicry (imitation of 'normal' or 'obvious' behaviour) | Orthodoxy — what is taken for granted | Assumption that capitalism is the 'natural' economic system |

*Exam tip:* The three pillars explain why compliance is not just about law (regulatory) but also about social legitimacy (normative) and unquestioned assumptions (cognitive). When formal institutions are weak (low regulatory pillar), normative and cognitive pillars carry more weight — directly embodying Proposition 2.

## 2.3 Legal Systems — Common Law vs. Civil Law

**Civil law** — a legal tradition that uses comprehensive statutes and codes as the primary means to form legal judgments. Importance of the *judge* applying the code.

**Common law** — a legal tradition shaped by precedents and traditions from previous judicial decisions. Importance of the *lawyer* building case law arguments.

| Feature | Common law | Civil law |
|---|---|---|
| Source of law | Judicial precedent (prior decisions) | Codified statutes |
| Role of judges | Interpret and effectively create law | Apply the written code |
| Historical root | Anglo-Saxon England; medieval case law | Roman-Byzantine; Justinian's *Corpus Iuris Civilis* (529–534 CE) |
| Typical regions | UK, US, Canada, Australia | Continental Europe, Latin America, Japan, China |
| Impact on firms | Greater reliance on case law; higher litigation risk; very detailed contracts required | More predictable statutory framework; concise contracts acceptable |

**Extended legal systems:**

| System | Basis | Countries | Business Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Civil Law | Written codes, comprehensive statutes | Continental Europe, Japan, China, Latin America | Predictable but rigid; less adaptable to new situations |
| Common Law | Precedents from previous judicial decisions | UK, USA, Australia, Canada, India | Flexible, adaptive, but less predictable |
| Theocratic Law | Religious texts (e.g., Sharia, Halakha) | Saudi Arabia, Iran (partially) | Prohibitions on interest (riba), specific contract rules |
| Mixed/Customary | Blend of traditions (colonial + indigenous) | Many African, Asian nations | Complexity; need for local legal expertise |

*Contract & liability example:* a Finnish firm exporting a lawn-mower to the US must supply a long safety handbook (common-law liability risk); the same product sold in Belgium can rely on a brief disclaimer shifting responsibility to the user.

---

## 2.4 Market Types — Liberal vs. Coordinated Economies

**Liberal market economy (LME)** — relies on market mechanisms, flexible labour markets and extensive equity financing (e.g., UK, US, Canada).

**Coordinated market economy (CME)** — emphasises stakeholder coordination, strong employment protection and long-term relational/bank financing (e.g., Germany, Belgium, Austria).

LMEs host a large share of listed firms; CMEs retain many family-owned or bank-financed enterprises. **Vocational training** — a dual classroom-plus-workplace system — is strong in CMEs (Germany, Austria, Switzerland) and supports a stable, skilled labour pool.

| Dimension | Liberal Market Economy (LME) | Coordinated Market Economy (CME) |
|---|---|---|
| Legal tradition | **Common law** (precedent-based, case law) | **Civil law** (comprehensive statutes and codes) |
| Examples | UK, USA, Australia, Ireland, Canada | Germany, Japan, Scandinavia, France, Belgium |
| Corporate governance | Shareholder capitalism; dispersed ownership; outsider control; hostile takeovers possible | Stakeholder capitalism; concentrated ownership; insider control; limited hostile takeovers |
| Labour markets | Flexible, hire-and-fire, weak unions | Regulated, strong worker protections, codetermination, strong unions |
| Firm financing | Equity markets (stock exchange), venture capital | Bank-based, long-term lending relationships |
| Innovation pattern | Radical innovation (disruptive, fast) | Incremental innovation (continuous improvement, patient) |
| Inter-firm relations | Arm's-length, contract-based, competitive | Network-based, relational, collaborative (e.g., German Mittelstand) |

## 2.6 Intellectual Property

| IP right | Core feature | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Patent | Exclusive right to make, use, sell an invention for up to 20 years | Philips patents licensed widely; ASML originated from Philips R&D |
| Copyright | Protects original works from unauthorised reproduction | Textbooks, software, music |
| Trademark | Distinguishes goods via symbols, names, logos | Counterfeit "Crocodile" imitations of Lacoste |
| Trade secret | Confidential business information | The Coca-Cola recipe |
| Right of publicity | Controls commercial use of a person's name/likeness | Unauthorised use of a celebrity image |

**TRIPS agreement (1994, part of WTO)** — standardises IPR protection globally, but enforcement varies enormously. Piracy and counterfeiting remain widespread in weak-IPR countries. For MNEs: strong IPR protection encourages FDI and technology transfer; weak IPR increases dissemination risk, making firms prefer internalization (wholly-owned greenfield) over licensing.

## 2.7 Transaction Costs & Political Systems

**Transaction cost** — the "blood, sweat and tears" a firm incurs to negotiate, enforce and monitor agreements. Sources: legal uncertainty, political volatility, and corruption.

**Emerging economy** — a country with rapid GDP growth but still-developing institutions, frequent policy changes and an evolving legal framework (e.g., Vietnam, Indonesia, South Africa).

Political systems are classified along several dimensions: democratic vs. authoritarian; proportional representation (Belgium, Netherlands) vs. first-past-the-post (UK, US); direct vs. indirect election; centralized vs. decentralized.

| System | Characteristics | Business Implications |
|---|---|---|
| Democracy | Elected government, individual freedom, property rights protection, judicial independence, transparency | Predictable policy, rule of law, contract enforcement, lower political risk |
| Totalitarianism | Power concentrated in single leader/party, limited individual freedoms, weak property rights, censorship | Policy unpredictability, expropriation risk, corruption, but potential for rapid decisions |

**Political risk types:** (1) expropriation — government seizure of private assets; (2) currency/transfer restrictions; (3) sudden regulatory changes; (4) corruption and bribery demands; (5) civil unrest and political instability.

**Risk management strategies:** political risk insurance, diversification across countries, joint ventures with local partners (political cover), proactive government relations.

---

## 2.8 Institutional Mechanisms for Investment Disputes

**ICSID (International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes)** — a UN-affiliated arbitration body that resolves disputes between foreign investors and host states, but lacks power to enforce rulings against sovereign decisions.

**Anchor case — Repsol v. Argentina (preview of Section 9)**
Spain's Repsol held a joint venture stake in YPF; the Argentine government expropriated it. ICSID could issue opinions but could not compel reversal; Repsol recovered roughly 50% of its investment. *Lesson: investment-protection regimes have real limits once political risk escalates.*

# 3. Informal Institutions — Culture, Language & Religion
*Syllabus topic: Informal institutions · Textbook Chapter 3*

> **🟪 MOCK EXAM SIGNAL — Sels sample Q3**
> Hofstede's dimensions tested directly. Key traps: (a) high power distance = asking subordinates for feedback seen as weak leadership — **TRUE**. (b) high uncertainty avoidance = LOW resistance to change — **FALSE** (high UA = HIGH resistance). (c) masculine societies are more accustomed to building consensus — **FALSE** (that's feminine societies). (d) Hofstede's 5th dimension is 'ethnocentrism' — **FALSE** (it's individualism vs collectivism).

---

## 3.1 Cultural Intelligence (CQ)

**Cultural intelligence (CQ)** — the ability to understand and adjust to other cultures; it complements IQ and EQ. Four components: drive (motivation), knowledge (understanding), strategy (planning), and action (behavioral adaptation).

CQ develops in three phases:
1. **Awareness** — recognising that people from other countries often have a different mindset.
2. **Knowledge** — the ability to identify specific cultural differences.
3. **Adaptation** — the ability to adjust one's behaviour to those differences.

**Culture** — the collective programming of the mind that distinguishes members of one group from another; largely intangible. Its key elements include cultural norms, religion and language.

---

## 3.2 Hofstede's Five Dimensions of National Culture

| Dimension | What it measures | Low end | High end | Belgium / Netherlands | Business Implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Power distance | Acceptance of unequal power distribution | Flat hierarchies, participative (Denmark 18, NL 38) | Steep hierarchies, autocratic (Malaysia 104) | Belgium 65 / NL 38 | High PD: feedback from subordinates = sign of weak leadership; decisions made at top |
| Individualism–collectivism | Personal vs. group identity | Group loyalty, harmony, relationships first (China 20) | Individual achievement, personal freedom (USA 91, NL 80) | Belgium 75 / NL 80 | Group rewards (collectivist) vs individual bonuses (individualist) |
| Masculinity–femininity | Competition vs. compassion | Care, quality of life, consensus, gender equality (NL 14, Sweden 5) | Competition, achievement, assertiveness (Japan 95, USA 62) | Belgium 54 / NL 14 | Feminine cultures build consensus; masculine cultures are more confrontational |
| Uncertainty avoidance | Tolerance for ambiguity | Comfort with ambiguity, risk-accepting (UK 35, Denmark 23) | Need for rules, structure, anxiety about unknown (Greece 112, Belgium 94) | Belgium 94 / NL 53 | High UA = resistance to change and need for structure, NOT simply 'risk-averse' |
| Long-term orientation | Time horizon of the society | Tradition, quick results, respect for precedent (Nigeria 13) | Persistence, thrift, adaptation, pragmatism (China 87, Japan 88) | Belgium 82 / NL 67 (approx.) | Long-term: patient investment, relationship building; short-term: quarterly results |

**Belgium vs Netherlands — A Cultural Comparison:**

| Dimension | Belgium | Netherlands | Business Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Power Distance | 65 (high) | 38 (moderate) | Belgian firms more hierarchical; Dutch firms more egalitarian |
| Individualism | 75 | 80 | Both individualist, NL slightly more so |
| Masculinity | 54 | 14 (very feminine) | Major difference: NL emphasises consensus and work-life balance |
| Uncertainty Avoidance | 94 (very high) | 53 (moderate) | Belgian firms more rule-bound and procedure-heavy |
| Long-Term Orientation | 82 | 67 | Both relatively long-term, Belgium more so |

**Limitations of Hofstede:** (1) static view — data from 1960s–70s, culture changes; (2) national-level generalisation — ignores within-country variation (Flanders vs Wallonia); (3) Western/IBM bias; (4) only five dimensions may be insufficient; (5) ecological fallacy — national averages don't predict individual behaviour.

## 3.3 Meyer's Culture Map — Eight Dimensions

| Dimension | Low-context (explicit) | High-context (implicit) | Key insight |
|---|---|---|---|
| Communicating | Direct, "to the point" (US, NL, Germany) | Reads between the lines (Japan, China, Korea) | Dutch directness can offend Japanese colleagues |
| Evaluating | Direct negative feedback (Dutch, Germans) | Indirect feedback (Japanese, Thai) | "This is wrong" vs "perhaps we could consider another approach" |
| Persuading | Principles-first (France, Italy: theory → data → conclusion) | Applications-first (US, Canada: result → supporting data) | French presentation starts with theory; American with result |
| Leading | Egalitarian (Denmark: call boss by first name) | Hierarchical (Japan/Korea: strict formal titles) | Flat organisations (Nordics) vs steep hierarchies (Asia) |
| Deciding | Consensual (Japan: ringi — group consensus, strong commitment) | Top-down (US: boss decides quickly) | German paradox: egalitarian leading but consensual deciding |
| Trusting | Task-based (US/NL: trust through competence and reliability) | Relationship-based (China/Brazil: trust through personal bonds — *guanxi*) | Doing business in China requires personal relationships before any deal |
| Disagreeing | Confrontational (France/Israel debate openly) | Avoids confrontation (Japan/Thailand) | French meeting: heated debate is constructive; Japanese: silence can mean disagreement |
| Scheduling | Linear time (Germany/Switzerland: punctuality is respect) | Flexible time (Nigeria/India: time is fluid) | A 15-minute delay means nothing in Lagos but is insulting in Zurich |

**High-context culture examples:** Chinese, Korean, Japanese, Arab. **Low-context culture examples:** German, Scandinavian, American/British, Canadian.

---

## 3.4 Gelfand's Cultural Tightness–Looseness

**Cultural tightness–looseness (Gelfand)** — the strength of social norms and the severity of sanctioning. **Tight cultures** have strong social norms and low tolerance for deviance; **loose cultures** are more permissive and tolerant of unconventional behaviour.

- *Tight examples:* Japan, Singapore, Pakistan, Malaysia, Germany
- *Loose examples:* Netherlands, Brazil, New Zealand, Australia

Tight cultures often emerged under historical threats (disease, invasion, resource scarcity, population density). **Business implications:** tight cultures demand greater conformity, have stricter codes of conduct, and may resist foreign business practices; MNEs entering tight cultures must adapt more carefully to local norms.

---

## 3.5 Religion, Language & Cultural Clusters

**Religion** shapes values, work ethic, and business norms. Key examples:
- Christianity (Protestant work ethic — Weber)
- Islam (prohibitions on *riba*/interest; halal requirements)
- Hinduism (caste system influences)
- Buddhism (collectivism, modesty)
- Confucianism (hierarchy, education, long-term relationships)

**Language** affects communication costs and is a major component of psychic distance. English as lingua franca reduces but does not eliminate language barriers.

# 4. Identifying & Assessing Firm Resources
*Syllabus topic: Identifying and assessing firm resources · Textbook Chapter 4*

> **🟪 MOCK EXAM SIGNAL — Sels MC Q3 (both exams)**
> Tangible vs intangible resources and VRIO tested in both mock exams. Key rule: patents, brands, copyrights, goodwill = **INTANGIBLE**. Plants, equipment, sales outlets = **TANGIBLE**. Human resources are their own category (not tangible). The O in VRIO = organisational alignment (policies, procedures, incentives) — tested in Slangen sample Q4.

> **🟪 MOCK EXAM SIGNAL — Sels MC Q2 (captive offshoring)**
> Captive offshoring = activity takes place abroad AND is an internal (in-house) mode. Tested directly in Sels MC Q2. Answer (c) in the mock: captive offshoring = internal mode + foreign location.

---

## 4.1 Resources vs. Capabilities

**Resources** — the assets a firm owns or controls (tangible, intangible, human).

**Capabilities** — a firm's ability to deploy and mobilise resources effectively to achieve desired outcomes; they embody how resources are used. Often more important than individual resources because they are harder to imitate (social complexity, path dependence).

| Resource type | Sub-categories | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Tangible** | Financial, physical, natural | ExxonMobil cash reserves; SAP data centres; mineral deposits; plants, equipment, real estate, raw materials |
| **Intangible** | Technological, brand equity, organisational knowledge | Nokia's early patents; Coca-Cola brand; Apple design patents; trade secrets, corporate goodwill, trust networks |
| **Human** | Skills, motivation & culture, organisational practices | Specialist engineers; Google campus culture; training systems; CEO expertise; tacit know-how |

*Key exam distinction:* Patents, brands, copyrights, goodwill = INTANGIBLE. Equipment, cash, plants = TANGIBLE. Human resources are their own category.

---

## 4.2 The VRIO Framework

VRIO assesses whether a resource can be a source of sustained competitive advantage:
- **V — Valuable:** enables the firm to exploit opportunities or neutralise threats.
- **R — Rare:** not widely possessed by competitors.
- **I — Inimitable:** hard to copy due to unique history, social complexity or causal ambiguity.
- **O — Organised:** the firm has structures, processes and incentives to exploit the resource.

| Valuable? | Rare? | Inimitable? | Organised? | Competitive implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No | — | — | — | Competitive **disadvantage** |
| Yes | No | — | Yes | Competitive **parity** (commodity) |
| Yes | Yes | No | Yes | **Temporary** competitive advantage |
| Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | **Sustained** competitive advantage |

**Sources of inimitability:** (1) *causal ambiguity* — competitors cannot identify what causes the firm's success; (2) *social complexity* — advantage embedded in culture, trust, routines that emerge over time; (3) *path dependence* — historical unique conditions that cannot be recreated.

**The O criterion** — organisational alignment: policies, structures, incentive systems, culture that allow the firm to exploit its V+R+I resources. A firm with perfect resources but wrong structure wastes its advantage. The organisational structure IS the O in VRIO — linking directly to Chapter 15 (Section 7).

*Worked example:* The Coca-Cola recipe is valuable, rare, inimitable and organised → sustained advantage. A generic soft-drink formula is valuable but not rare or inimitable → mere commodity.

---

## 4.3 Value Chain Analysis (Porter)

The value chain maps where value is created and where outsourcing may help.

| Activity Type | Activities | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Primary — Upstream | Inbound logistics, Operations | Receiving raw materials, Manufacturing/assembly |
| Primary — Downstream | Outbound logistics, Marketing & Sales, Service | Distribution, Advertising, After-sales support |
| Support | Firm infrastructure, HR, Technology development, Procurement | Management, Training, R&D, Purchasing |

Value chain analysis forces managers to think about firm resources at a micro, activity-based level — helps decide which activities to perform internally and which to outsource or offshore.

---

## 4.4 Outsourcing vs. Offshoring

**Know the difference (frequently tested)**

**Outsourcing** — hiring an external firm to perform an activity, regardless of geographic location (a question of *who* performs it: in-house vs. external).

**Offshoring** — locating an activity in a foreign country, whether internally or via an external provider (a question of *where* it is done).

**The 2×2 matrix (exam-critical):**

| | Domestic (Home) | Foreign (Abroad) |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal (In-house)** | In-house production | **Captive offshoring** (= FDI) |
| **External (Outsource)** | Domestic outsourcing | Offshore outsourcing |

*Captive offshoring = internal mode + foreign location. This is the answer to Sels MC Q2.*

**Reshoring** — bringing previously offshored activities back to the home country, driven by: supply chain disruption risks (Covid-19), rising labour costs in emerging markets, automation reducing the labour cost advantage, quality control issues, IP concerns, political pressure.

---

## 4.5 Benchmarking

Benchmarking compares a firm's resources and capabilities against rivals to find gaps.

The four-step process:
1. Identify competitors.
2. Select relevant resources and capabilities.
3. Assess importance and performance on a 1–5 scale.
4. Visualise importance against performance (radar/spider chart) to spot strengths and weaknesses.

*Illustrated by SAP vs. Oracle in ERP.* Note: benchmarking is a useful analytical tool — an inferior score suggests an issue may need to be addressed, but it does not imply that the competitor's structure and processes should always be imitated.

**Benchmarking exam format:** Pick 2 competing firms. List 4–6 key resources/capabilities. Score each on a 1–5 scale for both firms. Plot on a radar chart. Then apply VRIO to each resource: Does Firm A's brand meet V? R? I? O?

# 5. Entry Modes & Theories of Internationalization
*Syllabus topic: FDI and non-equity entry modes; theories of international expansion · Textbook Chapters 6 & 11*

> **🟪 MOCK EXAM SIGNAL — Slangen Q5 (FDI types)**
> Horizontal vs vertical FDI: Toyota assembly plant in Belgium = horizontal FDI (same activity as home). Toyota R&D center in Belgium = **upstream** vertical FDI (NOT downstream). Downstream = distribution, retail, after-sales service.

> **🟪 MOCK EXAM SIGNAL — Slangen open Q1 (OLI paradigm)**
> OLI is THE central theory of Ch6. The open question asked: explain FDI vs FPI, L-advantages, OLI vs Uppsala vs network model. Structure answer as O → L → I, explain each, then show how all three together = FDI.

> **🟪 MOCK EXAM SIGNAL — Slangen Q7 (licensing vs franchising) / Q6 (OEM)**
> Licensing = specific IP rights (NOT the entire business concept). Franchising = entire business concept. Statement "licensor gives licensee the right to use the entire business concept" is FALSE. OEM = makes parts/products for others based on their designs — does NOT refer to a firm that designs and manufactures its own products.

> **🟪 MOCK EXAM SIGNAL — Slangen Q8 / open Q1c (Uppsala model)**
> Uppsala = dynamic process of LEARNING *DURING* internationalization, NOT pre-entry market research. Statement (b) correct: internationalization is a dynamic process where firms take decisions based on earlier experiences. Statement (c) FALSE: Uppsala does NOT say firms must learn about a market BEFORE entering it.

---

## 5.1 Defining FDI

**Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)** — an investment aimed at controlling and managing value-adding activities in another country. The control threshold is ownership of at least **10%** of the foreign entity's voting securities.

**Portfolio investment** — ownership below the 10% threshold; the goal is profit from price changes, not managerial control (e.g., buying a single share). *No managerial control.*

---

## 5.2 Types of FDI

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Horizontal FDI** | The same value-chain activity as at home is performed abroad | Toyota car assembly in Belgium; Tesla building assembly plants in China and Germany |
| **Upstream Vertical FDI** | Earlier-stage activity abroad (inputs, R&D, raw materials) | Toyota R&D center in Belgium; mining operations in Australia |
| **Downstream Vertical FDI** | Later-stage activity abroad (distribution, retail, after-sales) | Toyota dealership network in Europe; Apple retail stores in China |
| **Greenfield FDI** | Establishing a new foreign subsidiary from scratch | A new Tesla plant in a foreign market |
| **Cross-border acquisition** | Buying an existing foreign firm | Foxconn's purchase of Sharp in Japan |

---

## 5.3 Non-Equity (Contractual) Entry Modes

**Exporting** — the direct sale of goods or services to foreign markets. Services can be exported four ways: remote delivery, on-site provision, cross-border consumption (the client travels in), and FDI-enabled local provision.

**Licensing** — the owner of an asset grants a foreign party the right to use **specific IP rights** (patent, technology) for royalties (e.g., ARM → chip designs; Coca-Cola → syrup recipe to bottlers). *Not the entire business concept.*

**Franchising** — granting a foreign entrepreneur the right to operate an **entire business concept** — brand, manuals, supply chain — for royalties (e.g., McDonald's, where over 90% of outlets are franchised).

**Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM)** — a company that produces parts and equipment that may be marketed by another manufacturer. An OEM executes design blueprints provided by other firms. It does NOT refer to a firm that both designs AND manufactures its own products.

**Export intermediaries:** a *sales agent* earns commission and carries no inventory risk; a *distributor* buys the product and resells it, bearing inventory risk.

| Mode | Definition | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct exporting | Firm sells directly to foreign buyer | Full control, market learning, higher margins | Requires market knowledge, logistics, after-sales |
| Indirect exporting | Uses intermediary (agent, trading house) | Low risk, minimal commitment, fast | Low learning, loss of control, agent priorities |
| Licensing | Grant specific IP rights for royalties | Low capital, rapid expansion | Dissemination risk, limited control, licensee may become competitor |
| Franchising | Grant entire business concept | Scalable, local operator bears most costs | Quality control, cultural adaptation, franchisee opportunism |
| Turnkey projects | Design + build entire operation, hand over 'key' | Project-based revenue, technology transfer | No ongoing presence, competitor may learn |
| Management contracts | Provide management expertise to foreign-owned operation | Low risk, recurring fee income (hotels, utilities) | Limited equity upside, reputational risk |
| Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) | Build facility, operate for period, then transfer to host | Long-term revenue stream | Political risk during long operating period |

---

## 5.4 The OLI Paradigm (Dunning's Eclectic Paradigm)

FDI is the optimal mode only when a firm simultaneously possesses all three advantages:

| Advantage | Full Name | Definition | Examples | Without It… |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **O** | Ownership Advantage | Firm-specific resources/capabilities giving competitive edge over local firms | Technology, brand, management expertise, patents, proprietary processes | No reason to go abroad — local firms would outcompete the foreign entrant |
| **L** | Location Advantage | Benefits of a specific foreign location over alternatives (including home country) | Market size, natural resources, cheap labour, clusters, institutions, infrastructure, talent pools | No reason to locate abroad — serve foreign markets via exports from home |
| **I** | Internalization Advantage | Benefits of keeping activities in-house rather than contracting out to foreign partners | Protect IP, avoid dissemination risk, reduce transaction costs, maintain quality control | License or franchise instead of FDI — use contractual mode |

**OLI Decision Logic:**

| Advantages Present | Optimal Mode | Logic |
|---|---|---|
| O + L + I | **FDI** (establish foreign subsidiary) | Firm has advantage, location is attractive, better to keep in-house |
| O + I (no L) | Domestic production + Export | Firm has advantage, prefers internal control, but no reason to produce abroad |
| O + L (no I) | **Licensing / Franchising** | Firm has advantage, location is attractive, but can use external partner safely |
| O only | Domestic production for domestic market | Advantage exists but no foreign location benefit and no internalization need |

*OLI vs Uppsala:* OLI = **static** cross-sectional decision framework (one entry decision). Uppsala = **dynamic** longitudinal process of gradual learning and increasing commitment over time.

---

## 5.5 Internationalization Process Models

| Model | Core Mechanism | Key Concept | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Uppsala Model** | Experiential learning → knowledge → commitment cycle. Start with psychically close markets → gradually increase commitment. | *Psychic distance:* perceived differences in language, culture, institutions | Cannot explain born globals, radical leaps, or digital-native firms |
| **Stages Models** | Firms follow predictable stages: no export → indirect export → direct export → sales subsidiary → production subsidiary | Linear, deterministic progression | Too rigid; ignores firm-specific factors; firms skip stages |
| **Network Model** | Relationships and network position drive internationalization. Three phases: extension → penetration → integration of business networks. | *Liability of outsidership:* being outside relevant networks is the key barrier | Assumes established networks; less relevant for startups |
| **Born Globals** | Firms internationalize rapidly from inception, skipping stages. | Founder experience, niche tech, digital platforms, small domestic market | Challenge all sequential models; high risk; resource-intensive |

**Uppsala vs Network:** Uppsala = firm-level experiential learning drives internationalization. Network = inter-firm relationships and network position drive market entry. Uppsala is firm-centric; Network is relationship-centric.

**Uppsala revised (2009):** Added network position — firms move from outsidership to insidership in foreign business networks. Bridges the gap between original Uppsala and the network model.

---

## 5.6 Internationalization Process & Risk

**Spill-over** — the flow of knowledge, technology or skills between a host-country environment and a foreign investor — learning runs both ways.

**Demand uncertainty** — the inability to reliably forecast market size, consumer preferences or macro-economic conditions abroad; high uncertainty favours low-commitment modes such as exporting.

**Expropriation** — the seizure or nationalization of foreign-owned assets by a host government, often without adequate compensation (e.g., Carlsberg losing its Russian breweries).

| Risk level | Recommended mode | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Low (stable legal environment) | FDI | Benefits of local presence outweigh minimal risk |
| Medium (some political tension) | Licensing / franchising | Limits asset exposure while leveraging the brand |
| High (risk of confiscation) | Exporting | Avoids sunk investment, preserves flexibility |

# 6. Strategic Decisions on FDI
*Syllabus topic: Strategic decisions on FDI · Textbook Chapter 12*

---

## 6.1 Four Strategic Objectives of FDI

| Objective | Goal | Typical driver / note |
|---|---|---|
| **Access to new markets** (market-seeking) | Serve new customers or follow existing clients abroad | The most common driver worldwide; diversifies revenue, improves customer proximity |
| **Access to natural resources** (resource-seeking) | Secure raw materials (oil, minerals) | Common in oil & gas and mining |
| **Enhancing firm efficiency** (efficiency-seeking) | Cut costs | Low-cost production, assembly near markets, tariff circumvention |
| **Acquiring capabilities / strategic assets** (capability-seeking) | Gain technology, knowledge, patents, brands, R&D | Capabilities = skills/knowledge; strategic assets = patents, brands, labs |

---

## 6.2 Selecting the Host Country

**Step 1** — Identify attractive locations by matching country characteristics to each objective (high demand for market entry; low cost and good infrastructure for efficiency; clusters/global cities for capability acquisition). Output: shortlist of 5–10 countries.

**Step 2** — Evaluate location disadvantages and weigh them against the advantages:

| Disadvantage | Impact on FDI |
|---|---|
| Cultural distance | Management and HR challenges |
| Legal & regulatory differences | Higher compliance costs |
| Geographic remoteness | Higher logistics expenses |
| Poor infrastructure | Limits operational efficiency |

**Global cities** — cities with high interconnectedness, cosmopolitanism, and many advanced producer services (consulting, finance, legal). Top examples: London, New York, Singapore, Hong Kong, Tokyo, Shanghai, Dubai, Paris, Sydney, Seoul. Especially valuable for capability-seeking FDI.

*Illustration:* For a US fast-food franchise, Canada beats larger markets such as Japan or Germany once disadvantages are added in — minimal cultural, legal and geographic distance can outweigh pure market size.

---

## 6.3 Timing — First-Mover vs. Late-Mover

| | First-mover advantages | First-mover disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| | Pre-empt scarce resources (suppliers, skilled labour) | High uncertainty about market potential |
| | Create entry barriers for later entrants | Pioneering costs (educating customers, building infrastructure) |
| | Build early relationships with governments/partners | Risk of choosing wrong market or timing |
| | Leverage technological leadership; customer lock-in | Competitors learn from your mistakes free of cost |

| | Late-mover advantages | Late-mover disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| | Reduced investment risk as uncertainty falls | Best resources and locations may be taken |
| | Lower marketing costs (pioneers bear promotion) | Brand loyalty already established for first-movers |
| | Avoid obsolete assets — adopt newer technology | Catch-up costs can be substantial |
| | Learn from pioneers' mistakes | Market may be saturated |

*Tesla as first mover:* bore high R&D and infrastructure risk; established automakers entered EV market later, after much of the uncertainty had receded.

**Emerging market MNEs (EMNEs)** are typically late movers that internationalize aggressively to catch up. The *'springboard' strategy:* EMNEs use international expansion to compensate for competitive disadvantages at home (e.g., Tata acquiring Jaguar Land Rover; Lenovo acquiring IBM's PC division; Geely acquiring Volvo).

---

## 6.4 Entry Mode — The 2×2 Matrix

| | Low / shared control | High control |
|---|---|---|
| **Greenfield** | Joint venture (new subsidiary with partners) | Wholly-owned greenfield (sole owner) |
| **Acquisition** | Partial acquisition (buy a stake) | Full acquisition (buy the entire firm) |

**Greenfield vs. Acquisition — key trade-offs:**

| Criterion | Greenfield (new plant) | Acquisition (buy existing firm) |
|---|---|---|
| Speed to market | Longer (construction, permits) | Immediate (operational business) |
| Control over assets | Built from scratch → full design freedom | Existing assets may limit flexibility |
| Cost structure | Higher upfront capital | Lower immediate cost but includes a premium |
| Risk | Construction and regulatory risk | Integration and cultural risk |
| Strategic fit | Best when unique processes/technology are needed | Best when brand, market presence or supply chains already exist |

**When acquisition speed isn't quick:** Legislative barriers (China restricts full foreign ownership, forcing JVs); national-security reviews (CFIUS in the US — e.g., Nippon Steel's protracted US deal); cultural & integration issues.

**WOG vs FA Decision Factors:**

| Factor | Favours WOG (Greenfield) | Favours FA (Acquisition) |
|---|---|---|
| Need for speed | Speed not critical; can build gradually | Speed is essential — need immediate market presence |
| Complementary local resources | Not needed; firm can replicate own capabilities | Critical — need local brand, networks, permits, workforce |
| IP leakage risk | High IP risk → keep in-house | Low IP risk |
| Industry concentration | Low concentration — adding capacity won't trigger price war | High concentration — greenfield adds supply, may provoke large incumbents |
| Institutional barriers | Foreign acquisition banned; greenfield incentivized | Institutional barriers to greenfield (permits, land); targets available |
| Target availability | No suitable acquisition targets | Quality targets exist with strategic fit and reasonable price |

**JV vs WOG Decision Factors:**

| Factor | Favours JV | Favours WOG |
|---|---|---|
| Complementary local resources | High need for partner's local knowledge, networks | Firm has sufficient local knowledge from prior experience |
| Dissemination risk | Low — partner unlikely to misuse shared knowledge | High — serious IP leakage risk; core technology at stake |
| Coordination with other subsidiaries | Low — JV can operate autonomously | High — need tight coordination with HQ and other units |
| Risk of expropriation | High — local partner provides political cover | Low — stable institutions; expropriation unlikely |
| Legal restrictions on foreign ownership | Ownership restrictions require a local partner (e.g., China historically) | No restrictions; full ownership permitted |
| Financial resources | Limited — shared investment reduces capital requirements | Sufficient for full ownership |

**Acquisition types:**

| Type | Definition | When Used |
|---|---|---|
| Conventional acquisition | Standard purchase of target firm (full or majority stake) | When target is well-functioning |
| Brownfield acquisition | Acquire then completely restructure — effectively a greenfield in disguise | When target has valuable location/permits but operations need complete overhaul |
| Multiple acquisitions | Serial buying strategy — acquire several firms over time | When building market position gradually |
| Staged acquisition | Gradual increase of ownership from partial to majority to full | When want to learn about target before full commitment; reduce risk progressively |

## 6.5 ⬛ INSERT: The Obsolescing Bargain Model

> *Content from Exam Handbook Ch.2 & Ch.12 — not in Study Guide. Listed explicitly in Exam Focus Ch.2 and appears in Repsol case analysis.*

**The Obsolescing Bargain** — explains how bargaining power between an MNE and a host government shifts over time.

- **Before entry:** The MNE has leverage — it holds mobile capital, technology, and can choose among multiple host countries. The host government offers incentives (tax breaks, SEZs) to attract the FDI.
- **After entry:** Once the MNE has committed (sunk costs — plants, equipment, trained workforce), bargaining power shifts to the government. The MNE's investment is now immobile. The government can demand more taxes, impose local content requirements, change regulations, or in extreme cases expropriate assets.
- **The bargain 'obsolesce'** — what was agreed at entry becomes obsolete as the host government realises its leverage.

*Application — Repsol/YPF:* Repsol sunk billions into Argentine operations. After the 2001 economic crisis, Argentina's government seized 51% of YPF in 2012, citing underinvestment. The obsolescing bargain: Repsol's sunk costs had eliminated its exit option. *Lesson: MNEs must assess political risk dynamically, not just at entry. Build local alliances and diversify across jurisdictions.*

*Application — Carlsberg/Russia:* Following the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, Western sanctions forced Carlsberg to divest its Russian subsidiary (Baltika). Russia seized Carlsberg's assets — a modern form of expropriation showing that political risk extends beyond traditional emerging markets.

# 7. Corporate Strategies & Structures for MNEs
*Syllabus topic: Corporate strategies and structures for MNEs · Textbook Chapter 15*

> **🟪 MOCK EXAM SIGNAL — Slangen Q10**
> Transnational strategy = highest knowledge exchange among subsidiaries. Tested directly. Correct answer: (a) transnational strategy. Home replication and GSS have one-way flows; localization has minimal flows. HIGH priority exam topic: know all 4 strategies, their I-R position, matching structure, knowledge flow, and examples.

---

## 7.1 The Integration–Responsiveness (I-R) Framework

**Global integration** — strategies that aggregate operations across countries to capture synergies such as economies of scale. Upstream activities (R&D, sourcing, production) are easier to integrate than downstream ones.

**Local responsiveness** — strategies that adapt products and activities to each host country's conditions — driven by regulatory differences and customer preferences. Downstream activities (marketing, sales) need close local interaction.

Managers assess both pressures (high/low) to select one of four strategies.

---

## 7.2 The Four International Strategies

| Strategy | Integration | Responsiveness | Structure | Knowledge Flow | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Home replication** | Low | Low | International division | One-way: HQ → subsidiaries | IKEA (early), Walmart (early) |
| **Localization (multidomestic)** | Low | High | Geographic area | Minimal — each subsidiary autonomous | Construction firms; local beer industry; AB InBev/Heineken local brands |
| **Global standard (GSS)** | High | Low | Global product division | One-way: HQ → subsidiaries (centralized) | Airbus, ExxonMobil, Apple, Intel |
| **Transnational** | High | High | Global matrix / Multiplex | **Multi-directional: HQ ↔ subs ↔ subs** | Unilever, Bosch, P&G, ABB |

**Strategy details:**
- *Home replication:* copies the domestic model into each market with no adaptation and no cross-subsidiary coordination. Risk: assumes uniform consumer needs (cf. Walmart's withdrawal from Germany).
- *Localization:* duplicates the full value chain but adapts each autonomous subsidiary to its host country. Strong local fit, but limited economies of scale and no knowledge sharing.
- *Global Standards Strategy (GSS):* extensive aggregation into a few specialized mega-units (centres of excellence) producing a standardized product; downstream subsidiaries act mainly as sales pipelines. Risk: supply chain concentration (one factory serving the world).
- *Transnational:* seeks economies of scale AND local adaptation simultaneously — centralize upstream, localize downstream — via a complex matrix with dual (product and geographic) lines of authority. Stimulates knowledge exchange between ALL units (*glocalization*).

---

## 7.3 Organizational Structures

| Structure | Design | Matches Strategy | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| International Division | Separate division handles all foreign operations | Home Replication | Simple; clear separation of domestic/foreign | Foreign ops = secondary; limited local autonomy; siloed |
| Geographic Area | Organized by region/country; each area semi-autonomous | Localization | Deep local knowledge; responsive to local needs | Duplication; poor knowledge sharing; no global scale |
| Global Product Division | Organized by product line; each with global mandate | Global Standards | Scale economies; clear product focus; efficient | Ignores geography; supply chain concentration risk |
| Global Matrix | Dual reporting: geographic AND product dimensions | Transnational | Balances local + global; facilitates knowledge flow | Complex, slow decisions, political conflict, expensive |
| Multiplex (EY model) | Flexible, project-based structure beyond traditional matrix | Transnational (evolved) | Adaptive; suits professional services; leverages talent globally | Requires sophisticated management, IT, and culture |

*Chandler's principle:* **"Structure follows strategy"** — the right organizational structure must match the chosen international strategy.

---

## 7.4 The AAA Typology (Global Strategy Levers)

| Strategy | Primary goal | Mechanism | Trade-offs | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Aggregation** | Cost reduction through uniformity / centralization | Standardize products, centralize operations, create global platforms | Reduces local fit; may miss local preferences | ExxonMobil, Airbus, Apple, IKEA |
| **Adaptation** | Local responsiveness to tastes and regulations | Modify products, marketing, operations for each market | Higher costs; reduced scale economies | Unilever (local product variants), McDonald's (local menus) |
| **Arbitrage** | Exploit geographic differences in resources, cost or tax | Source where cheap, sell where expensive; labour, tax, regulatory arbitrage | Vulnerable to convergence; political backlash | Infosys (Indian IT labour), Zara (low-cost production), Shell (HQ location) |

Firms can pursue multiple A's simultaneously, though tensions exist. Apple combines aggregation (standardized global products) with arbitrage (manufacturing in China for cost). Unilever combines adaptation (local formulations) with aggregation (shared R&D and brand architecture).

|---|---|---|
| Home replication | HQ → subsidiaries (top-down) | Moderate |
| Localization | Minimal cross-subsidiary exchange | Low |
| Global standard | HQ ↔ centres of excellence | Moderate |

## 7.6 ⬛ INSERT: Knowledge Types, Nonaka's SECI Cycle & Knowledge Governance

> *Content from Exam Handbook Ch.15 — not in Study Guide. Appears in Exam Focus for Ch.15.*

### Knowledge Types (Nonaka)

| Knowledge Type | Individual | Organizational |
|---|---|---|
| **Explicit** (codifiable, transferable) | Skills learned from reading manuals, formal education | Databases, SOPs, patents, documented processes, codified best practices |
| **Tacit** (hard to codify, experience-based) | Personal experience, intuition, 'gut feel', craftsmanship | Organizational culture, embedded routines, collective know-how, 'the way we do things' |

### Nonaka's SECI Knowledge Creation Cycle

| Stage | Process | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **S — Socialization** | Tacit → Tacit | Learning by observation, apprenticeship, working together — tacit knowledge shared directly without articulation |
| **E — Externalization** | Tacit → Explicit | Articulating experience into concepts, documents, frameworks, or manuals |
| **C — Combination** | Explicit → Explicit | Combining databases, reports, systems — reconfiguring existing explicit knowledge into new forms |
| **I — Internalization** | Explicit → Tacit | Learning by doing — practicing documented procedures until they become second nature |

This cycle creates new organizational knowledge continuously.

### Reverse Knowledge Transfer & Boundary Spanners

**Reverse knowledge transfer** — subsidiaries send knowledge back to HQ. Critical for organizational learning: local innovations can become global best practices. *Example:* Unilever's Indian subsidiary developed single-use shampoo sachets (affordable for BoP consumers), later adopted globally.

**Boundary spanners** — individuals who bridge organizational boundaries (expatriates, bicultural managers, people with experience in multiple units). Critical for knowledge transfer because they understand both sending and receiving contexts.

### Communities of Practice (CoPs)

**Communities of Practice (CoPs)** — informal groups of professionals who share knowledge, solve problems, and develop competencies around common interests — across organizational and geographic boundaries. CoPs can be physical or virtual. They facilitate tacit knowledge sharing that formal systems cannot capture.

*Challenges:* maintaining engagement over time, crossing cultural boundaries, balancing local and global knowledge, avoiding 'not-invented-here' (NIH) syndrome.

### Knowledge Governance Challenges

| Challenge | Problem | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge hoarding | Subsidiaries withhold knowledge to protect their power or bargaining position vis-à-vis HQ | Incentive alignment (reward sharing), CoPs, boundary spanners, rotation programs |
| Absorptive capacity | Receiving unit lacks the ability or background to assimilate new knowledge | Invest in recipient's education/training; HRM practices; send experienced people |
| Social capital deficit | Lack of trust, personal relationships, and shared norms between units | Cross-unit teams, expatriate assignments, shared projects, social events |
| NIH syndrome | Not-invented-here: rejection of externally developed knowledge because it wasn't created locally | Joint development projects, personnel rotation, showcase successful transfers |

**Social capital** has three dimensions: *structural* (network connections — who knows whom), *relational* (trust and norms), and *cognitive* (shared language, mental models, and values). All three are needed for effective knowledge transfer.

**Absorptive capacity (Cohen & Levinthal)** — the ability to recognize the value of new external knowledge, assimilate it, and apply it commercially. Higher absorptive capacity = better knowledge transfer.

## 7.7 Subsidiary Roles

**Worldwide mandate** — a subsidiary given responsibility for a product line, function, or technology with global or regional scope. Requires: strong local capabilities, entrepreneurial subsidiary management, HQ trust.

**Subsidiary initiative** — local managers identify and pursue opportunities beyond their formally assigned role. Initiatives bubble up from subsidiaries rather than being directed by HQ. Can lead to worldwide mandates if successful.

## 8.1 Competitive Dynamics & Collusion

**Competitive dynamics** — how a firm's strategic choices (price, capacity, innovation) interact with rivals' moves — often analysed with game theory.

**Collusion** — coordinated behaviour among firms to influence market outcomes such as price, output or market shares.

| Type | Definition | Legal status |
|---|---|---|
| **Tacit collusion** | Indirect coordination through signalling (public statements, price trends); no explicit agreement | Generally not prohibited; legal grey zone — very hard to prove |
| **Explicit collusion (cartel)** | Direct negotiation of prices, output or market division; formal organization fixing prices | **Illegal** under EU Art. 101 TFEU; heavy fines and sanctions |
| **Predatory pricing** | Pricing below cost to eliminate competitors, then raising prices | Illegal (abuse of dominance, Art. 102 TFEU); a form of dumping when cross-border |
| **Dumping** | Selling abroad below production cost or below home-market price | Countered by anti-dumping duties (WTO-compliant) |

*OPEC* is the classic cartel. *The vitamin cartel* (Roche, BASF, Aventis): set prices in joint meetings, fined, with whistle-blowers receiving reduced penalties.

---

## 8.2 Five Conditions That Favour Collusion

1. **Homogeneous products** — little differentiation, so price is the main lever.
2. **Few firms (oligopoly)** — easier to monitor and coordinate.
3. **A price leader** — a dominant firm sets a benchmark others follow.
4. **High entry barriers** — capital-intensive industries deter newcomers.
5. **High market commonality** — firms meet in many markets, enabling mutual forbearance (tacit collusion sustained across multiple markets).

---

## 8.3 Game Theory & the AMC Framework

**Prisoner's dilemma** — explains why cartels are inherently unstable: each firm is tempted to secretly cut prices (cheat). In repeated interactions ('shadow of the future'), firms can sustain the high (collusive) price because cheating today triggers retaliation tomorrow.

**Signalling** — firms signal competitive intentions through observable actions: entering a rival's key market (mutual forbearance signal); price-match guarantees; announcing capacity expansions to deter entry; filing anti-dumping complaints as a signal ('it's time to talk').

**AMC Framework** — a test of whether a competitor will strike back after a move:
- **A — Awareness:** does the rival know of the move?
- **M — Motivation:** does it have incentive to retaliate (does the action threaten its core market)?
- **C — Capacity:** does it have the resources to respond?

If any element is missing, no competitive response occurs.

**Competitive strategies:**

| Strategy | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Blue-ocean strategy | Create uncontested market space rather than fighting in a crowded "red ocean" (Kim & Mauborgne) | Cirque du Soleil combining circus and theatre; Nintendo Wii targeting casual gamers |
| Defender strategy | Protect home market from foreign entrants using local advantages | Local beer brands defending against global brewers using cultural identity |
| Extender strategy | Aggressively expand abroad leveraging home capabilities | Zara extending fast fashion model globally from Spain |
| Co-opetition | Simultaneous cooperation and competition between firms | Samsung supplying screens to Apple while competing in smartphones |

---

## 8.4 Measuring Concentration — The HHI

**Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)** — HHI = Σ(s²) where s = each firm's market share as a percentage. Comprehensive concentration measure (C4/C8 ratios give quicker snapshots).

*Examples:*
- Perfect monopoly: HHI = 10,000 (= 100²)
- Two firms at 50%: HHI = 50² + 50² = 5,000
- Four firms at 40%, 30%, 20%, 10%: HHI = 1,600 + 900 + 400 + 100 = 3,000

**HHI thresholds (used by DG Competition — also expressed as decimals in some texts):**

| HHI (using % shares) | HHI (decimal equivalent) | Interpretation | Merger implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| < 1,000 | < 0.01 | Highly competitive | Merger normally cleared unconditionally |
| 1,000 – 2,500 | 0.01 – 0.25 | Moderately concentrated | Scrutinised if **delta HHI > 250** |
| > 2,500 | > 0.25 | Highly concentrated | Scrutinised if **delta HHI > 150**; mergers usually blocked unless strong remedies offered |

*For mergers:* calculate pre-merger HHI, post-merger HHI, and **delta HHI** (the change). The delta matters as much as the absolute level.

---

## 8.5 EU Competition Policy — Three Pillars

| Pillar | Legal Basis | What It Targets |
|---|---|---|
| Anti-trust / Cartels | **Art. 101 TFEU** | Agreements between firms that restrict competition (price-fixing, market division, output limitation) |
| Abuse of Dominance | **Art. 102 TFEU** | Unilateral conduct by dominant firms that harms competition (predatory pricing, tying, refusal to deal) |
| Merger Control | **EU Merger Regulation (EUMR)** | Mergers and acquisitions that Significantly Impede Effective Competition (SIEC test) |

**SIEC test (Significant Impediment to Effective Competition)** — replaced the old 'dominance test' in 2004. A merger is blocked if it would significantly impede effective competition in the EU internal market.

**Merger control process:**

| Phase | Duration | What Happens | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Notification | Before completion | Mergers with EU dimension must be notified to DG Competition | Mandatory for above-threshold deals |
| Phase I | **25 working days** | Initial review: does the merger raise serious competition concerns? | Approve unconditionally OR refer to Phase II |
| Phase II | **90 working days** (extendable) | In-depth investigation with market testing, economic analysis | Approve, approve with remedies, or block |

*Most mergers (>90%) are cleared in Phase I. Blocking is rare but remedies (typically divestitures) are common.*

**Theories of Harm:**

| Theory | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Horizontal unilateral effects | Merger removes direct competitor → less competitive pressure → higher prices | Two airlines merging on overlapping routes |
| Horizontal coordinated effects | Fewer firms post-merger → easier to sustain tacit collusion | AB InBev/SABMiller: fewer large brewers = easier coordination |
| Vertical input foreclosure | Merged firm refuses to supply downstream rivals with essential inputs | Chipmaker merging with phone maker → rival phone makers lose chip supply |
| Vertical output foreclosure | Merged firm refuses to buy from upstream rivals | Retailer merging with supplier → rival suppliers lose distribution access |
| Conglomerate effects | Portfolio power: bundling products from different markets to foreclose competition | GE/Honeywell (blocked by EU) |

**Remedies:** *Structural* (divestiture — selling business units to restore competition) are preferred over *behavioral* (commitments to maintain market access) because structural remedies are self-enforcing and permanent.

---

## 8.6 Anti-Dumping

When foreign firms sell below cost or below home-market price in the EU, the Commission can impose **anti-dumping duties** (typically for 5 years, renewable). Process: EU producers file complaint → investigation → provisional measures → definitive duties.

*Example:* Chinese steel dumped in EU at below-cost prices, leading to tariffs of 20–70%.

*Countervailing duties* — offset foreign subsidies (distinct from anti-dumping duties).

---

## 8.7 Anchor Case — AB InBev & SABMiller

The world's no. 1 brewer (AB InBev) acquired no. 2 (SABMiller) in a ~€19bn deal. Overlapping shares created HHI values above 2,500 in several Central- and Eastern-European markets. **Coordinated effects concern:** fewer large players post-merger would make tacit collusion easier — conditions were favourable (homogeneous product, transparent pricing, multimarket contact, high barriers to entry). **Remedy:** AB InBev was required to divest SABMiller's entire stake in MillerCoors (USA) and divest the Peroni, Grolsch, and Meantime brands (Europe) — a structural remedy ensuring competitive overlap was eliminated rather than managed through behavioural commitments.

## 9.1 Mergers vs. Acquisitions

**Merger** — two roughly equal firms combine, creating a new legal entity while both parties cease to exist as separate entities (e.g., AB InBev + AmBev).

**Acquisition** — a dominant acquirer absorbs a target, which ceases to exist as a separate legal entity; the acquirer continues (e.g., DuPont → Danisco; Allianz → Tua Assicurazioni).

| Feature | Merger | Acquisition |
|---|---|---|
| Relative size | Roughly equal | Acquirer larger than target |
| Legal outcome | A **new entity** is formed | The **target is eliminated**; acquirer continues |
| Common term | "Statutory merger" | Often called a "takeover" |

Acquisition variants: partial vs. full; public vs. private target — including privatisation (a state-owned firm sold to private owners).

---

## 9.2 Three Motives Behind M&A

| Motive | Description | Exam Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Synergy (primary)** | Combined capabilities create value greater than the sum of parts — market power, cost savings, complementary resources, cross-selling | Cost savings (eliminate duplication), revenue enhancement, resource complementarity, market access |
| **Hubris** | Managers overestimate ability to create value; overpay for target | Winner's curse: bidding competition drives price above rational value; CEO overconfidence |
| **Managerial self-interest** | Managers pursue growth for personal benefit (compensation, prestige, empire) | Agency problem: managers' interests diverge from shareholders'; compensation tied to firm size |

**Acquisition premium** — amount paid above target's market value; average 40–50%. Higher premium = harder to generate enough value to justify the price.

---

## 9.3 The M&A Process & Causes of Failure

**M&A process:** (1) financial due diligence; (2) strategic-fit assessment; (3) organisational-fit assessment (cultural compatibility, retention); (4) post-acquisition integration.

**Common causes of failed acquisitions:**
- **Overvaluation** — paying excessive premiums (hubris/competition-driven).
- **Cultural clashes** — nationalistic or corporate-culture resistance; talent exodus. *Examples: DaimlerChrysler, AOL-Time Warner.*
- **Poor strategic fit** — acquiring for diversification without synergy logic.
- **Loss of key talent** — target's best employees leave post-acquisition.
- **Overestimated synergies** — revenue synergies are especially hard to achieve.
- **Integration mismanagement** — moving too fast or too slow.
- **Regulatory blocks** — antitrust authorities reject the deal over concentration concerns.

*The post-acquisition integration phase is where most value is created or destroyed.*

**M&A graveyard examples:** blocked Deutsche Börse – London Stock Exchange merger; Kraft Heinz/Unilever (withdrawn); Qualcomm/NXP Semiconductors (blocked by China); Monsanto's failed bid for Syngenta.

---

## 9.4 Horizontal vs. Vertical Mergers — EU Review

**Horizontal merger** — combines firms at the same production stage (direct competitors) — raises concentration concerns.

**Vertical merger** — combines firms at different stages (e.g., manufacturer buying a supplier) — raises foreclosure concerns.

| Horizontal merger test (three questions) | Vertical merger test (three questions) |
|---|---|
| 1. Will the merged firm attain a dominant market share? (Check post-merger HHI.) | 1. Will the merged firm control multiple production stages? |
| 2. Will consumers benefit from cost savings or faster innovation? | 2. Is there an economic incentive to foreclose rivals from essential inputs? |
| 3. Will removing competition enable price-raising behaviour? | 3. Would the merger significantly impede effective competition ("raising rivals' costs")? |

---

## 9.5 Strategic Alliances as an Alternative to M&A

**Strategic alliance** — a cooperative arrangement letting two or more firms share resources, technology or market access without creating a new legal entity.

An alliance is preferred to a full takeover when: a merger would be blocked by competition authorities; market-transaction costs are high (capabilities cannot simply be rented); or partners bring complementary technologies. Alliances can also serve as a transition phase.

| Alternative | Equity involved? | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic alliance | No | Collaboration without ownership (e.g., joint marketing, R&D sharing) |
| Joint venture | Yes | Co-ownership of a new entity for shared risk and reward |

*Example:* Star Alliance — KLM provides the Schiphol hub and partners contribute route networks and frequent-flyer programmes. A full takeover would have been barred by EU antitrust rules.

---

## 9.6 Anchor Case — Repsol & the YPF Expropriation (Argentina)

Repsol (Spain) held a **49% stake** in YPF; the Argentine government held 51%, giving it majority control and the ability to alter terms unilaterally — culminating in expropriation in 2012. Repsol recovered roughly 50% of its investment through ICSID arbitration in Geneva, but ICSID could not compel reversal.

**Consequences:** Argentina's business-friendliness ranking fell sharply (from 139 to 185 globally); energy trade deficit surged; many MNEs became reluctant to enter Argentina.

**Core lesson:** political risk can outweigh market opportunity, especially in strategic sectors such as energy. Robust investment-protection clauses (as in EU–Singapore or EU–Australia agreements) help mitigate such disputes. *This case also illustrates the Obsolescing Bargain model (see Section 6.5).*

## Practice MC Questions with Explanations

**Q1: MNE Definition (Ch1)**

Which of the following statements about multinational enterprises (MNEs) is **wrong**?
(a) MNEs are firms that have undertaken FDI.
(b) MNEs control and manage value-added activities in at least two countries.
(c) MNEs engage in international business.
(d) All internationally-active firms are MNEs.

> **Answer: (d).** Not all internationally-active firms are MNEs. A firm that only exports is doing IB but is not an MNE because it has not undertaken FDI. MNE status requires FDI — investment that involves managerial control of value-added activities in foreign countries. A firm that buys shares in a foreign company (FPI) is also not an MNE because portfolio investment lacks managerial control.

---

**Q2: Legal Systems (Ch2)**

Which statement about countries' legal systems is correct?
(a) Common law uses comprehensive statutes and codes.
(b) Civil law is shaped by precedents.
(c) Coordinated market economies tend to have civil law systems, whereas liberal market economies tend to have common law systems.
(d) Civil law systems are characterized by case law.

> **Answer: (c).** CME countries (Germany, Japan, France) use civil law (comprehensive written codes). LME countries (UK, USA, Australia) use common law (precedent-based, case law). Options (a) and (b) reverse the definitions. Option (d) is wrong because case law characterizes common law, not civil law.

---

**Q3: Hofstede (Ch3)**

Which statement about Hofstede's cultural dimensions is correct?
(a) Asking subordinates for feedback is often regarded as weak leadership in high power distance countries.
(b) Employees show low resistance to change in high uncertainty avoidance countries.
(c) Managers in masculine societies are more accustomed to building consensus.
(d) Hofstede identified five dimensions: power distance, ethnocentrism, masculinity, uncertainty avoidance, and long-term orientation.

> **Answer: (a).** In high power distance cultures, hierarchical authority is expected. A boss asking subordinates for feedback signals weakness. (b) is wrong: high UA = HIGH resistance to change. (c) is wrong: feminine (not masculine) societies build consensus. (d) is wrong: the dimension is individualism, not ethnocentrism.

---

**Q4: VRIO (Ch4)**

Which statement about firm resources is correct?
(a) A lower benchmarking score means the rival's resource should always be imitated.
(b) A firm's equipment is a primary intangible resource.
(c) The O component of VRIO focuses on whether the firm has appropriate policies and procedures to support exploitation of key resources.
(d) Human resources are tangible resources.

> **Answer: (c).** The O in VRIO = Organisation — does the firm have the right policies, procedures, structures, and incentive systems to capture value from its V+R+I resources? (a) is wrong: a lower score doesn't mean automatic imitation — context matters. (b) is wrong: equipment is tangible, not intangible. (d) is wrong: human resources are their own category, not tangible.

---

**Q5: FDI Types (Ch6)**

Statement I: If Toyota establishes a car assembly plant in Belgium, it engages in horizontal FDI.
Statement II: If Toyota establishes an R&D center in Belgium, it engages in downstream vertical FDI.
Which characterization is correct?

> **Answer: Statement I is true; Statement II is false.** Statement I: Toyota makes cars in Japan and Belgium — same activity in both locations = horizontal FDI. Statement II: R&D is an UPSTREAM activity (earlier in the value chain), not downstream. So it's upstream vertical FDI, not downstream. Downstream would be distribution, retail, or after-sales service.

---

**Q6: Uppsala Model (Ch11)**

Which statement about internationalization process models is correct?
(a) They focus on a single foreign expansion decision.
(b) According to Uppsala, internationalization is a dynamic process of learning where firms take decisions based on earlier experiences.
(c) According to Uppsala, firms first need to learn about a foreign market before entering it.
(d) Stages models describe internationalization as a process of network building.

> **Answer: (b).** Uppsala is a dynamic process — firms learn through experience during internationalization and progressively increase commitment. (a) is wrong: process models cover multiple decisions over time (unlike OLI which is one decision). (c) is wrong: Uppsala says firms learn DURING entry through experiential knowledge, not BEFORE entry. (d) is wrong: stages models describe sequential stages of increasing commitment, not network building — the network model describes network building.

---

**Q7: M&A Motives (Ch14)**

Peng and Meyer distinguish three sets of motives for cross-border M&As. Which ones?
(a) Short-term, synergistic, and managerial.
(b) Long-term, hubris, and managerial.
(c) Hidden, synergistic, and hubris.
(d) Synergistic, hubris, and managerial.

> **Answer: (d).** The three motives are: (1) Synergistic — genuine value creation from combining firms; (2) Hubris — managers overestimate their ability to create value, overpay; (3) Managerial self-interest — empire building, compensation tied to firm size. Options (a), (b), and (c) introduce non-existent categories.

---

**Q8: MNE Strategy (Ch15)**

Which MNE strategy is characterized by the highest amount of knowledge exchange among foreign subsidiaries?
(a) Transnational.
(b) Home replication.
(c) Global standards.
(d) Localization.

> **Answer: (a).** Transnational strategy has multi-directional knowledge flows: HQ ↔ subsidiaries ↔ subsidiaries. Home replication and global standards have one-way flows (HQ → subsidiaries). Localization has minimal flows (each subsidiary is autonomous). The transnational strategy explicitly stimulates knowledge exchange between all units to spread local innovations globally.

## Open Question Practice Templates

### Template 1: OLI Paradigm Question

*If asked "Explain the OLI paradigm," structure your answer as:*

1. **Define OLI** — Dunning's eclectic paradigm explains why firms choose FDI over alternatives (licensing, exporting, staying domestic).
2. **O-advantage** — firm-specific resources giving competitive edge over local firms (technology, brand, management). *Without O:* no reason to go abroad — local firms would outcompete the foreign entrant.
3. **L-advantage** — location-specific benefits of a particular foreign country (market size, cheap labour, natural resources, institutions, clusters). *Without L:* serve foreign markets via exports from home; no reason to produce abroad.
4. **I-advantage** — benefits of keeping operations in-house rather than contracting (protect IP, reduce transaction costs, maintain quality). *Without I:* use licensing/franchising instead.
5. **Conclusion** — all three advantages must be present simultaneously for FDI to be the optimal choice.

*Exam tip:* Use a table (O / L / I rows, definition + example + "without it" columns). Compare OLI vs Uppsala if asked: OLI = static decision; Uppsala = dynamic learning process.

---

### Template 2: VRIO Benchmarking Question

*If asked "Apply VRIO to two firms," structure your answer as:*

1. Name two firms in the same industry.
2. List 4–6 key resources/capabilities (e.g., brand, supply chain, technology, human capital, store network, digital platform).
3. Score each resource for both firms on a 1–5 scale.
4. Draw two radar/spider charts — one per firm — showing relative strengths.
5. For each resource, apply VRIO: Is Firm A's brand Valuable? Rare compared to B? Inimitable? Is Firm A Organised to exploit it?
6. Conclusion: identify which resources give sustained competitive advantage, temporary advantage, or no advantage for each firm.
7. Link back to overall competitive position: why does one firm outperform the other?

---

### Template 3: Merger Control Question

*If asked "Explain the controls for a merger," structure as:*

1. Name the authority (**DG Competition**, European Commission) and legal basis (EUMR, Art. 101/102 TFEU).
2. Define the **SIEC test** — Significant Impediment to Effective Competition — standard since 2004.
3. Explain the process: notification → **Phase I** (25 working days) → Phase II if concerns raised (90 working days, extendable).
4. Identify the relevant **theory of harm** for the case: horizontal unilateral, horizontal coordinated, vertical input/output foreclosure, or conglomerate.
5. **Calculate or estimate HHI** if data given: Σ(s²); state pre-merger, post-merger, and delta HHI.
6. Discuss likely **outcome and remedies**: structural divestiture preferred over behavioural commitments.
7. Apply to the specific case with concrete details.

*Exam tip:* The professor values precise definitions and logical application over lengthy prose. Use tables where possible.
