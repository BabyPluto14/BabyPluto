1 · What Exactly is a Financial Option?

Start here. Every complexity builds on this one idea.

An option is a contract between two people. It gives the buyer the right — but never the obligation — to buy or sell something at a fixed price, before a fixed date. The seller (called the writer) collects a fee and must fulfill the contract if the buyer chooses to use it.

That phrase — right but not obligation — is everything. It means the buyer can always walk away if the deal is bad for them. The worst they can lose is the fee they paid upfront. This fee is called the premium.

🏠
Real-Life Analogy — House Purchase Option You find a house listed at €300,000. You're not sure yet whether to buy it — you're waiting for your job transfer to be confirmed. You pay the seller €5,000 to "reserve" the right to buy the house at €300,000 any time in the next 3 months.
  • Good outcome: Your transfer is confirmed, house prices rise to €340,000. You exercise your right and buy at €300,000. You immediately have €40,000 in equity, minus the €5,000 premium = +€35,000 gain.
  • Bad outcome: Your transfer falls through. You don't want the house. You simply don't exercise. You lose only the €5,000 premium. −€5,000 max loss.
  • Key insight: The seller (writer) must sell at €300,000 if you demand it — even if the market price is now €340,000. They took on that obligation in exchange for your €5,000.
The 5 Things That Define Any Option

1. Underlying Asset

What the option is based on — a stock (e.g. Apple), an index (S&P 500), a currency (EUR/USD), a commodity (oil). Options do not directly own the asset.

2. Strike Price (K)

The fixed price at which you have the right to buy or sell. Also called the exercise price. Set when the contract is created.

3. Expiry Date (T)

The deadline. After this date, the option is worthless. A European option can only be exercised on this exact date. An American option can be exercised any time before it.

4. Premium

The upfront price paid by the buyer to the writer. This is the option's market value — what it costs to acquire the right. Non-refundable whether you exercise or not.

5. Type: Call or Put

A call gives the right to buy. A put gives the right to sell. That is the entire difference between the two product types.

2 · Call Options — The Right to Buy

A call gives you the right to purchase an asset at the strike price. You profit when prices rise.

A call option gives the holder the right to buy the underlying asset at the strike price (K) on or before the expiry date. You buy a call when you believe the asset's price will rise.

Worked Example — Buying a Call (Long Call)
Example Long Call on Apple Stock

Setup: Apple stock currently trades at €150. You believe it will rise over the next 3 months. You buy 1 call option:

  • Underlying: Apple (AAPL)
  • Strike price (K): €155
  • Expiry: 3 months from now
  • Premium paid: €8 per share (each contract = 100 shares, so total cost = €800)

Now let's walk through every possible outcome at expiry:

🚀 Scenario A
Apple rises to €180

You exercise the call. You have the right to buy at €155. The market price is €180.

Profit per share = €180 − €155 − €8 (premium) = +€17 per share

On a 100-share contract: +€1,700 profit. Your €800 investment grew to €2,500.

Note: you don't have to physically buy the shares — in practice you just receive the cash difference.

😐 Scenario B
Apple rises slightly to €160

You exercise the call — barely profitable to do so.

Profit per share = €160 − €155 − €8 = −€3 per share

Wait — shouldn't you exercise? You technically exercise to recover €5 (160−155), reducing your loss from €8 to €3. But still a net loss of €300 on the contract.

Breakeven price = Strike + Premium = €155 + €8 = €163. You only profit once the stock exceeds €163.

📉 Scenario C
Apple falls to €120

You do NOT exercise. Why would you buy Apple at €155 when the market price is only €120? That would be irrational.

You let the option expire worthless. Loss = the premium only = −€8 per share = −€800 total.

This is the power of options: No matter how far Apple falls — to €100, €50, €0 — your loss is capped at the €800 premium. You cannot lose more than what you paid.

── Long Call Profit Formula ──────────────────
Profit = max(S_T − K, 0) − Premium

Where S_T = stock price at expiry

Example A: max(180 − 155, 0) − 8 = +17 per share Example B: max(160 − 155, 0) − 8 = −3 per share Example C: max(120 − 155, 0) − 8 = −8 per share (option expires worthless)

Breakeven = Strike Price + Premium = 155 + 8 = 163
✅
The Long Call Summary Max loss = premium paid (€800). Max gain = unlimited (stock could go to infinity). You need the stock to rise above the breakeven price (strike + premium) to make money. The position benefits from rising prices and higher volatility.
What About Selling a Call? (Short Call)

The short call is the other side of the same contract. You are the writer — you sold someone else the right to buy Apple from you at €155. You collected the €8 premium upfront.

📉 Best Case
Apple stays below €163

The buyer doesn't exercise (it's not profitable for them). You keep the entire €8 premium per share as pure profit. +€800 max gain.

🚀 Worst Case
Apple rises to €200, €250...

The buyer exercises. You must sell Apple at €155 even though the market price is €200. You lose €200 − €155 = €45 per share, minus the €8 premium you received = −€37 per share = −€3,700 on the contract.

If Apple goes to €300, you lose €137 per share. There is no cap on the loss. This is why short call positions are extremely risky without owning the underlying stock.

⚠️
Short Call = Unlimited Risk Selling a call is risky because your loss grows without limit as the stock price rises. Professional investors who sell calls typically own the underlying stock (a "covered call") to cap the risk. Naked call writing (selling calls without owning the stock) is one of the most dangerous strategies in finance.

3 · Put Options — The Right to Sell

A put gives you the right to sell an asset at the strike price. Think of it as insurance against falling prices.

A put option gives the holder the right to sell the underlying asset at the strike price (K) on or before the expiry date. You buy a put when you believe the asset's price will fall, or when you already own the asset and want to protect against a drop in its value.

🌾
Intuition — The Farmer's Insurance A wheat farmer will harvest 10,000 bushels in 6 months. Current price is €5/bushel. The farmer fears prices might fall by harvest time. He buys a put option with a strike of €4.80 — locking in the right to sell at €4.80 no matter what. If wheat crashes to €3, he exercises the put and still sells at €4.80. He's paid an insurance premium for that guarantee. If wheat rises to €6, he ignores the put and sells on the open market at €6.
Worked Example — Buying a Put (Long Put)
Example Long Put on a Stock You Own

Setup: You own 100 shares of a company currently worth €80 each (total: €8,000). You're worried the stock might fall ahead of an earnings announcement. You buy a put option:

  • Strike price (K): €75
  • Expiry: 1 month from now
  • Premium paid: €4 per share (total cost: €400)

This is called a protective put — you're insuring your stock holding.

🚀 Scenario A
Stock rises to €100

You do NOT exercise the put. Why sell at €75 when the market pays €100?

Your shares are worth €10,000. Minus the €400 premium cost = net gain of +€1,600 vs. your original €8,000 + €400 invested.

The put expires worthless, but you still benefited from the stock's rise. The €400 was the "insurance cost."

📉 Scenario B
Stock crashes to €50

You exercise the put. You sell your 100 shares at €75 (the strike), even though the market price is only €50.

Without the put: your shares would be worth €5,000 — a loss of €3,000 on your €8,000 investment.

With the put: you sell at €75 × 100 = €7,500, minus €400 premium = net proceeds of €7,100. The put saved you from a €3,000 loss — you lost only €900 total (the €400 premium + the €500 gap between your entry price and the strike).

── Long Put Profit Formula ───────────────────
Profit = max(K − S_T, 0) − Premium

If stock falls to 50: max(75 − 50, 0) − 4 = +21 per share (pure put profit) If stock stays at 80: max(75 − 80, 0) − 4 = −4 per share (option expires worthless) If stock rises to 100: max(75 − 100, 0) − 4 = −4 per share

Breakeven = Strike Price − Premium = 75 − 4 = 71 (stock must fall below 71 for the put to be profitable on its own)
🛡️
The Long Put Summary Max loss = premium paid (€400 — this is your insurance cost). Max gain = strike price minus premium (€71 × 100 = €7,100 if the stock goes to zero). You need the stock to fall below the breakeven (strike − premium) to profit on the put itself. Used as portfolio insurance by virtually every institutional investor.

4 · The 4 Positions — Side by Side

Every option trade is one of these four positions. Know them cold.

Combining 2 products (call, put) × 2 sides (buy, sell) gives 4 fundamental positions. Here they are summarised with a concrete example for each, all using the same stock at €100 and a strike price of €100.

📞 Position 1 — Long Call (Buy a Call)

What you do: Pay €7 premium. Gain the right to buy stock at €100.

You want: Stock to rise above €107 (breakeven).

Max loss: −€7 (the premium)

Max gain: Unlimited

Example: Stock rises to €130. Profit = €130 − €100 − €7 = +€23

Typical user: Investor believing the stock will rise, or a company hedging against rising input costs.

📞 Position 2 — Short Call (Sell/Write a Call)

What you do: Collect €7 premium. Take on the obligation to sell stock at €100 if exercised.

You want: Stock to stay below €107 (breakeven).

Max gain: +€7 (the premium)

Max loss: Unlimited

Example: Stock rises to €130. Loss = −(€130 − €100) + €7 = −€23

Typical user: Investor who owns the stock and wants to earn extra income ("covered call").

📤 Position 3 — Long Put (Buy a Put)

What you do: Pay €6 premium. Gain the right to sell stock at €100.

You want: Stock to fall below €94 (breakeven).

Max loss: −€6 (the premium)

Max gain: Up to €94 (if stock goes to zero)

Example: Stock falls to €70. Profit = €100 − €70 − €6 = +€24

Typical user: Investor expecting a drop, or someone insuring an existing stock position.

📤 Position 4 — Short Put (Sell/Write a Put)

What you do: Collect €6 premium. Take on the obligation to buy stock at €100 if exercised.

You want: Stock to stay above €94 (breakeven).

Max gain: +€6 (the premium)

Max loss: Up to −€94 (if stock goes to zero)

Example: Stock falls to €70. Loss = −(€100 − €70) + €6 = −€24

Typical user: Investor happy to buy the stock if it falls to the strike price — earns premium while waiting.

Complete Summary Table
Position You pay/receive Breakeven Profit when… Max Loss Max Gain
Long Call PAY premium K + premium Stock rises above breakeven Premium Unlimited
Short Call RECEIVE premium K + premium Stock stays below breakeven Unlimited Premium
Long Put PAY premium K − premium Stock falls below breakeven Premium K − premium (stock → 0)
Short Put RECEIVE premium K − premium Stock stays above breakeven K − premium (stock → 0) Premium
🔑
The Golden Rule of Options Buyers (long positions) always have limited loss and asymmetric upside. Writers (short positions) have limited gain but large or unlimited risk. This asymmetry is why buyers pay a premium — they're buying protection and upside. Writers earn the premium as compensation for taking on the risk.

Moneyness tells you whether immediate exercise would be profitable.

Moneyness describes the relationship between the current stock price and the option's strike price. It answers the question: "If I exercised this option right now, would I gain anything?"

Example Moneyness with Numbers

Stock price is currently €90. You have two options:

  • Call option, K = €100 → OTM (you'd buy at €100, but market is only €90 — no benefit)
  • Call option, K = €80 → ITM (you'd buy at €80, immediately sell at €90 — €10 intrinsic value)
  • Put option, K = €100 → ITM (you'd sell at €100, but market is only €90 — €10 intrinsic value)
  • Put option, K = €80 → OTM (you'd sell at €80, but market is €90 — you'd rather sell on the market)
Intrinsic Value vs Time Value

Every option's premium has two components:

💎 Intrinsic Value

The profit you'd make from exercising right now.

  • Call: max(S − K, 0)
  • Put: max(K − S, 0)

OTM options have zero intrinsic value. ITM options always have positive intrinsic value.

Example: Call K=€100, Stock=€110 → Intrinsic value = €10

⏳ Time Value

The extra premium above intrinsic value — the value of having time left before expiry.

Time value reflects the possibility that the stock moves further in your favour before expiry. It decays to zero at expiry — this is called theta decay.

Example: Same call trades at €14 in the market. Time value = €14 − €10 = €4

Total Premium = Intrinsic Value + Time Value

💡
Why do OTM options still have value? Even if an option is currently worthless to exercise (OTM), there is still time before expiry for the stock to move into profitable territory. You're paying for that possibility. This is time value. An option with 6 months to expiry is worth more than the same option with 1 week to expiry — there's more time for something good to happen.

6 · What Makes an Option More or Less Valuable?

Five variables drive every option's price. Understand the logic behind each one.

You don't need to memorise the Black-Scholes formula to understand what makes options more expensive or cheaper. There are 5 key variables — and the intuition behind each is straightforward.

1. Stock Price (S) ↑ → Call ↑, Put ↓

As the stock price rises, a call option moves deeper into the money (more intrinsic value). The higher the stock price relative to the strike, the more valuable a call becomes — and the less valuable a put becomes.

Example: A call with K=€100. When S=€90 it's OTM. When S=€110 it has €10 intrinsic value. When S=€130 it has €30 intrinsic value.

2. Strike Price (K) ↑ → Call ↓, Put ↑

A higher strike makes it harder to profit from a call (the stock must rise higher before you're in the money). It makes a put more valuable (you get to sell at a higher price).

Example: Two calls, both on the same stock at €100. Call A has K=€90 (already ITM, worth more). Call B has K=€120 (far OTM, cheaper).

3. Time to Expiry (T) ↑ → Both ↑

More time = more opportunity for the stock to move in your favour = higher option value. This is why long-dated options are more expensive than short-dated ones.

Example: A 6-month call is worth more than a 1-week call on the same stock. The 1-week option has very little time for the stock to rally above the strike.

4. Risk-Free Rate (r) ↑ → Call ↑, Put ↓

Higher interest rates reduce the present value of the strike price you would pay (for a call). This makes calls more attractive (you effectively pay less in today's money). Puts work in reverse — higher rates reduce the PV of what you'd receive from selling.

Example: When central banks raise interest rates sharply (as in 2022–23), call option values increased slightly, all else equal.

⭐ 5. Volatility (σ) ↑ → BOTH Calls and Puts ↑ — The Most Important Driver

This is the most important and most counterintuitive relationship in options. Higher volatility — meaning the stock price swings more wildly — makes both calls and puts more valuable. Here's the critical asymmetry:

  • If volatility helps: A highly volatile stock might rocket far above the strike — creating huge call profits.
  • If volatility hurts: If the stock crashes, the call buyer just doesn't exercise. Loss stays capped at the premium.
  • Option holders benefit from big moves in either direction but don't suffer extra loss from the bad moves (because they can always just not exercise).
Numerical illustration:
Stock A: always stays near €100 (low volatility). A call at K=€110 is nearly worthless — stock never gets close.
Stock B: swings between €70 and €140 (high volatility). Same call at K=€110 is very valuable — there's a real chance it hits €140.

This is why options traders care enormously about implied volatility (IV) — the market's forecast of future volatility, extracted from current option prices. High IV = expensive options. Low IV = cheap options. The VIX (S&P 500 implied volatility) is called the "fear gauge" — when markets are fearful, volatility spikes and option prices jump.

7 · The Black-Scholes Formula — Intuition Without the Math

The formula looks complex. The idea behind it is simple. Let's break it apart piece by piece.

In 1973, Fischer Black and Myron Scholes solved one of finance's biggest puzzles: how much should an option fairly cost? Before their work, pricing was guesswork. Their formula gives a single mathematical answer using just 5 inputs.

🔑
The Core Idea — Replicating Portfolio Black-Scholes works by asking: "What combination of the stock + a risk-free loan would perfectly mimic the option's payoff?" Because these two things produce identical outcomes, they must have the same price in an efficient market — otherwise traders would arbitrage the difference until it disappears. This is called the Law of One Price.
── The Black-Scholes Formula ─────────────────────────────
C = S · N(d₁) − K · e⁻ʳᵀ · N(d₂)

d₁ = [ ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)·T ] / (σ·√T) d₂ = d₁ − σ·√T

S = current stock price K = strike price T = time to expiry in years (e.g. 6 months = 0.5) r = annual risk-free rate (e.g. 5% = 0.05) σ = annual volatility (e.g. 30% = 0.30) N(·) = probability from standard normal distribution e = 2.718... (used to discount the future payment)
Breaking the Formula into Plain English

The formula has two parts:

Part 1: S · N(d₁)

This is the expected benefit of receiving the stock.

S = what the stock is worth today.
N(d₁) = the probability (adjusted for risk) that you'll exercise and receive the stock.

Think of this as: "What is the expected value of the stock I'll receive, weighted by the chance I actually receive it?"

Part 2: K · e⁻ʳᵀ · N(d₂)

This is the present value of what you pay.

K = strike price (what you pay to exercise).
e⁻ʳᵀ = discount factor (future payment in today's money).
N(d₂) = probability that you'll exercise the option.

Think of this as: "What is the present value of the strike price I'll pay, weighted by the probability I'll actually pay it?"

Call Price = (Expected value of stock you receive) − (PV of what you pay)

This is the same logic as any financial asset: its value = what you get − what you pay, both adjusted for time and probability.

Worked Numerical Example
Example Calculating a Call Option Price with Black-Scholes

Given:

  • Stock price S = €100
  • Strike price K = €100 (at the money)
  • Time to expiry T = 1 year
  • Risk-free rate r = 5%
  • Volatility σ = 20%

Step 1 — Calculate d₁ and d₂:

d₁ = [ln(100/100) + (0.05 + 0.20²/2) × 1] / (0.20 × √1) = [0 + (0.05 + 0.02)] / 0.20 = 0.07 / 0.20 = 0.35 d₂ = 0.35 − 0.20 × 1 = 0.15

Step 2 — Look up N(d₁) and N(d₂) from normal distribution table:

  • N(0.35) ≈ 0.637 (63.7% probability)
  • N(0.15) ≈ 0.560 (56.0% probability)

Step 3 — Apply the formula:

C = 100 × 0.637 − 100 × e⁻⁰·⁰⁵ × 0.560 = 63.7 − 100 × 0.9512 × 0.560 = 63.7 − 53.27 = €10.45

Interpretation: The fair value of this at-the-money call with 1 year to expiry is approximately €10.45. This is the premium a rational buyer should pay and writer should accept.

⚠️
Important Limitations of Black-Scholes
  • Only valid for European options (exercisable only at expiry)
  • Assumes volatility is constant over the option's life — in reality it changes (the "volatility smile" problem)
  • Assumes stock prices follow a smooth, continuous log-normal distribution — but real markets have sudden jumps and "fat tails"
  • Does not account for dividends in the basic form

8 · Hedging vs Speculation — Two Very Different Uses

The same option contract can serve completely opposite purposes depending on who uses it and why.

🛡️ Hedging — Reducing Risk You Already Have

  • You have an existing exposure to price risk
  • The option offsets an existing risk — like taking out insurance
  • The cost (premium) is a business expense, not a bet
  • You accept a smaller outcome in the good scenario to protect against the bad scenario

Examples of hedgers:
Airlines buy call options on jet fuel — limits exposure to oil price spikes.
Exporters buy put options on foreign currency — limits damage if their domestic currency strengthens.
Investors buy put options on their stock portfolios — limits loss if markets crash.

📈 Speculation — Taking on Risk for Profit

  • You have no existing exposure — you're creating a new position
  • You're making a directional bet on price movement
  • Options offer leverage — a small premium controls a large position
  • Potential for large gains on small investment — but also total loss of premium

Examples of speculators:
A trader buying call options on NVIDIA before an earnings announcement.
A hedge fund buying put options on bank stocks anticipating a rate crisis.
A retail investor buying OTM call options ("lottery tickets") on a meme stock.

Hedging Example Car Manufacturer Buying Steel

A German car manufacturer needs 10,000 tonnes of steel in 6 months. Current price is €600/tonne. Total exposure: €6,000,000.

  • Fear: Steel prices rise to €800/tonne → extra cost of €2,000,000
  • Hedge: Buy call options on steel with strike €620/tonne. Premium = €15/tonne = €150,000 total cost.
  • If steel rises to €800: Exercise call options — buy at €620. Saving = (€800 − €620) × 10,000 = €1,800,000. Net gain from hedge = €1,800,000 − €150,000 premium = €1,650,000 saved.
  • If steel stays at €600: Don't exercise. Lose only the €150,000 premium. This was the "insurance cost" for peace of mind.
Speculation Example Trader Betting on an Earnings Surprise

A trader believes Company X will beat earnings estimates. Stock is at €50, earnings announcement in 2 weeks.

  • Buying 100 shares directly costs €5,000. If stock rises to €60, gain = €1,000 (20% return).
  • Instead, buy call options: K=€52, premium = €2/share. Cost = €200 for a 100-share contract.
  • If stock rises to €60: Option profit = €60 − €52 − €2 = €6 per share = +€600 profit on a €200 investment = +300% return.
  • If stock falls to €45: Option expires worthless. Loss = €200 only. Compared to owning 100 shares: loss would have been €500.

Options allow speculators to make larger bets with smaller capital — this is financial leverage.

9 · The Long Straddle — Profiting from Uncertainty Itself

What if you don't know which direction prices will move — but you know something big is coming?

A long straddle involves buying both a call option and a put option on the same asset, with the same strike price and the same expiry date. You pay two premiums, but you profit if the price moves significantly in either direction.

🌍
Intuition — The Brexit Vote Example The night before the UK's Brexit referendum in June 2016, traders didn't know if the UK would vote Leave or Remain. But they knew the result would cause a large move in the British pound in one direction. A straddle on GBP/USD was ideal: if Leave won, the pound would crash (put profits). If Remain won, the pound would rally (call profits). The direction didn't matter — only the size of the move.
Example Long Straddle on Earnings Announcement

Setup: Company reports earnings next week. Stock currently at €100. Big move expected but direction unknown.

  • Buy call option: K=€100, premium = €6
  • Buy put option: K=€100, premium = €5
  • Total cost = €11 per share
  • Breakeven: stock must move above €111 or below €89
Stock Price at ExpiryCall PayoffPut PayoffNet Profit/Loss
€130+€30 − €6 = +€240 − €5 = −€5+€19 ✅
€115+€15 − €6 = +€90 − €5 = −€5+€4 ✅
€111+€11 − €6 = +€50 − €5 = −€5€0 (breakeven)
€1000 − €6 = −€60 − €5 = −€5−€11 (max loss)
€890 − €6 = −€6+€11 − €5 = +€6€0 (breakeven)
€750 − €6 = −€6+€25 − €5 = +€20+€14 ✅

📖
The Short Straddle — the opposite bet Selling both a call and a put collects both premiums (+€11 in this example). The short straddle writer profits if the stock barely moves — they keep both premiums if the stock stays between €89 and €111. But if the stock moves dramatically in either direction, losses can be very large. This strategy is used by traders who believe a market event will cause less movement than other traders expect — essentially, selling insurance that they believe is overpriced.
🏢
Corporate Use Case — Currency Risk Hedging An international import-export company receives revenue in both USD and EUR. If EUR/USD rises, their USD revenue is worth less in euros. If EUR/USD falls, their EUR costs rise. They face risk in both directions — so a long straddle on EUR/USD protects them regardless of which way the exchange rate moves. The premium is a known, fixed hedging cost that caps their worst-case outcome.

10 · Common Mistakes & Exam Traps

The misconceptions that catch students out in exams — and in real life.

✗

"An option is like buying a stock"

Why it's wrong: An option is a right, not an asset. Buying a stock means you own a piece of the company — you receive dividends and bear all gains/losses. Buying an option gives you a conditional right. You do not own the underlying stock unless you exercise. The payoff profiles are completely different.

✗

"Higher volatility is bad for option holders"

Why it's wrong: This is the most common mistake. Volatility benefits option holders because their downside is capped (they just don't exercise) but their upside is uncapped. A rational option holder always benefits from more volatility. It is direct stockholders who fear volatility — not option holders.

✗

"If the option is out of the money, I've lost all my money"

Why it's wrong: An OTM option still has time value as long as there is time before expiry. It can still become profitable if the stock moves. Only at expiry does an OTM option expire worthless. Before expiry, you can sell the option in the market and recover some of the premium.

✗

"The option writer (seller) takes on limited risk"

Why it's wrong: The option buyer has limited risk (only the premium). The option writer has potentially unlimited risk (for calls) or very large risk (for puts). This asymmetry is the fundamental risk imbalance in options — the writer takes on the risk, the buyer pays for the protection.

✗

"Black-Scholes works for all types of options"

Why it's wrong: Black-Scholes in its basic form is only valid for European options on non-dividend-paying stocks. It assumes constant volatility and continuous stock price movement — both simplifications. For American options (early exercise possible), dividend-paying stocks, or exotic options, different and more complex models are needed (binomial trees, Monte Carlo simulation, etc.).

✗

"If an option is in the money, I must exercise it immediately"

Why it's wrong: For American options, early exercise is almost never optimal for calls on non-dividend-paying stocks. The option still has time value — you capture more total value by selling the option in the market than by exercising it early. You only lose the time value by exercising early. The exception: deeply ITM puts close to expiry, or calls on stocks about to pay a large dividend.

Financial Options — The Core Principles to Remember

1. An option is a right, not an obligation — the buyer can always walk away. Loss is capped at the premium.
2. Calls profit from price rises. Puts profit from price falls.
3. Buyers (long) have limited loss / asymmetric upside. Writers (short) collect premium but face large/unlimited loss.
4. The 5 value drivers: S, K, T, r, σ. Volatility increases the value of both calls and puts — the most important and counterintuitive result.
5. Black-Scholes prices a European call as: (expected stock gain) − (PV of strike price paid), each weighted by exercise probability.
6. Options are used for hedging (reducing existing risk) or speculation (directional bets with leverage). Same contract, opposite purpose.
