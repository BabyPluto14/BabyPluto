1 · What is a Bond?

A bond is essentially a loan — but packaged as a tradeable security. When a company or government needs money, instead of going to a bank it can issue bonds to many investors. Each investor lends a fixed amount and in return receives regular interest payments (coupons) and their money back at the end.

Think of it this way: if you buy a bond, you are the bank.

The Three Parties

Issuer — the borrower (government, company, bank). They sell the bond to raise cash.

Bond-holder — the lender/investor. They buy the bond and receive future cash flows.

Market — investors can resell bonds to each other on the secondary market.

Why Bonds? (vs. Stocks)

Bonds are "fixed-income" — the payments are predetermined, unlike dividends on stocks.

Bondholders have a legal claim to payments and are paid before shareholders in bankruptcy.

This makes bonds generally less risky than stocks — but also lower return.

AB InBev Example: In September 2023, AB InBev (maker of Budweiser, Stella Artois) had 74 different bonds outstanding — each with different maturity dates, currencies, and coupon rates. Large companies routinely manage dozens of bonds simultaneously.

2 · The Global Bond Market

The global bond market is enormous — larger than the global stock market. As of 2024 estimates, it is worth over $130 trillion, making it one of the most important financial markets in the world.

Sovereign Bonds — 62.2%

Issued by governments (e.g., US Treasury, German Bunds, Japanese JGBs). These are typically the safest bonds since governments can raise taxes or print currency.

Corporate Bonds — 37.8%

Issued by companies. Higher yield than government bonds because there's more risk the company could default. AB InBev, Apple, Toyota all issue corporate bonds.

3 · Key Terminology

Nominal / Par / Face Value
The reference amount printed on the bond. Usually $1,000 or €1,000. This is NOT necessarily the price you pay or receive — it's the baseline for calculating coupon payments. Example: a 5% coupon on a $1,000 face value = $50/year.
Maturity / Maturity Date
The date when the bond expires. On this date, the issuer makes the final coupon payment AND repays the principal (usually par value). A bond maturing in 10 years pays coupons every year for 10 years, then repays $1,000.
Coupon Payment
The periodic interest payment the bond-holder receives. Can be annual, semi-annual, or quarterly. Named "coupon" because in the old days bonds had physical paper coupons you'd tear off and hand in to collect interest.
Coupon Rate
Coupon rate = Annual Coupon ÷ Par Value. A bond with par value $1,000 and coupon rate 6% pays $60/year. This rate is fixed at issuance and does not change (for standard bonds).
Yield to Maturity (YTM)
The total annual return you'd earn if you buy the bond today and hold it until maturity. It's the discount rate that makes the present value of all future cash flows equal to the current bond price. YTM fluctuates with market interest rates.
Issue Price vs. Bond Price
Issue price = price when the bond is first sold (primary market). Bond price = current market price (secondary market). Both can differ from par value. If interest rates rise after issuance, the bond price falls below par value (and vice versa).
At / Above / Below Par
At par: Bond price = Face value (e.g., $1,000 = $1,000). YTM = coupon rate.
Below par (discount): Bond price < Face value. YTM > coupon rate.
Above par (premium): Bond price > Face value. YTM < coupon rate.
Coupon Rate vs. YTM — The Key Difference: The coupon rate is fixed forever. The YTM changes every day as market interest rates move. If market rates rise, your fixed coupon looks less attractive, so the bond price drops — and the YTM (calculated from that lower price) rises to match market rates.

4 · Types of Bond Issuers

🏛️ Governments ("Sovereigns")

Treasury Bills (<1 year) — short-term government debt, often zero-coupon

Treasury Notes (1–10 years) — medium-term government bonds

Treasury Bonds (>10 years) — long-term government bonds; most sensitive to interest rate changes

Examples: US Treasury, German Bunds, UK Gilts, Belgian OLOs

🏢 Companies ("Corporates")

Commercial Paper (<1 year) — short-term, money market instrument

Short-term bonds (<5 years) — also classified as money market

Long-term bonds (>5 years) — the classic corporate bond

Examples: Apple, Microsoft, AB InBev, Tesla all issue corporate bonds

🏦 Financial Institutions ("Financials")

Certificate of Deposit (CD) — bank issues a timed deposit paying fixed interest

Savings Certificate — similar, offered to retail customers

These are important in banking and are also subject to interest rate risk

5 · Bond Modalities (Special Types)

🕳️ Zero-Coupon Bond
Pays no periodic coupons. Sold at a deep discount and redeemed at par value. Your return comes entirely from the price difference. Example: buy for $700 today, receive $1,000 in 5 years. US Treasury Bills work this way. High interest rate risk because all value is at the end.
📞 Callable Bond
The issuer has the right to redeem (call back) the bond before maturity, usually at par value. Issuers do this when rates fall — they refinance at lower rates. Bad for investors (who lose higher coupons). Callable bonds therefore pay a higher yield to compensate investors for call risk.
🔄 Convertible Bond
Can be converted into shares of the issuing company at a fixed price. Attractive to investors who want bond safety but upside if stock rises. Usually pays a lower coupon because of this conversion option's value.
〰️ Floating Rate Bond
Coupon rate is not fixed — it resets periodically based on a reference rate like LIBOR (now SOFR) + a spread. Example: "SOFR + 1.5%". If interest rates rise, your coupon rises too. Much less interest-rate risk than fixed-rate bonds.
♾️ Perpetual Bond
Never matures — pays coupons forever with no final repayment. Rare but exists (e.g., UK Consols). Value = Annual Coupon ÷ YTM. Very high interest rate risk. Also known as "Consols" or "Perps".

6 · Bond Pricing — NPV of Future Cash Flows

The Core Principle

A bond's price is simply the present value (NPV) of all its future cash flows — both coupon payments and the final repayment — discounted at the bond's YTM.

This is identical to the NPV logic used throughout corporate finance. The YTM is the opportunity cost (required return) for bond-holders.

// Annual coupon bond — general formula Price = C/(1+r)¹ + C/(1+r)² + ... + C/(1+r)ⁿ + FV/(1+r)ⁿ // Simplified using annuity formula Price = C × [1 - 1/(1+r)ⁿ] / r + FV / (1+r)ⁿ Where: C = periodic coupon payment (= coupon rate × par value) r = YTM per period (= annual YTM / payments per year) n = total number of periods FV = final payment (usually = par value)
The Golden Rule: YTM ↑ → Price ↓ (and vice versa)

Bond price and yield always move in opposite directions. If market rates rise, your existing bond's fixed coupons look less attractive, so its price falls until its yield matches the market rate. This is the most important relationship in bond analysis.

When YTM = Coupon Rate

Bond trades AT PAR (Price = Face Value). Your coupon rate matches the market rate, so no premium or discount.

When YTM > Coupon Rate

Bond trades BELOW PAR (discount). Market rates are higher than your coupon, so price drops to compensate buyers.

Semi-Annual Coupons (very common in the US)

Many bonds (especially US government bonds) pay coupons twice a year. Simply adjust:

  • Coupon per period = Annual coupon ÷ 2
  • Periods = Years × 2
  • YTM per period = Annual YTM ÷ 2

The formula is exactly the same — just with periods instead of years.

7 · Worked Examples

Example 1 — IBM Bond (Annual Coupon, Find Price)

Problem: Today is October 1, 2022. An IBM bond pays $35 every September 30 for 5 years. In September 2027 it also makes the final repayment of $1,000. The bond is rated AAA (YTM = 2.5%). What is the bond's price?

1
Identify the cash flows:
Coupon (C) = $35 per year  |  FV = $1,000  |  n = 5 years  |  r = 2.5%
2
Apply the bond pricing formula:
Price = 35/(1.025)¹ + 35/(1.025)² + 35/(1.025)³ + 35/(1.025)⁴ + (35+1000)/(1.025)⁵ = 34.15 + 33.31 + 32.50 + 31.71 + 907.47
3
Or use the annuity shortcut:
Annuity PV = 35 × [1 - 1/(1.025)⁵] / 0.025 = 35 × 4.6458 = 162.60 FV PV = 1000 / (1.025)⁵ = 1000 / 1.1314 = 884.00 Total Price = 162.60 + 884.00
Price ≈ $1,046.60 — The bond trades ABOVE par (premium) because the coupon rate (3.5%) > YTM (2.5%)

Intuition: The market rate is only 2.5%, but this bond pays 3.5%. That's a better deal, so investors bid the price up above $1,000.

Example 2 — German Bond with Non-Standard Final Payment

Problem: Today you purchase a German bond with nominal value €100, 5% coupon for 6 years. The final payment is €102 (not par). YTM = 3.8%. What is the price?

1
Identify cash flows:
C = 5% × €100 = €5 per year  |  FV = €102 (not par!)  |  n = 6  |  r = 3.8%
2
Calculate:
Annuity PV = 5 × [1 - 1/(1.038)⁶] / 0.038 = 5 × 5.2126 = 26.06 FV PV = 102 / (1.038)⁶ = 102 / 1.2506 = 81.56 Total Price = 26.06 + 81.56
Price ≈ €107.62 — Trades above par because coupon rate (5%) > YTM (3.8%)

Note: The final payment of €102 (instead of €100) adds extra value. Always plug in the actual final payment, not automatically par value.

Example 3 — Semi-Annual Coupons (US Government Bond)

Problem: 2-year US Government bond, nominal $1,000, annual coupon rate 4% paid semi-annually. Investors demand 2.48% return on 6-month investments. Find the price.

1
Adjust for semi-annual payments:
C per period = 4%/2 × $1,000 = $20
n = 2 × 2 = 4 periods
r per period = 2.48% (already given as 6-month rate)
2
Calculate:
Annuity PV = 20 × [1 - 1/(1.0248)⁴] / 0.0248 = 20 × 3.8058 = 76.12 FV PV = 1000 / (1.0248)⁴ = 1000 / 1.1038 = 906.03 Total Price = 76.12 + 906.03
Price ≈ $982.15 — Trades below par because the YTM per period (2.48%) implies ~4.96% annual, which is higher than the 4% coupon rate.

Key: Always adjust both the coupon AND the discount rate to the same periodicity.

Example 4 — Quarterly Coupons, Solve for Final Payment

Problem: 3-year US Government bond, $5,000 nominal, 4% annual coupon paid quarterly. Price = $5,000. Expected 1.25% return per quarter. What must the final payment be?

1
Adjust for quarterly payments:
C per period = 4%/4 × $5,000 = $50 per quarter
n = 3 × 4 = 12 periods
r = 1.25% per quarter
2
Set up the equation and solve for FV:
5000 = 50 × [1 - 1/(1.0125)¹²] / 0.0125 + FV / (1.0125)¹² 5000 = 50 × 11.0785 + FV / 1.16075 5000 = 553.93 + FV / 1.16075 FV / 1.16075 = 4446.07 FV = 4446.07 × 1.16075
Final Payment ≈ $5,160 — The final payment must exceed par value for this bond to be correctly priced at par given the quarterly compounding.
Example 5 — Given Market Price, Find YTM (Reverse Problem)

Problem: A $1,000 Treasury bond expires in 5 years, coupon rate 1.5%. Market price = 101.15% (i.e., $1,011.50). What is the YTM?

1
Identify known values:
Price = $1,011.50  |  C = 1.5% × $1,000 = $15/year  |  FV = $1,000  |  n = 5
2
Set up equation — solve for r (use trial & error or financial calculator):
1011.50 = 15 × [1 - 1/(1+r)⁵] / r + 1000 / (1+r)⁵ // Since price > par → YTM < coupon rate → YTM < 1.5% // Try r = 1.27%: PV ≈ 1011.50 ✓
YTM ≈ 1.27% — Less than the coupon rate (1.5%) because the bond trades at a premium (above par).

In Excel: use the RATE() or YIELD() function. In a financial calculator: N=5, PV=−1011.50, PMT=15, FV=1000, solve for I/Y.

8 · Bond Risks

📉 Interest Rate Risk

The most important bond risk. When market interest rates rise, existing bond prices fall. Long-term bonds and low-coupon bonds are most exposed. Measured by duration and volatility (see Section 10).

💀 Default / Credit Risk

The issuer fails to make promised payments. Measured by credit ratings (Moody's, S&P, Fitch). Higher default risk = higher YTM demanded by investors. Government bonds typically have the lowest default risk.

🔁 Re-Investment Risk

When you receive coupon payments, you must reinvest them. If rates have fallen by then, you reinvest at a lower rate than expected, reducing your total return below the original YTM.

📞 Call Risk

For callable bonds: the issuer may redeem the bond early, typically when rates fall. You lose the high coupon and must reinvest at lower market rates. Compensated by higher initial yield.

📈 Inflation Risk

If inflation rises above expectations, the fixed coupon payments lose purchasing power. Your real return is lower than expected. Inflation-linked bonds (e.g., US TIPS) address this by linking payments to a price index.

9 · Bond Ratings

Three major credit rating agencies — Moody's, S&P, and Fitch — assess the creditworthiness of bond issuers. A higher rating = lower default risk = lower YTM demanded = higher bond price.

This line matters enormously. Many pension funds and institutional investors are legally required to hold only investment-grade bonds. When a bond is downgraded below BBB (called a "fallen angel"), forced selling can cause sharp price drops. The yield spread over government bonds widens dramatically below investment grade.

10 · Duration & Volatility (Modified Duration)

Macaulay Duration — "How Long Until You Get Paid Back?"

Duration is the weighted average time (in years) until you receive the bond's cash flows — weighted by their present values. It tells you how long it takes to "recover" the bond's price in present value terms.

A zero-coupon bond has duration = maturity (all value comes at end). A coupon bond has duration < maturity (you get coupons along the way).

// Macaulay Duration formula Duration = Σ [ t × PV(CFₜ) ] / Bond Price Where: t = time period (year 1, 2, 3, ...) PV(CFₜ) = present value of cash flow at time t Σ = sum over all periods
Duration Example — 5-Year Bond, 9% Coupon, 8.5% YTM

Given: 5-year, 9% coupon, $1,000 face value, YTM = 8.5%

C = $90/year  |  FV = $1,000  |  First find bond price, then compute duration.

1
Find the bond price:
Price = 90 × [1 - 1/(1.085)⁵] / 0.085 + 1000/(1.085)⁵ = 90 × 3.9406 + 1000 × 0.6650 = 354.65 + 665.00 = $1,019.65
2
Calculate each cash flow's PV and weight by time:

Duration = 4,331.24 / 1,019.65 = 4.25 years
Macaulay Duration = 4.25 years — Even though this is a 5-year bond, you effectively "get paid back" in 4.25 years due to coupons received along the way.

Modified Duration (Volatility) — Price Sensitivity to Rate Changes

Modified duration tells you the % change in bond price for a 1 percentage point change in YTM. This is the key measure of interest rate risk.

// Modified Duration (Bond Volatility) Modified Duration (VOL%) = Macaulay Duration / (1 + YTM) // Interpretation ΔPrice% ≈ −VOL% × ΔYTM // Example: VOL = 4.0%, YTM rises by 0.5% ΔPrice% ≈ −4.0% × 0.5% = −2.0% price decrease
Example Calculation

From the duration example above: Duration = 4.25 years, YTM = 8.5%

Modified Duration = 4.25 / (1 + 0.085) = 4.25 / 1.085 = 3.92%

If YTM rises by 1%, the bond price falls by approximately 3.92%.

If YTM rises by 0.25% (25 basis points), price falls by ≈ 3.92% × 0.25 = 0.98%.

Three Main Drivers of Bond Volatility

⬆️
Higher Coupon Rate → Lower Duration → Lower Volatility

More coupon cash flows arrive early, pulling the duration (weighted average time) closer to today. The bond is less sensitive to rate changes because you recover value sooner.

⬆️
Longer Maturity → Higher Duration → Higher Volatility

A 30-year bond is far more exposed to rate changes than a 2-year bond. The distant cash flows are discounted more heavily, making the price very sensitive to rate shifts.

⬇️
Lower YTM → Higher Volatility

At lower rates, the discount factor is gentler — distant cash flows retain more of their value. This makes the price more sensitive to changes. At very low rates, a small change in yield causes a large price movement.

Practical Takeaway: Long-duration bonds (long maturity + low coupon + low YTM) are extremely interest rate sensitive. A 30-year zero-coupon bond can lose 20–30% of its value from a 1% rise in rates. Short-duration bonds (short maturity + high coupon) are much more stable.

11 · Inflation & Interest Rates

Nominal vs. Real Interest Rate — The Fisher Equation

// Fisher Equation (approximation) Real Rate ≈ Nominal Rate − Inflation Rate // Fisher Equation (exact) (1 + Nominal Rate) = (1 + Real Rate) × (1 + Inflation Rate) // Example Nominal Rate = 5%, Inflation = 3% Real Rate ≈ 5% − 3% = 2% Exact Real Rate = (1.05 / 1.03) − 1 = 1.942%
Nominal Interest Rate

The actual rate you earn/pay. Does NOT adjust for inflation. This is the rate on your bank account, bond coupon, or loan contract. What you see in the market.

Real Interest Rate

Adjusted for inflation — measures the true gain in purchasing power. If nominal rate = inflation rate, your real return is zero (you broke even in real terms).

Two Consistent Approaches to Valuation Under Inflation

Approach 1: Nominal Rate → Nominal Cash Flows

Use the nominal discount rate (which includes inflation compensation) to discount nominal cash flows (which are not adjusted for inflation). Standard bond pricing uses this approach — bond coupons are nominal, YTM is nominal.

Approach 2: Real Rate → Real Cash Flows

Strip out inflation from both: use the real discount rate to discount inflation-adjusted cash flows. Useful when comparing across time periods or countries with different inflation rates. Must be internally consistent — don't mix real rates with nominal flows.

Inflation Is Back (Post-2021)

After two decades of low inflation (2000–2020), inflation surged globally post-COVID. US CPI hit ~9% in 2022. This caused the Fed and ECB to rapidly raise interest rates. Bond prices fell sharply — 2022 was one of the worst years on record for bond returns, with long-duration bond portfolios losing 20–30%. This illustrates how real-world inflation and interest rate risk directly impact bond investors.

12 · The Yield Curve & Inverted Yield Curve

The yield curve plots interest rates (Y-axis) on government bonds of different maturities (X-axis) — from 3-month bills to 30-year bonds.

Normal (Upward Sloping)

Long-term rates > Short-term rates. Normal state of affairs. Investors demand higher compensation for longer time horizons (more uncertainty). Signals healthy economic expectations.

Inverted Yield Curve

Short-term rates > Long-term rates. Historically, this has preceded recessions in the US in nearly every case. Investors flock to long-term bonds for safety, driving long-term yields down.

Example from lecture: 5y vs. 2y: −34 basis points (inverted). 10y vs. 2y: −2 basis points (nearly flat).

Key Spread to Watch: The "10Y–2Y spread" (difference between 10-year and 2-year Treasury yields) is the most watched yield curve indicator. When it goes negative (inverted), economists start predicting recession 12–18 months ahead. It was inverted in 2022–2023 before normalizing in 2024.

13 · Chapter Recap

Terminology & Modalities

Par value, coupon rate, YTM, maturity. Special types: zero-coupon, callable, convertible, floating, perpetual.

Bond Risks & Ratings

Interest rate, default, re-investment, call, inflation risk. Rating agencies grade creditworthiness → spread over government bonds.

Bond Value = NPV

Price = PV of all coupons + PV of final payment, discounted at YTM. Can solve for price, YTM, or final payment.

YTM ↑ → Price ↓

Negative relationship between yield and price is the most important bond principle. The main manifestation of interest rate risk.

Duration & Volatility

Macaulay Duration = weighted avg. time of cash flows. Modified Duration = % price change per 1% change in YTM.

Nominal vs. Real Rates

Real Rate = Nominal Rate − Inflation. Always apply nominal rates to nominal flows, or real rates to real flows.

Yield Curve

Plots yield vs. maturity. Normal = upward slope (trust in economy). Inverted = short-term rates > long-term rates (recession warning signal). Key spread: 10Y minus 2Y Treasury yield.
