Where this unit sits

The previous unit studied firms with market power — price setters who face a downward-sloping demand curve and whose outcomes are generally not Pareto-efficient. This unit studies the opposite polar case: price-takers in competitive markets. The two guiding questions:

  • How does the behaviour of a price-taking firm differ from a price-setting one?
  • Can competition improve market outcomes? (Spoiler: under strong assumptions, competitive equilibrium is Pareto-efficient — it exhausts all gains from trade.)

Section AIntroduction

Two kinds of firm sit at opposite ends of a spectrum:

Price setter (previous unit)A firm with market power can set its own price. It faces a downward-sloping demand curve, produces where MR = MC with a markup over cost, and the outcome is generally not Pareto-efficient (some gains from trade go unrealised).
Price taker (this unit)A firm in a market with many firms selling identical products cannot influence the price. It takes the market price as given and just decides how much to produce. Its supply depends purely on its costs.

The unit builds up the price-taking model, derives the market supply curve, finds the competitive equilibrium, and asks whether competition delivers an efficient outcome — then stress-tests that conclusion with shifts, elasticities and taxes.

Listen for in the lectureKeep the contrast alive throughout: a price setter chooses a point on a demand curve; a price taker chooses a quantity along its own cost curve at a price it cannot change. Almost every result in this unit flows from that single difference.

Section BDemand & Supply

The two curves
  • Demand curve — the total quantity all consumers together want to buy at each price. It represents the willingness to pay (WTP) of buyers.
  • Supply curve — the total quantity all firms together would produce at each price. It represents the willingness to accept (WTA) of sellers.

Supply in a competitive market

When there are many firms selling identical (homogeneous) products:

  • Firms are price-takers.
  • The demand curve facing an individual firm is horizontal at the current market price (sell as much as you like at that price; charge a cent more and you sell nothing). Contrast this with the downward-sloping market demand curve.
  • A price-taking firm cannot benefit from charging a different price and cannot influence the market price.
  • Its supply therefore depends only on the costs it incurs in production.

The individual firm's supply — a bakery

A bakery can make 120 loaves at a constant marginal cost of €1.5. It can produce more, but must pay overtime, so marginal cost jumps to €2.6 beyond 120. The market price is €2.35.

The supply decision
  • If price > marginal cost of a unit → produce and sell it.
  • If marginal cost > price → do not produce it.
  • Optimal production is where price = marginal cost, so the firm's supply curve is its marginal cost curve.
Why P = MC is profit-maximising (MR = MC)Marginal revenue is the extra revenue from one more unit. The profit-maximising condition is MR = MC. For a price-taker, each unit sells at the market price, so MR = P. Hence the price-taker's rule P = MC is exactly the general MR = MC rule — perfectly consistent.

Should the firm produce at all? The shut-down check

Producing where P = MC maximises profit given that you produce. But you must also check that revenue covers costs:

  • Produce only if P ≥ AC (price at least covers average cost).
  • Otherwise exit the market (unless it is a short-run phenomenon). So the supply curve is the part of the MC curve that lies above the AC curve.

From firm to market supply

The market supply curve is the horizontal sum of all firms' quantities at each price — add up how much every firm supplies at each price.

Definition — price elasticity of supplyHow responsive quantity supplied is to price: εS = %Δ quantity supplied%Δ price Same characteristics as the elasticity of demand: a flatter supply curve is more elastic (very responsive), a steeper one is more inelastic. Elasticity will turn out to determine who bears a tax and how much prices move after a shock.

Section CCompetitive Equilibrium

Combine supply and demand to determine the equilibrium price and quantity.

Definition — competitive equilibriumA situation where all buyers and sellers are price-takers and, at the prevailing market-clearing price P*, quantity supplied = quantity demanded.

Why the price is pushed to P*

  • If price is above P* → excess supply (sellers want to sell more than buyers will buy) → downward pressure on price until P = P*.
  • If price is below P* → excess demand → buyers bid the price up until P = P*.
  • Because products are identical, buyers will buy from any seller, and everyone takes the prevailing price as given.

Worked example — tanker (shipping) market

Demand and supply as functions of price p:

D = 40 − 0.15p     S = −10 + 0.10p

Market clears when S = D:

40 − 0.15p = −10 + 0.10p  ⟹  50 = 0.25p  ⟹  p = 200,   D = S = 10

Efficiency — all gains from trade are exploited

Key efficiency resultAt the competitive equilibrium the sum of consumer and producer surplus is maximised — there is no deadweight loss. Every unit whose willingness to pay exceeds its marginal cost gets produced, and no unit beyond that. This is not the same as the point where consumer surplus alone is maximised (buyers would prefer a lower price, but that would shrink total surplus).

When is competitive equilibrium Pareto-efficient? Assumptions & caveats

Assumptions for efficiency
  • Participants are price-takers (many firms, homogeneous products).
  • Contracts are complete.
  • Product quality/characteristics are observed.
  • The transaction only affects buyers and sellers — no external effects (e.g. no pollution, cf. Unit 12).
Caveats
  • If the assumptions fail, the allocation may not be efficient.
  • Fairness: the split of total surplus depends on elasticities — the side that is less elastic captures a smaller share (share of surplus is inversely related to elasticity).
  • Genuine price-takers are hard to find in real life.
Check yourselfAt the tanker equilibrium, what happens to price if a regulation fixes p = 220? Who is rationed — buyers or sellers? Why does the market-clearing price maximise total surplus but not consumer surplus?

Section DFactors that Affect Equilibrium

The whole supply or demand curve can shift due to exogenous shocks — technological change, a change in popularity, input prices, entry/exit. Buyers and sellers then adjust until the market clears again at a new equilibrium.

A supply shift — improved baking technology

  1. Better technology lowers marginal costs → the supply curve shifts down/out.
  2. At the old price there is now excess supply → a move along the demand curve.
  3. The price falls to a new, lower equilibrium (and quantity rises).
Common misconceptionPeople often expect the market price to fall by the same amount as the cost reduction. Not so — how much price falls (vs how much quantity rises) depends on the price elasticities of demand and supply. This is why manufacturers complain that retailers don't cut retail prices one-for-one with wholesale prices.

Other shocks — entry and exit

  • The supply curve also shifts through market entry/exit. If existing firms earn economic rents and the costs of entry are not too high, new firms enter → supply shifts outward (down).
  • Demand shocks are analysed the same way; their price impact depends on supply elasticity.

Short-run vs long-run equilibrium

In the bakery market, suppose equilibrium A has price €2. At that price an individual bakery is making a profit (you can see it because price sits above its average cost). That profit attracts entry.

Long-run equilibrium conditionNew bakeries keep entering (each wanting a share of the rents) → supply shifts out and flattens → market price and profits fall — until economic rent is zero: P = MC = AC At this long-run point (C) firms just break even and entry stops.
Short-run vs long-run elasticityThe number of firms is exogenous in the short run but endogenous in the long run. Therefore supply is more elastic in the long run, as more firms can enter. Investment warning: a positive demand shock raises prices, but the rise is smaller in the long run; basing an investment on the short-run price spike risks over-investment.

Section EApplications — Taxes

Governments tax to raise revenue and/or discourage consumption. Two types:

Two kinds of tax
  • Ad valorem tax — a fixed percentage of value (e.g. VAT).
  • Specific (unit) tax — a fixed amount per quantity (e.g. €10 per airline ticket).
A tax can be collected from either side, and it drives a wedge between three prices:
  • Seller price pS — what the seller keeps after paying the tax.
  • Buyer price pB — what the buyer actually pays, tax included.
  • Market price p — the price at which the good trades.

For a specific tax t: if the seller remits it, pS = p − t and pB = p. If the buyer remits it, pB = p + t and pS = p. In both cases: express demand and supply as functions of the market price, then set supply = demand.

Worked example — a €10 airline-ticket tax

Demand and supply (thousands of tickets):

qD = 4920 − 5·pB     qS = −1746.67 + 103·pS

No tax: buyer price = seller price = market price → equilibrium b at p = 800, q = 920.

Tax of €10 collected from sellers (pS = p − 10): supply becomes qS = −1746.67 + 10⁄3(p − 10) = −1780 + 10⁄3p. Setting qD = qS gives market price p = 804 (= the buyer price), quantity q = 900, and seller price pS = 804 − 10 = 794.

Tax equivalence — who "pays" doesn't matterCollect the same €10 from buyers instead (pB = p + 10): demand becomes qD = 4920 − 5(p + 10) = 4870 − 5p. Equating with the untaxed supply gives market price p = 794, buyer price 794 + 10 = 804, seller price 794, quantity 900 — exactly the same outcome. Whether the buyer or the seller physically remits the tax is irrelevant to prices and quantities. Subsidies are just negative taxes and are analysed the same way (shift supply down or demand up).

Surplus, government revenue and deadweight loss

Before the tax, total welfare = consumer surplus + producer surplus. The tax rearranges this:

  • Consumer surplus — area under demand above the buyer price (804).
  • Producer surplus — area above supply below the seller price (794).
  • Government revenue — the tax × quantity traded (10 × 900).
  • Total welfare = CS + PS + government revenue.
Why deadweight loss arisesThe tax drives a wedge between the marginal cost of producing and the marginal benefit to consumers. Some consumers whose willingness to pay exceeds the cost of production can no longer buy → total surplus falls and quantity traded is too low.

Tax incidence — who really bears the tax?

Incidence & elasticityThe effect of a tax is the same whether collected from buyers or sellers (equivalence). With moderate elasticities: the buyer price rises by less than the tax, the seller price falls by less than the tax, and the quantity falls. Crucially, the side of the market that is less sensitive to price (less elastic) bears the larger share of the tax.

Real-world example — Denmark's butter (saturated-fat) tax

In 2011 Denmark introduced a tax per kg on saturated fat, ≈ 22% of the average butter price. Consumption of butter and related products fell 15–20%. But the tax was scrapped soon after because of the administrative burden of collecting it.

Section FPerfect Competition

The efficiency result of Section C is the pay-off of a specific idealised market structure, perfect competition. It pulls together every assumption used along the way.

The perfectly competitive modelA market is perfectly competitive when:
  • there are many buyers and sellers, all price-takers;
  • products are homogeneous (identical);
  • there is free entry and exit in the long run;
  • information is good and there are no external effects.
Under these conditions the market reaches P = MC in the short run and P = MC = AC in the long run, and the equilibrium is Pareto-efficient — it exhausts all gains from trade with no deadweight loss.
Listen for in the lecturePerfect competition is a benchmark, not a description of reality ("hard to find price-takers in real life"). Its value is as a yardstick: departures from it — market power (previous unit), taxes (Section E), and externalities (Unit 12) — are exactly the cases where the efficiency result breaks, and each creates a deadweight loss.
Check yourself
  • Which single assumption fails for a firm with market power, and why does that create deadweight loss?
  • In long-run equilibrium a competitive firm earns zero economic profit — is it therefore making an accounting loss? (Recall Unit 2: economic vs accounting profit.)

Wrap-upSummary, Formulas & Glossary

The unit in one paragraph

In a competitive market, firms are price-takers: each faces a horizontal demand at the market price and produces where P = MC (so its supply curve is its marginal-cost curve above AC). Summing firms gives the upward-sloping market supply. The competitive equilibrium, where supply = demand, maximises total surplus (CS + PS) with no deadweight loss — provided price-taking, complete contracts and no externalities hold. Shocks shift the curves; how price vs quantity responds depends on elasticities, and entry drives the long run to P = MC = AC (zero rent). A tax drives a wedge between buyer and seller prices, is independent of who remits it, transfers surplus to government, and creates a deadweight loss; its incidence falls more heavily on the less elastic side.

Exam-style questions to practise

  1. A firm has MC = 2 + 0.01Q. At a market price of €5, how much does it supply, and what is its producer surplus?
  2. Demand is Q = 100 − 2P, supply Q = −20 + 2P. Find the equilibrium price and quantity, and identify excess supply/demand at P = 35 and P = 25.
  3. A €4 specific tax is levied on a good. Explain why the outcome is identical whether buyers or sellers remit it.
  4. Using elasticities, explain who bears more of a tax on cigarettes (inelastic demand) versus a tax on a specific brand of soft drink (elastic demand).
  5. Draw the short-run and long-run adjustment to a permanent rise in demand for bread, and explain why the long-run price rise is smaller.
  6. Why does a competitive equilibrium maximise total surplus but not consumer surplus? Illustrate the deadweight loss created by a price ceiling.
