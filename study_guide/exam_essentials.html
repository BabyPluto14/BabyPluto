If you read one pageThe 12 things you must know

  1. Demand slopes down (= willingness to pay), supply slopes up (= marginal cost / willingness to accept). They cross at the equilibrium (P*, Q*).
  2. To find equilibrium: set quantity demanded = quantity supplied, solve for p, then plug back in for q.
  3. Consumer surplus = triangle under demand, above price. Producer surplus = triangle above supply, below price. Both are ½·base·height.
  4. Elasticity ε = (%ΔQ)/(%ΔP) in absolute value. ε>1 elastic, ε<1 inelastic. The slope coefficient is NOT the elasticity.
  5. Inelastic demand: raising price raises revenue. Elastic demand: raising price lowers revenue.
  6. A shift's split into price vs quantity depends on elasticity. More elastic demand → shift moves quantity a lot, price a little.
  7. A tax drives a wedge: buyer price rises, seller price falls, quantity falls. It doesn't matter who legally pays — same outcome.
  8. Tax incidence: the more INELASTIC side bears the bigger share. A tax creates a deadweight loss.
  9. Competitive (price-taking) firm: produce where P = MC. Its supply curve is its MC curve (above AC). Exit if P < AC.
  10. Long run under competition: entry drives price to P = MC = AC (zero economic profit).
  11. Firm with market power: MR < P. For linear demand, MR has the same intercept but is twice as steep. Produce where MR = MC, then read the price up on the demand curve (so P > MC).
  12. Markup rule: (P − MC)/P = 1/ε. More elastic demand → smaller markup. Market power → deadweight loss; competition removes it.

Core skill 1Solve for the Equilibrium

The method (works every time) Step 1. Write demand and supply as quantities: qD(p) and qS(p).
Step 2. Set qD = qS and solve for the price p*.
Step 3. Put p* back into either equation to get q*.
Worked exampleDemand q = 200 − 5p, supply q = −50 + 20p.
Set equal: 200 − 5p = −50 + 20p → 250 = 25p → p* = 10. Then q* = 200 − 5(10) = 150.
Exam trap"Normal slopes" means demand down, supply up. If a question says supply is perfectly inelastic it's a vertical line (fixed quantity); perfectly elastic is a horizontal line (fixed price). Excess supply (price too high) pushes price down; excess demand (price too low) pushes it up.

Consumer & producer surplus — the master picture

Surplus formulas (straight-line curves) CS = ½ × Q* × (demand choke price − P*)   (choke = the price where q = 0)
PS = ½ × Q* × (P* − supply intercept price)
Worked example (same numbers)Invert: demand p = 40 − q/5 (choke 40); supply p = 2.5 + q/20 (intercept 2.5). At q*=150, p*=10:
CS = ½·150·(40−10) = 2250.   PS = ½·150·(10−2.5) = 562.5.

The #1 elasticity trapIf demand is q = 200 − 5p, the 5 is NOT the elasticity — it's the slope coefficient (dq/dp = −5). Elasticity also multiplies by P/Q: at p=10, q=150, ε = 5·(10/150) = 1/3 (inelastic). Always compute P/Q.
Worked exampleUsed cars have elasticity 0.6 (inelastic). If price rises 10%, quantity falls only 0.6×10 = 6%. Since quantity falls less than price rose, total spending on used cars rises.

Shifts: elasticity decides price vs quantity

A cost fall (or good harvest) shifts supply right/down; a popularity change shifts demand. How the new equilibrium splits into a price change vs a quantity change depends on elasticity.

Classic MCQ"Price will fall by the same amount as costs." False — the price change depends on the elasticities of BOTH curves, almost never one-for-one. Also: a price change is a movement along a curve, not a shift of it.
Two-shock questions (both curves move)If supply shifts left and demand shifts left (e.g. fewer shrimp caught + less popular): quantity falls for certain, but the price change is ambiguous — it depends on the relative size of the two shifts.

Core skill 3Taxes — the Wedge

How to solve a specific (per-unit) tax t Let p = market price. If the seller remits the tax: seller keeps p − t, buyer pays p. Rewrite supply using (p − t), set new supply = demand, solve.
Buyer price − seller price = t always. Quantity falls.
Worked exampleSupply q = −80 + 20p, demand q = 130 − 10p. Pre-tax: p = 7, q = 60. A €3 seller tax → supply becomes q = −80 + 20(p−3) = −140 + 20p. New: −140 + 20p = 130 − 10p → p (buyer) = 9, seller price = 6, q = 40. Buyer price +2, seller price −1.
Incidence — who really pays (memorise) The more INELASTIC side of the market bears the LARGER share of the tax.
· Perfectly inelastic supply → sellers bear it all, consumer price unchanged.
· Perfectly elastic supply → buyers bear it all, market price rises by the full t.
· It makes no difference whether the buyer or the seller legally pays — identical prices & quantities. Subsidies = negative taxes.
Trap"The impact on the producer price is larger if supply is more elastic." False — more elastic supply means sellers bear less, so the producer price moves less.

Unit 8The Competitive (Price-Taking) Firm

Price-takerMany firms, identical product → each firm takes the market price as given and faces a horizontal demand at that price. It only chooses how much to produce.
The rules (know these cold) 1. Produce where P = MC (because for a price-taker, MR = P, and profit-max is MR = MC).
2. The firm's supply curve = its MC curve, for the part above AC.
3. Shut down / exit if P < AC. Producer surplus = area between price and MC.
4. Market supply = horizontal sum of all firms' MC curves.
5. Long run: entry drives price down to P = MC = AC → zero economic profit.
Efficiency (the big Unit 8 result)The competitive equilibrium maximises total surplus with no deadweight loss — provided price-taking, complete contracts and no externalities. This is what "perfect competition is efficient" means.
Traps· "MR = price" is only true for a price-taker. · "Producer surplus is zero in perfect competition" is false in general (only if supply is perfectly elastic / constant MC). · Strong economies of scale → markets tend to be more concentrated.

Unit 7The Firm With Market Power

Market powerA firm selling a differentiated product faces a downward-sloping demand curve and can set a price above marginal cost. To sell one more unit it must lower the price on all units → MR < P.
The two must-know facts Linear demand P = a − bQ ⟹ MR = a − 2bQ — same intercept, twice as steep.
Profit max: MR = MC. Find Q there, then read the price up on the demand curve (so P > MC).
Worked example (monopoly)Demand q = 200 − 5p → inverse p = 40 − q/5, so MR = 40 − 2q/5. MC = 2. Set MR = MC: 40 − 2q/5 = 2 → q = 95, p = 40 − 95/5 = 21. Consumer surplus = ½·(40−21)·95 = 902.5.
The markup rule (Lerner) (P − MC)/P = 1/ε — the price markup equals the inverse of the demand elasticity.
More elastic demand (bigger ε, more competition) → smaller markup, price closer to MC. As ε → ∞ you get perfect competition (P = MC, no DWL).
MC & AC (cost side, both units) AC = C(Q)/Q (slope of the ray from the origin); MC = cost of one more unit (slope of the total-cost curve). If MC < AC → AC falling; if MC > AC → AC rising. So MC cuts AC at the minimum of AC. Economies of scale = AC falls as output rises (spreading fixed costs; increasing returns).

Top exam traps (from past papers)
  • Slope coefficient ≠ elasticity (multiply by P/Q).
  • Who legally pays a tax doesn't change the outcome.
  • More elastic side of the market bears less of a tax.
  • A cost cut does not cut price one-for-one.
  • MR = P only for a price-taker; for market power MR < P.
  • Setting P > MC (market power) is not Pareto efficient (deadweight loss).
  • Economies of scale ≠ economies of scope (scale = one good, output↑; scope = two goods, shared input).
  • "Increasing returns / a >proportional output rise" = economies of scale, not diseconomies.
Two-minute self-test
  1. Demand q = 100 − 2p, supply q = −20 + 2p. Find p*, q*, CS, PS. (p*=30, q*=40; CS=½·40·(50−30)=400; PS=½·40·(30−10)=400.)
  2. A monopolist faces p = 60 − 2q, MC = 4. Find q, p. (MR = 60 − 4q = 4 → q = 14, p = 32.)
  3. A €4 tax; demand more inelastic than supply. Who bears more? (Buyers — the more inelastic side.)
