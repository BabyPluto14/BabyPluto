Where this unit sits

Previous units modelled choices that did not depend on other people's decisions. Adam Smith argued that self-interested individuals, guided by the "invisible hand", can produce outcomes good for society (entrepreneurship, innovation). But self-interest can also be harmful. This unit uses game theory to model interactions where my payoff depends on your action, and asks: why do these problems arise, and what can we do about them?

Definition — social dilemmaA social dilemma is a situation in which actions taken independently by self-interested individuals result in a socially sub-optimal outcome — e.g. traffic jams, climate change, COVID-19. Two classic forms:
  • Tragedy of the commons — common resources are over-exploited (Lloyd 1833, overgrazing; Hardin 1968). E.g. fishing, climate, a shared kitchen.
  • Free riding — benefiting from others' contributions to a cooperative project without contributing yourself.
The unit asks how altruism, repetition, and government policy can resolve them.

Section BGame Theory: Key Concepts

Definitions
  • Social interaction — a situation involving more than one party, where one's actions affect both their own and others' outcomes.
  • Strategic interaction — a social interaction where people are aware of how their actions affect others.
  • Strategy — an action a player can take in a social interaction.
Games are studied with game theory (used in social science and biology).
Definition — a gameA game describes a social interaction through five elements:
  1. Players — who is involved.
  2. Feasible strategies — the actions each player can take.
  3. Order of play — what each player knows when deciding.
  4. Information — what each knows when choosing an action.
  5. Payoffs — outcomes for every combination of actions.

Worked example — crop choice

Two farmers, Anil and Bala, each choose which crop to specialise in — Rice or Cassava. They interact only once (a one-shot game) and choose simultaneously (neither knows the other's choice). Payoffs depend on market prices and land quality.

Best response & Nash equilibrium

Definitions
  • Best response — the strategy that yields the highest payoff, given the other player's strategy.
  • Nash equilibrium — a set of strategies (one per player) such that each player's strategy is a best response to everyone else's. No player can gain by deviating unilaterally. (After John Nash, 1928–2015, Nobel 1994 — see A Beautiful Mind.)

Finding the Nash equilibrium of the crop game by checking best responses:

  • If Bala plays Rice, Anil's best response is Cassava (6 > 4).
  • If Bala plays Cassava, Anil's best response is Rice (6 > 5).
  • If Anil plays Rice, Bala's best response is Rice (4 > 3).
  • If Anil plays Cassava, Bala's best response is Rice (6 > 5).
Listen for in the lectureReading a bi-matrix: to find Anil's best response, fix a Bala column and compare the bottom-left numbers; to find Bala's, fix an Anil row and compare the top-right numbers. A cell is a Nash equilibrium when it is best for both at once. A game can have more than one Nash equilibrium — or none in pure strategies.

Section B · cont.Finding Nash Equilibria

A 3×3 example

Player 1 chooses a row (T/M/B); Player 2 a column (L/C/R). Find every best response:

  • If Player 2 plays L, Player 1's best is T (2 > 1 > 0).
  • If Player 2 plays C, Player 1's best is B (2 > 1 > 0).
  • If Player 2 plays R, Player 1's best is B (2 > 1 > 0).
  • Likewise for Player 2. Only in cell (B, R) are both strategies best responses to each other — no player can improve by unilaterally changing. That is the Nash equilibrium.

Dominant strategies & the prisoner's dilemma

Definitions
  • Dominant strategy — a strategy that is the best response regardless of what the other player does (does not always exist).
  • Dominant strategy equilibrium — a Nash equilibrium in which every player plays a dominant strategy.

Back to Anil and Bala, now deciding how to deal with pest insects: Toxic Tide (kills every insect but pollutes) vs Integrated Pest Control / IPC (less effective, no pollution).

Definition — prisoner's dilemmaA game with a dominant-strategy equilibrium in which playing the dominant strategy yields lower individual AND total payoffs than another (cooperative) outcome. Here both would be better off at (IPC, IPC) = (3, 3), but self-interest drives them to (Toxic, Toxic) = (2, 2). (Originally told as two burglars caught by police.)
Check yourself · exam MCQWhich is correct?
  • (a) "An equilibrium in dominant strategies is always a Nash equilibrium." — True. If each plays a best response regardless of the other, it is certainly a best response to what the other actually does.
  • (b) "The prisoner's dilemma outcome is never a Nash equilibrium, as all players can improve by changing strategies." — False. It is a Nash equilibrium; no one can improve by changing unilaterally (both must change together to reach the better outcome).
  • (c) "A strategy combination that maximises the sum of payoffs is always a Nash equilibrium." — False. The efficient (IPC, IPC) maximises the total but is not a Nash equilibrium.
Answer: (a).

Section CEvaluating Outcomes — the Pareto Criterion

Definitions — Pareto
  • Allocation A Pareto-dominates B if at least one party is better off under A and nobody is worse off.
  • An allocation is Pareto-efficient if nobody can be made better off without making somebody worse off.
Often many outcomes are Pareto-efficient — e.g. every way of splitting €1000 between two people is efficient as long as no money is left on the table.

Caveats of the Pareto criterion

Two important limits
  • It often can't choose. With many efficient allocations, the Pareto criterion is silent about which to pick. It may not even rank an efficient against an inefficient one — e.g. (T, I) is efficient but does not Pareto-dominate the inefficient (T, T).
  • It says nothing about fairness. Many unfair allocations are Pareto-efficient — e.g. keeping €99.99 of a found €100 and giving your friend 1 cent is efficient (no money wasted) yet hardly fair.
Listen for in the lecturePareto efficiency is a low bar: "no waste", not "good" or "fair". The Nash equilibrium of a prisoner's dilemma is Pareto-inefficient (both could do better) — which is exactly why we look for ways to escape it in Section D.

Section DResolving Social Dilemmas

The prisoner's-dilemma outcome arose for three reasons. Each suggests a route out:

Three routes out of a social dilemma
  1. Players only cared about their own payoffs → introduce social preferences / altruism.
  2. Nobody could make players pay for the consequences of their actions on others → introduce repeated games, social norms, peer punishment.
  3. Players could not coordinate beforehand → change the rules of the game (institutions and policies).

D.i — Social preferences: altruism

Social/prisoner's dilemmas arise when players care only about their own payoffs. But in experiments many players show altruism — they choose the dominated (cooperative) strategy. If altruism is strong enough, the socially desirable outcome can be reached (in Golden Balls, (split, split) is common; likewise in public-good games).

How altruistic are we? The public-good game

Public-good game (prisoner's dilemma for n players)Each of n players gets T tokens and chooses how many to keep (zi) vs contribute to a common pool (gi = T − zi). The payoff is: payoffi = zi + βn(g1 + g2 + ⋯ + gn)
  • If 1 > β/n, a purely self-interested player keeps all tokens (private return on a contributed token, β/n, is below 1).
  • If β > 1, it is socially optimal to contribute everything (each token multiplied by β > 1 across the group).
  • For 1 > β/n and β > 1 together, the game is a social/prisoner's dilemma.

Experimental finding: in public-good games worldwide (β = 1.6, n = 4, 20 tokens each), people start by contributing a lot (≈ 8–14 of 20), far above the self-interested prediction of zero — clear evidence of altruism/cooperation. But without punishment, contributions decline period by period as cooperators get discouraged by free-riders.

Modelling altruistic preferences

Represent preferences with indifference curves over the two players' payoffs and combine with the feasible set (as in Unit 3). Example: Zoë wins £200 in a lottery — will she keep it all, or share with her flatmate Yvonne?

Section D · cont.Repeated Games & Punishment

So far, one-shot games. Better outcomes can arise in repeated interactions through social norms, reciprocity, and peer punishment: behaving selfishly today has consequences in future periods, so it may no longer be a dominant strategy.

Worked example — the cartel's dilemma (two petrol stations)

Jupiter and Saturn each choose to Maintain a high price or Cut it. In a one-shot game, cutting is a dominant strategy for both → both cut (the cartel's dilemma).

Trigger strategy in the repeated gameWhen the game repeats, a firm can follow a "trigger" strategy: start with the high price, and keep it as long as the rival does too; if the rival cuts, retaliate with a low price for a while, then return to the high price. This creates a trade-off: high profit today from cutting vs high profits in the future from maintaining. Cooperation (Maintain, Maintain) can be an equilibrium if players value future money highly enough — because punishment becomes possible.

Punishment sustains cooperation — the evidence

In the public-good game, contributions normally decline over the rounds. But when players are allowed to punish free-riders (at a cost to themselves), contributions become much higher and stay high — peer punishment sustains cooperation. The willingness to punish, even at personal cost, is itself a social preference.

Listen for in the lectureThe key insight: repetition turns a one-shot prisoner's dilemma into a game where defection can be punished later, so the dominant strategy to defect disappears. This is how social norms, reputation and reciprocity support cooperation in real life without any government.

Section D · cont.Rules of the Game — Sequential Games

The third route: change the rules. One important rule is the order of play. When players move in sequence (observing earlier choices), the game is drawn in extensive form (a game tree).

Definitions
  • Extensive form (game tree) — nodes are points where a player picks a move; branches are the possible choices.
  • Backward induction — solve by first finding the optimal choice at the final nodes, then reasoning backward to the first node.

Worked example — two TV stations

Channels Z and Delta choose a time slot (7:30 or 8:00 pm) for the evening news. Z chooses first, and Delta observes Z's choice before deciding.

The ultimatum game

Definition — ultimatum gameA sequential game for dividing an economic rent (e.g. a cash prize). The Proposer offers a split; the Responder observes it and accepts (both get the proposed amounts) or rejects (both get nothing).
Why real offers are generousThe Proposer's offer may be motivated by altruism, fairness (50–50), inequality aversion, social norms, or reciprocity. Responders routinely reject low offers — punishing unfairness at a cost to themselves. Evidence (Kenyan farmers vs US students): offers are consistent with social preferences but also with expected-payoff maximisation (proposers offer more where low offers are more likely to be rejected). And the rules matter: introducing competition between responders (two responders vying to accept) pushes outcomes back toward the self-interested prediction.

Section ECoordination — Multiple Equilibria

Not every social dilemma is a prisoner's dilemma. Some games have several Nash equilibria, and the problem is coordinating on one of them.

Driving on the left or the right

Two drivers approach each other; each chooses to drive on the Left or the Right. If they choose the same side, all is well (payoff 0); if they mismatch, they crash (−10 each).

Coordination games & institutionsWith multiple equilibria, no player has an incentive to deviate from either, so the outcome is indeterminate without coordination. Institutions and conventions select the equilibrium: a traffic law (drive on the right), or a coordinated switch. Sweden's "Dagen H" (3 Sept 1967) — the whole country switched from left to right overnight — is a real example of institutions moving everyone from one equilibrium to another.

Climate change as a game

For purely self-interested players, "business as usual" (BAU) is a dominant strategy (a prisoner's dilemma), and an emissions treaty would deliver the socially optimal outcome. But if players have inequality aversion and reciprocity, the game can become a coordination game with two Nash equilibria, one of which is the social optimum (both restrict).

Listen for in the lectureThe type of game depends on preferences: pure self-interest → prisoner's dilemma (one bad equilibrium); social preferences → coordination game (a good equilibrium becomes available). The lecturer's point: changing preferences or rules changes the game itself, and institutions exist to steer us toward the good equilibrium (cf. the Dvorak keyboard, another coordination trap).

Wrap-upSummary, Formulas & Glossary

The unit in one paragraph

Social interactions are modelled as games (players, strategies, order, information, payoffs). Players choose best responses, and a Nash equilibrium is a profile where everyone is best-responding — nobody gains by deviating alone. A dominant strategy is best whatever others do; when a dominant-strategy equilibrium leaves everyone worse off than a cooperative outcome, it is a prisoner's dilemma — a Pareto-inefficient Nash equilibrium. The Pareto criterion only flags "no waste" (not fairness) and often can't rank outcomes. Social dilemmas can be escaped through social preferences/altruism, repetition with punishment (trigger strategies, reputation), or changing the rules (sequential order, institutions). Some games instead have multiple equilibria, where the challenge is coordination, and institutions select the good equilibrium.

Exam-style questions to practise

  1. Define a Nash equilibrium. In the crop game, verify that (Cassava, Rice) is Nash by checking both players' best responses.
  2. Why is the prisoner's-dilemma outcome a Nash equilibrium and Pareto-inefficient? Reconcile these with a 2×2 matrix.
  3. In a public-good game with β = 1.6 and n = 4, show that a self-interested player contributes zero but full contribution is socially optimal.
  4. Explain how a trigger strategy can sustain the (Maintain, Maintain) outcome in the petrol-station game. What condition on the value of future profits is needed?
  5. Solve the two-TV-station game by backward induction. Why does moving first benefit Channel Z?
  6. Give the two Nash equilibria of the driving game. Why can't the Pareto criterion pick between them, and what selects the outcome in practice?
