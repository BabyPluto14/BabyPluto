Where this unit sits

The previous units showed that a perfectly competitive market generates an efficient (Pareto-optimal) outcome. But that result relies on strong conditions: property rights must be well defined, institutions must be able to enforce them, and they must be written into complete, enforceable contracts. Often these conditions fail — and the market delivers an inefficient outcome. This unit catalogues the main market failures and the tools to fix them.

Section AIntroduction

In perfectly competitive markets the market generates an efficient outcome — but only if:

  • property rights are well defined,
  • institutions can enforce those property rights, and
  • they can be written into complete and enforceable contracts.

Often these conditions are not satisfied — most importantly because of external effects (externalities) and asymmetric information.

Motivating example — the pesticide chlordecone

The banana weevil caused large losses on banana plantations. The pesticide chlordecone was highly effective against it — but hazardous to humans and the ecosystem (known since the 1970s). It was used heavily on Caribbean plantations in Guadeloupe and Martinique until 1993:

  • The chemical washed off the land into rivers, contaminating freshwater prawn farms and lobster fisheries.
  • By 2012, Martiniquean men had the highest rate of prostate cancer in the world; neurological damage in children was documented.
The core ideaThe banana plant owners did not take into account the costs they imposed on fishermen (and on health). A cost (or benefit) that one party's decision imposes on another, but that the decision-maker ignores, is an externality — and it is a source of market failure.

Section BExternal Effects

Definition — externalityAn external effect (externality) arises when one party directly conveys a cost or benefit to another party that is not reflected in market prices.
  • a cost imposed on others → negative externality (e.g. factory emissions);
  • a benefit conferred on others → positive externality (e.g. a store's advertising helping neighbours).
Why externalities break efficiencyEconomic efficiency requires marginal social benefit = marginal social cost. But an individual firm or consumer only considers its own (private) costs and benefits. With externalities, marginal social cost/benefit differs from marginal private cost/benefit — so the private optimum is not the efficient one. Maximum efficiency is not achieved.

Negative externality — bananas and fisheries

Abstracting from the health issues, growing bananas imposes a cost on fisheries (lower catch). Three cost concepts:

Three marginal costs
  • Marginal private cost (MPC) — the plantation's own marginal cost of growing bananas.
  • Marginal external cost (MEC) — the extra cost imposed on fishermen per ton of bananas.
  • Marginal social cost (MSC = MPC + MEC) — the full cost to society.

The private outcome is not efficient

The plantation produces where its marginal benefit (= the price of bananas) equals its marginal PRIVATE cost — point A at 80,000 tons. Society, however, should produce where marginal benefit equals marginal SOCIAL cost — point B.

Worked example — a Pareto improvement existsAt 80,000 tons the marginal external cost is $275. Fishermen would pay up to $275 to cut banana output by one ton. Cutting production by one ton lowers the plantation's revenue by $400 (the price) but also lowers its cost by nearly $400 (MPC ≈ price at A), so the plantation would accept any compensation above roughly zero to cut back. Since fishermen will pay up to $275 and the plantation needs almost nothing, both can be made better off — the hallmark of an inefficient starting point.

Positive external effects

Actions can also confer benefits on others: a positive externality where the marginal social benefit exceeds the marginal private benefit. Example: a large store (e.g. FNAC) opening in a shopping street also benefits other shops selling non-competing products. Positive externalities lead to under-provision (too little of the good activity), the mirror image of the over-production under negative externalities.

Listen for in the lectureThe whole section rests on one move: replace the private cost/benefit the decision-maker sees with the social one. Negative externality → private cost too low → too much produced. Positive externality → private benefit too low → too little. Every policy below is a way to make the decision-maker face the social figure.

Section B · solutionsFixing Externalities

Solution #1 — Bargaining (the Coase idea)

Coase theoremLegally assign property rights over the externality (e.g. the right to pollute, or the right to clean water). Then private bargaining between the parties will reach a Pareto-efficient allocation regardless of which party holds the rights — in the absence of transaction costs. Private bargaining can be more effective than government intervention because the parties have more of the relevant information.

But transaction costs — the costs of acquiring information, enforcing the contract, or organising collective action — can be a major obstacle in reality.

Practical limits of bargaining
  • Collective action — finding a representative and agreeing how to split the gains within each side.
  • Missing information — calculating each fisherman's exact loss and each plantation's contribution to pollution.
  • Enforcement — a court may struggle to verify whether plantations complied.
  • Limited funds — fishermen may not have the money to pay the required compensation.

Solution #2 — Government intervention

Three instruments, each with different distributional consequences (and each with limits: missing information / measuring social costs, and lobbying).

(a) Regulation

The government caps banana production at the Pareto-efficient level (38,000 tons). Plantation profits fall (lower volume), but there is no tax bill.

(b) Pigouvian taxes / subsidies

Definition — Pigouvian tax/subsidyA tax on firms generating a negative externality (or a subsidy for a positive one), set equal to the marginal external cost at the Pareto-efficient level. It forces producers to face the full social cost of their decisions, so their private optimum coincides with the social optimum.

(c) Compensation

Why do external costs occur? Incomplete contracts

Root causeExternal costs cause market failure because of incomplete contracts — contracts that do not specify, in an enforceable way, every aspect of the exchange affecting all parties. Contracts that would internalise external costs are unenforceable because the relevant information is not verifiable or is asymmetric (not known to the decision-maker). So in reality it is impossible to use contracts or property rights to fold all social costs into the decision.
Real-world twist — Disney's secret land purchasesDisney bought the land around its Anaheim park secretly (and in Orlando via multiple shell companies / agents; in Hong Kong via an option on adjoining real estate). Why secretly? Because a park generates a huge positive externality on surrounding land values. If sellers knew Disney was buying, they would hold out for the higher post-park price — so Disney bought quietly to internalise the externality (capture the land-value gain) itself.

Section COther Types of Market Failure

Besides externalities, markets fail to reach a Pareto-efficient outcome for three more reasons: public goods, asymmetric information, and market power.

Public goods

Goods are classified along two dimensions — whether they are rival (my use reduces yours) and whether they are excludable (I can stop non-payers using them).

Definitions
  • Non-rival — use by one person does not reduce its availability to others (a flood-control dam, street lighting, national defence, a TV broadcast).
  • Non-excludable — it is impossible to exclude anyone from access (fish in the open ocean, flood protection).
  • A public good is a non-rival good (which may or may not be excludable).

Why markets fail for public goodsMarkets allocate private goods well, but tend to fail for the other three types:
  • Non-rival goods have a marginal cost of zero, so pricing at P = MC is impossible unless provision is subsidised or there is market power.
  • Non-excludable goods can't be priced at all — the provider cannot exclude non-payers (the free-rider problem). Example: if one farmer builds a community irrigation project, others enjoy the external benefit for free, so it is hard to make them pay or to write a contract guaranteeing the efficient level.

Asymmetric information

DefinitionInformation is asymmetric when one party knows something relevant to the transaction that the other does not. Two forms:
  • Hidden action → a moral hazard problem (an action one party takes that the other can't observe). Example: insured people take more risks.
  • Hidden attributes → an adverse selection problem (a characteristic one party can't observe). Example: used-car buyers don't know the car's quality, but sellers do.
Adverse selection — health insuranceInsurance is voluntary and the insurer can't observe buyers' health; buyers know theirs, and the less healthy are keener to buy. To break even the insurer must charge a high price, so only the less healthy buy — most buyers already have a problem. The result is a missing market: many healthier people who would like insurance stay uninsured.
Moral hazard — car insuranceAny insurance also has a hidden-action problem: once covered, the buyer takes more risk (full damage cover → more careless driving). Insurers can write some contractual limits but cannot enforce others (e.g. your driving speed), so the behaviour can't be fully controlled.

Market power — price above marginal cost

Firms with market power set price above marginal cost. This is a market failure because the allocation is not Pareto-efficient (some consumers who value the good above its marginal cost don't buy it). Policies try to limit market power — but with two cautions:

  • Incentives for innovation — some markup can be the reward that funds R&D.
  • Market power arising solely from product differentiation may be fine — people value having different choices.

Section DLimits to Markets

Everything so far asked when markets fail to be efficient. A different, deeper question: even where a market would function, should it allocate the good at all?

Arguments against using markets for everything
  • Repugnant markets — creating a market for some goods/services would violate ethical or social norms (e.g. a market in people — slavery).
  • Other institutions may be more effective — governments, families.
  • Crowding out — market mechanisms can crowd out norms and social preferences (paying for something can destroy the intrinsic motivation to do it).
  • Merit goods — goods that should be available to everyone regardless of ability to pay (e.g. education, basic health care).
Listen for in the lectureThis section is normative, not just positive: it's about values, not only efficiency. A market can be perfectly efficient and still be the wrong way to allocate something. Keep the two questions separate in the exam — "is it efficient?" vs "should it be a market at all?"

Wrap-upSummary, Formulas & Glossary

The unit in one paragraph

Competitive markets are efficient only when property rights are complete and enforceable. They fail through externalities (decisions impose uncosted effects on others — MSC ≠ MPC, so the private optimum over- or under-produces), public goods (non-rival goods have zero marginal cost; non-excludable goods invite free-riding), asymmetric information (hidden actions → moral hazard; hidden attributes → adverse selection and missing markets), and market power (P > MC). Externalities can be tackled by bargaining (Coase — efficient regardless of who holds the rights, absent transaction costs), or by government regulation, Pigouvian taxes/subsidies, or compensation — instruments that reach the same efficient quantity but differ in who bears the cost. Finally, some goods arguably should not be allocated by markets at all (repugnant markets, crowding out, merit goods).

Exam-style questions to practise

  1. A factory's MPC = 10 + 0.5Q and it imposes MEC = 0.5Q on neighbours; the price is 40. Find the private and the socially efficient output, and the size of the deadweight loss.
  2. Explain the Coase theorem. Why does the efficient outcome not depend on whether the polluter or the victim holds the property right? When does it break down?
  3. Compare regulation, a Pigouvian tax and compensation for the banana example: which reaches the efficient quantity, and how do they differ in distribution?
  4. Classify each as rival/excludable: a lighthouse, a congested motorway, a patented drug, an ocean fishery. Which are public goods, and why might markets under-provide them?
  5. Distinguish moral hazard from adverse selection with an insurance example of each. How does adverse selection produce a "missing market"?
  6. Give two goods you think should not be allocated by markets, and justify using the "limits to markets" arguments.
