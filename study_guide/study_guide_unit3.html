Section AIntroduction

Since 1870, living standards have risen enormously in most countries. But the gains have not all been taken as extra income: there are large disparities in free time and income across countries. Some rich countries work long hours; others have converted much of their productivity gains into shorter working weeks and longer holidays.

Listen for in the lectureThe framing question for the whole unit: a new technology raises the productivity of labour — how does that affect (i) living standards and (ii) the free time and working hours people choose? Keep both halves in mind; the answer turns out to hinge on two opposing forces (income and substitution effects).

Section BScarcity & Choice: Key Concepts

Individuals want to consume as much as possible and have as much free time as possible. But a day has only a limited number of hours — this is scarcity. Increasing consumption means sacrificing free time (you must work to earn). So we need a model of how a person picks the best combination of the two.

Two ingredients of the model of constrained choice
  • Preferences — how the individual ranks combinations of consumption and free time.
  • The trade-off (constraint) — the feasible combinations of consumption and free time, i.e. how much consumption an hour of free time costs.

Simplifying assumptions

  • Individuals don't care about the future, so there are no savings.
  • Individuals cannot borrow.
  • Therefore income = consumption (whatever you earn, you spend now).
Why make "unrealistic" assumptions?Recall from Unit 2: a model deliberately omits detail to expose the essential mechanism. Ruling out saving/borrowing lets us focus cleanly on the one trade-off that matters here — free time vs consumption.

Preferences and utility

People like both free time and consumption, and can rank the many possible combinations.

Definition — utilityUtility is a numerical indicator of the value a person places on an outcome. Higher-utility combinations are preferred to (chosen over) lower-utility ones.

Indifference curves

Definition — indifference curveAn indifference curve shows all combinations of two goods that give the same utility (the person is "indifferent" between them).

Four properties you must know and be able to justify:

The four properties
  1. Downward-sloping — because more of a good is better.
  2. Higher curves = higher utility — they contain bundles with more of both goods.
  3. They never cross (see below).
  4. They get flatter to the right — a diminishing marginal rate of substitution (convex to the origin).

Property 3 — why indifference curves cannot cross

Property 4 — the marginal rate of substitution (MRS)

Definition — marginal rate of substitutionThe MRS is the amount of one good a person is willing to give up to get one more unit of the other, holding utility constant. Graphically it is the (magnitude of the) slope of the indifference curve at a point. It diminishes as you move right: the more you already have of a good, the less of the other you'll sacrifice for still more of it.
Listen for in the lectureThe MRS is a subjective willingness to trade, read off preferences. Later it will be set equal to the MRT (an objective trade-off read off the constraint) to find the optimum. Keep the two straight: MRS = preferences, MRT = constraint.

Section B · applicationPutting a Price on the Priceless

The MRS is powerful because it lets us value things that have no market price — marriage, children, health. There's no market, so no observed monetary value, and asking "how much would you pay for…?" directly is unreliable. The trick is indirect measurement via happiness.

  • Ask people "How satisfied are you with your life?" and relate the answer to observable characteristics (income, marital status, health, employment…).
  • The MRS between, say, marriage and income then reveals the willingness to pay for marriage — how much extra income would compensate a single person for being unmarried, at constant happiness.

Section B · the constraintThe Feasible Frontier & the MRT

Preferences tell us what the person wants; now we need what is possible. The trade-off between consumption and free time is drawn as the feasible frontier.

Definitions
  • Feasible frontier — the combinations of free time and consumption that can just be reached (work all available hours → max consumption, no free time; work none → max free time, no consumption).
  • Feasible set — the frontier plus everything inside it (all attainable combinations).
Definition — marginal rate of transformationThe slope of the feasible frontier is the opportunity cost of free time — the consumption you forgo for one more hour off. This is the marginal rate of transformation (MRT): the rate at which free time can be transformed into consumption (i.e. the wage).
Check yourself
  • Why must indifference curves be convex (flatten to the right)? What would a concave one imply about willingness to trade?
  • The MRS and MRT are both "slopes" — whose slope is each, and what does each measure (willingness vs ability to trade)?

Section CDecision-Making Under Scarcity

Now combine the two halves. Which feasible combination will the person choose? This is a constrained choice problem: pursue an objective subject to a constraint.

The constrained optimisation
  • Objective: maximise utility.
  • Constraint: the feasible frontier.
The optimal choice is the combination lying on an indifference curve as far from the origin as possible while still being feasible.
Optimality condition — MRS = MRTThe utility-maximising choice is where the indifference curve is tangent to the feasible frontier. At that point the rate at which the person is willing to trade the goods (MRS) equals the rate at which they can trade them (MRT): MRS = MRT

Why the tangency is best — the intuition

At a non-tangency point the two rates differ, and you can always improve. Consider a point D where the indifference curve is steeper than the frontier (MRS > MRT):

Listen for in the lectureThe logic is completely general: whenever MRS ≠ MRT there is an unexploited gain, so only the tangency can be optimal. If MRS > MRT, take more free time; if MRS < MRT, work more. This is the same "equate the margins" idea you'll meet again and again in microeconomics.

Section DIncome & Substitution Effects

Set-up — the summer-job example

You are planning your 10-week summer break. You can work at $90 per day, but you also value time for friends, holiday and study. How many days should you work?

Your feasible frontier has slope −$90/day (the wage = the MRT = the opportunity cost of a day off). The optimum is the tangency A.

A pure increase in income (a gift)

Your mother gives you $1000, no strings attached. This does not change the wage, so the frontier shifts up in parallel by $1000 — the opportunity cost of free time is unchanged. You re-optimise at a new tangency.

Key takeawayA rise in unearned income (no change in the wage) has only an income effect: more of everything you value, including free time. We expect free time to rise or stay equal — never fall.

Section D · cont.A Wage Rise: Two Opposing Effects

Now a genuinely different job pays $130 per day. Unlike the gift, a higher wage does two things at once, and they pull free time in opposite directions:

The two effects of a wage increase
  • Income effect — at your old hours you now earn more, so you can afford more of everything, including more free time (free time is normal). Pushes free time up.
  • Substitution effect — free time is now more expensive (each hour off costs $130 rather than $90 of consumption), so you substitute away from it. Pushes free time down.

We separate them with a hypothetical frontier: a line with the old slope (−$90) drawn tangent to the new, higher indifference curve.

The overall effect Overall effect = Income effect + Substitution effect Because the two effects have opposite signs for free time, the net direction depends on which is larger — and that depends on individual preferences. Free time (and hence hours worked) can rise or fall when the wage rises.
Listen for in the lectureThe construction is fiddly the first time. Anchor it on three points: A (old optimum), D (new optimum, on the true new frontier), and C (the hypothetical point — same slope as old, same indifference curve as new). Income effect = A→C, substitution = C→D. The consumption axis reveals the puzzle's resolution; the free-time axis reveals its sign.

Section EApplication to Technological Change

Differences over time

Now use the model to explain the historical fall in working hours. Take a US worker in 1900 at point A. Over the century, technological progress raised the wage, steepening the budget constraint. Decompose exactly as in Figure 13:

  • Income effect: higher wage → afford more → free time and consumption both rise (A → C).
  • Substitution effect: free time dearer → free time falls, consumption rises (C → D).
  • Net: historically the income effect dominated, so free time increased — the working week and working year got shorter as countries got richer.
This is exactly Figure 13The "technological change over time" diagram is the wage-rise decomposition, with the income effect winning. That is the model's explanation for the long-run decline in hours worked in rich economies.

Cross-country differences

Why do equally rich countries choose such different hours (Figure 1)? The model's baseline answer is differences in preferences. But preferences are shaped by more than taste:

  • Culture / norms — differing attitudes to work and leisure.
  • Politics — legal limits on working hours, mandated holidays.
  • Social preferences — e.g. "keeping up with the Joneses": if status depends on relative consumption, people over-work.

Is this a good model?

WeaknessesNot literally realistic: people don't compute MRS/MRT, and most cannot freely choose their hours.
Why it still worksA good approximation: over time people learn the mix that suits them; hours shift through culture and politics (indirect choice); and people choose which jobs to apply for. It explains real patterns — hours across countries and over time — via preferences and income/substitution effects.

Wrap-upSummary, Formulas & Glossary

The unit in one paragraph

A person chooses between consumption and free time under scarcity. Preferences are drawn as convex, non-crossing indifference curves, whose slope is the MRS (willingness to trade). The feasible frontier is the constraint, whose slope is the MRT = the wage = the opportunity cost of free time. The best feasible bundle is the tangency where MRS = MRT. A pure income gain shifts the frontier out in parallel (only an income effect: free time rises or stays equal). A wage rise rotates the frontier and triggers two opposing forces — an income effect (free time ↑) and a substitution effect (free time ↓) — whose net depends on preferences. Historically the income effect has dominated, so richer societies work fewer hours.
