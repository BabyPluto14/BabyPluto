The one-sentence version

Classical economics assumes we choose rationally and always benefit from more options. Real psychology shows we make systematic, predictable errors (behavioral economics), and that beyond a point more choice actively harms well-being through paralysis and discontent (the paradox of choice).

Part One

1.Introduction: the world of choice

Before any theory, the chapter starts with you. The point of the opening is to make an invisible thing visible: choice is so constant that we stop noticing it, and we almost never stop to ask whether we are any good at it.

1.1  Your choices

Take a moment and actually answer these — the exercise only works if you make it concrete rather than abstract:

  • Which choices did you make today? Not just the big ones. What to wear, whether to snooze the alarm, coffee or tea, which route to take, which message to answer first, whether to read this now or later.
  • How many options did you actually have for each one? Often far more than you consciously considered.
  • How did you make them? Did you weigh things up, or did you default to habit, to what you did last time, to what was easiest?
  • Was it easy or difficult? Notice which choices felt effortless and which drained you.
  • Are you happy with the result? And the deeper version: are you satisfied with the choices you have made across your life — study, relationships, career?
Why we start here

Two patterns usually surface. First, the sheer volume of daily choices is far larger than we assume — a modern adult makes thousands of small decisions a day. Second, most of those decisions are made automatically, not through the careful reasoning we like to imagine. Holding those two facts together is the whole reason this chapter exists: if choosing is this frequent and this automatic, the quality of our choices matters enormously — and it is not guaranteed.

1.2  Choice is ubiquitous

Choices sit on a spectrum of weight:

  • Small choices — which cereal, which of 40 shampoos, which show to stream, which of dozens of near-identical products on a shelf. Individually trivial, but they arrive relentlessly and in enormous variety.
  • Great choices — what to study, which career to pursue, where to live, whether and whom to marry, how to invest for retirement, medical decisions. Rare, high-stakes, and often irreversible.

The key observation is that modern life has multiplied both kinds. Domains that once came with a default answer — one pension, one phone company, one local doctor, a marriage arranged or strongly expected — have all been converted into open menus. This expansion is usually sold to us as pure progress. Part of this chapter is asking whether that is entirely true.

1.3  Why choice matters

Choice is not just a convenience; in Western thought it is treated as close to a moral good. The argument runs on two levels:

  • Individual well-being. Choice is the vehicle of autonomy. Being able to select what fits your goals, tastes and values is what lets you author your own life rather than have it dictated to you. Freedom of choice is tied to dignity, agency and self-determination.
  • Well-being of society. A market economy and a democracy both run on choice — consumers choosing between products, citizens choosing between ideas and representatives. The assumption is that free choice channels resources to what people actually want, producing prosperity and welfare.
“Freedom is the right to choose: the right to create for oneself the alternatives of choice. Without the possibility of choice, and the exercise of choice, a man is not a man but a member, an instrument, a thing.” — Thomas Jefferson

Notice how strong Jefferson's claim is: without choice you are not fully a person. That is the cultural water we swim in — the near-automatic belief that more choice is always better. This chapter does not reject that belief so much as test it.

The two questions that drive the whole chapter

(1) Can we actually choose well?   (2) Do we really benefit from having so much choice?   The rest of the chapter shows that the honest answers are “not as well as we think” and “not always” — and explains exactly why.

Part Two

2.How do we choose?

2.1  Rational choice theory — the “Homo economicus” ideal

The classical economic answer to “how do we choose?” is elegant and optimistic. It imagines a decision-maker nicknamed Homo economicus — “economic man” — who chooses through a clean, logical procedure:

  1. Identify your goals. Know what you are actually trying to achieve.
  2. Evaluate the importance of each goal. Decide how much each one matters — the weights.
  3. Rank the options against those weighted goals.
  4. Choose the best — the option that scores highest.
  5. Evaluate in light of future goals. Check the choice still serves you over time, and learn from it.

This procedure only works if a set of strong assumptions holds true:

  • Complete information — you know all the options and all their relevant features.
  • Perfect logic — you reason without error and your preferences are stable and consistent.
  • Cost–benefit analysis — you can weigh each option's costs against its benefits and compare them on a common scale.
  • Stable, self-known preferences — you know what you want, independent of how the options happen to be presented.

The comforting conclusion of this model is baked in: because the chooser is rational and fully informed, choice necessarily benefits us. Give people more options and a rational agent can only end up equally well off or better — never worse. This is exactly why classical economics treats more choice as an unqualified good.

2.2  “But…” — where the model breaks

Keep the five rational steps in mind, because the rest of the chapter is essentially a catalogue of the ways real humans fail to execute them. Two research traditions supply the critique:

The two-pronged critique

1 · Behavioral economics (Dan Ariely). We are not irrational in a random, hopeless way — we are irrational in orderly, repeatable ways. We make the same mistakes again and again, which means they can be studied, predicted, and even exploited. Ariely's phrase for this is “predictably irrational.”

2 · The paradox of choice (Barry Schwartz). Even setting aside our reasoning errors, the sheer quantity of choice carries its own costs. Too many options produce two negative outcomes: paralysis (we can't decide at all) and discontent (once we do decide, we're less satisfied with what we chose).

So the plan is set. Part 3 dismantles the “we choose well” assumption. Part 4 dismantles the “more choice is better” assumption. Together they overturn the tidy picture of Homo economicus.

Part Three

3.Behavioral economics: predictable mistakes

3.0  Introduction

The central claim of behavioral economics is worth stating precisely: we are not that rational, but our irrationality is systematic. If our errors were random noise, they would cancel out on average and we could ignore them. Instead they lean consistently in the same direction — which is what makes them both scientifically interesting and commercially valuable. Marketers, menu designers and negotiators all use these effects on us, whether they know the theory or not.

This section examines four of the most important error patterns:

3.1 Relativity 3.2 Anchoring 3.3 Emotions 3.4 Diversification

For each one, notice the recurring structure the slides use: (A) the intuition, (B) how it contradicts a specific step of rational choice theory, (C) the experiment that demonstrates it, and (D/E) the real-world applications and nuances.

3.1  Relativity

A · The intuition

We are very poor at judging things in absolute terms, and very good at judging them relative to whatever sits next to them. The classic demonstration is the Ebbinghaus illusion: two centre circles of identical size look different — one surrounded by large circles seems small, one surrounded by small circles seems large. Your eyes are not measuring the circle; they are comparing it to its neighbours. The mind does the same thing with prices, salaries, partners and life choices.

B · Which rational step it breaks — Step 1

Rational choice theory assumes you already know your goals and the value of each option, fixed in advance. Relativity says the opposite: we don't know values on their own — only in context, relative to other things. Change the comparison set and the “value” of the very same option changes. That undermines Step 1 (knowing your goals/values) before you even begin.

Sample research · the “decoy” effect

Ariely's famous example (the Economist subscription, echoed by the “trip to Rome” and dating experiments): when people choose between options, adding a deliberately inferior third option that is similar to one of the others makes that similar option look better and shifts choices toward it — even though a rational agent should ignore an option nobody wants.

Trip example
Rome-with-free-breakfast vs. Paris-with-free-breakfast vs. Rome-without breakfast. The “decoy” (Rome without breakfast) makes Rome-with-breakfast look like a bargain, pulling choices to Rome.
Dating example
Bring a slightly less attractive version of yourself and you look better by comparison. We evaluate a person relative to the nearest similar person, not on an absolute scale.

Take-away: our preferences can be engineered simply by controlling what each option is compared against.

C · Relativity and money: why more rarely feels like enough

Relativity explains a well-documented finding: beyond a modest level, more money does not make people much happier (Clark, Frijters & Shields, 2008). The reason is comparison. We don't judge our salary in absolute terms; we judge it against a reference group — colleagues, neighbours, friends. When your income rises, your reference group tends to rise with it, so the comparison resets and the extra money stops delivering satisfaction. You are on a treadmill: running to stay in the same emotional place.

D · Nuance: not everything is relative

Crucially, relativity does not apply equally to all goods (Solnick & Hemenway, 2005). Some things we care about mainly in relative terms — we want more than the people around us. Other things we care about in absolute terms — we want a lot of them regardless of what anyone else has.

Solnick & Hemenway asked people whether they would rather earn $50k while others earn $25k, or $100k while others earn $200k. For income and status, many people chose to be absolutely poorer but relatively richer. For things like health and vacation time, they chose the absolute maximum regardless of others. The lesson: the goods we chase hardest for status (money, car, house) are exactly the ones where relativity poisons our satisfaction — while the things that reliably matter (health, safety, relationships) are the ones we treat absolutely.

E · Everyday examples

  • The premium decoy. A coffee machine priced at $99.99 sits next to a plain one at $49.99. The expensive model does two jobs: some people buy it, and — more importantly — it makes the $49.99 model feel modest and reasonable. Retailers routinely stock a product they don't expect to sell, purely to reshape the comparison.
  • Executive pay. Top managers' salaries are driven heavily by comparison to other top managers (as Vlerick's research on Belgian listed companies showed). Because status is positional, pay ratchets upward: no executive wants to be paid below the peer benchmark, so the benchmark keeps climbing.
Discussion
  • How can you apply the logic of relativity — e.g. to make yourself happier (choose your comparison set deliberately) or to sell something (add a decoy)?
  • Which relative vs. absolute goods dominate your own daily life?
  • Who suffers more from relativity — maximizers or satisficers? (Hold that question; §4.4 answers it.)

3.2  Anchoring

A · The intuition

We follow previous reference points. Once a number or a past experience is in mind, it acts as an anchor that pulls our subsequent judgements toward it — even when the anchor is arbitrary and logically irrelevant. Where relativity is about comparing options side by side, anchoring is about how an earlier value contaminates a later one.

B · Which rational steps it breaks — Steps 2, 3, 4

Rational choice assumes we value options through genuine cost–benefit analysis. Anchoring says value is instead constructed from whatever anchor happens to be nearby. This corrupts weighting the options (Step 2), ranking them (Step 3) and choosing (Step 4), because the whole valuation is quietly tethered to an irrelevant starting number.

Core experiment · “arbitrary coherence” (Ariely, Loewenstein & Prelec, 2003)
Participants
Business students (people who should value goods rationally).
Task 1
Write down the last two digits of your Social Security / ID number.
Task 2
State the maximum you'd pay for several products (wine, gadgets, chocolate).
Independent variable
The size of that essentially random two-digit number.
Dependent variable
The size of the bids.
Result
The higher the ID number, the higher the bids — students with high numbers bid up to several times more for the identical product than students with low numbers.

Why it's devastating: a number the participants knew was meaningless still shaped what they were willing to pay. Ariely calls this “arbitrary coherence”: the absolute level of what we'll pay is arbitrary (set by the anchor), yet our choices relative to that anchor stay coherent — so we never notice the anchor doing the work.

C · Anchoring and value — “make it hard to get”

“In order to make a man or a boy covet a thing, it is only necessary to make the thing difficult to attain.” — Mark Twain, The Adventures of Tom Sawyer

Tom Sawyer turns the tedious chore of whitewashing a fence into something the other boys pay him to be allowed to do — simply by acting as though it were a rare privilege. The anchor here is framing: is this an unpleasant task you must be paid to endure, or a treat you'd pay to enjoy? Ariely tested exactly this.

Anchoring & framing experiment — the poetry reading
Participants
Students.
Condition 1a
Told they would have to pay to hear Ariely read poetry.
Condition 1b
Told they would be paid to listen to the same reading.
Condition 2
Then told the reading is actually free.
Measure
Would they participate, and on what terms?

Result: the first anchor set the whole frame. Those first told they would pay treated the reading as valuable and were willing to attend for free; those first told they'd be paid treated it as a chore and now needed payment to attend the very same free event. The initial framing — not the experience itself — determined its perceived worth.

Discussion
  • Where do sellers set anchors for you? (“Was €199, now €99”; the manufacturer's “suggested” price; the most expensive dish placed at the top of a menu to make the rest look cheap.)
  • Your first salary, your first rent, the first price you ever paid for coffee — how are these still anchoring what you think is “normal” today?

3.3  Emotions colour our thinking

A · The intuition — hot vs. cold states

We badly overestimate how consistent we are. In reality our decisions swing with our emotional state, and — critically — we cannot accurately predict, while calm, how we will decide while aroused. Psychologists call this a hot–cold empathy gap.

Two decision-making states
‘COLD’ state‘HOT’ state
Rational, deliberateEmotional, aroused (hunger, fear, pain, anger, excitement, desire)
Long-term, goal-directedImmediate gratification, impulsive
ThinkingDoing
Prefrontal cortex (planning, control)Limbic system (emotion, drive)

B · Which rational steps it breaks — Steps 2, 3, 4

The rational model assumes stable preferences. But the person who calmly plans (cold) and the person who actually decides in the moment (hot) can want genuinely different things. Because we plan in a cold state for choices we'll make in a hot state, we systematically mis-weight, mis-rank and mis-choose — for example, promising to diet, save, or behave safely, then doing the opposite when temptation is live.

Experiment · arousal and decision-making (Ariely & Loewenstein, 2006)
Participants
35 male students.
Design
Each answered the same questions about attraction, willingness to take risks, and how far they'd go to obtain sex — once in a calm (cold) state and once in a highly aroused (hot) state (a within-subjects design, so each person is his own comparison).
Independent variable
Emotional state: neutral vs. aroused.
Dependent variable
Judgements about risky and safe behaviour.

Result: in the hot state the same individuals reported dramatically greater willingness to engage in risky and ethically questionable behaviour, and far less commitment to “safe” choices, than they had predicted for themselves while cold. The gap between the two states was large — and the cold-state self had no idea how differently the hot-state self would decide.

D · Implications

So what?

Good decisions are not just about willpower in the moment — they are about designing your cold-state self to protect your hot-state self. Don't rely on resolving to resist temptation when aroused; instead make the decision in advance and remove the option: don't keep the snacks in the house, arrange safe transport before the party, automate savings so the money never reaches your hot-state hands. Sex education, addiction treatment, and financial planning all work better when they account for the hot state rather than assuming people are always in the cold one.

3.4  Diversification bias & the urge to keep options open

A · The story of Xiang Yu

The Chinese general Xiang Yu led his army across a river to attack the enemy. While his troops slept, he ordered the cooking pots smashed and the boats burned — deliberately destroying his army's options to retreat or even to eat. With no way back, the soldiers had only one direction to fight in, and they won. The paradoxical moral: eliminating your own options can make you stronger. Yet almost everything in our psychology pushes the other way — we cling to options even when they cost us.

B · The idea, vs. rational choice — Step 4

Behavioral economics finds three related tendencies:

  • We don't like to lose options, even bad ones we'll never use.
  • We diversify — spread our bets — even when it's unnecessary or actively silly.
  • We avoid focusing our resources, even when there is really only one good choice.

This breaks Step 4 (choosing the best): instead of committing to the best option, we spend real time and money keeping worse options alive “just in case.”

Experiment · the “doors” game (Shin & Ariely, 2004)
Participants
Students.
Setup
A computer game with three doors. Clicking inside a door earns money; each door pays out in a different range. You have a limited budget of clicks, so every click spent switching between doors is a click not spent earning money behind the best one.
Independent variable
In one condition, a door you ignored would shrink and eventually disappear; in the other, all doors stayed open no matter what.
Dependent variable
How much effort players spent keeping doors open.

Result: when doors threatened to vanish, players wasted clicks running back to keep them alive — even though those doors were worth no more, and the frantic door-tending lowered their total earnings. The mere possibility of losing an option triggered irrational effort to preserve it. In a later phase, players shifted toward disappearing doors even more strongly as availability shrank.

Conclusion: we treat a closing option as a loss, and loss-aversion makes us pay to avoid it — even when the option is worthless and keeping it open makes us poorer.

Practical relevance
  • Staying in a dead-end job or relationship “to keep options open.” Taking five subjects you're mediocre at instead of committing to the two you're great at. Keeping dozens of browser tabs, half-read books, unfinished side-projects — all open doors that quietly drain you.
  • Sometimes, like Xiang Yu, the strongest move is deliberately closing a door so you can commit fully to what remains.

3.5  Conclusion — mapping the biases onto the rational model

The elegance of the section is that each bias attacks a specific step of rational choice theory. Seen together, they show the model failing at every stage — not randomly, but systematically and predictably:

Which rational step each bias corrupts
Rational choice stepBehavioral bias that attacks it
1 · Know your goals / valuesRelativity — value exists only in context, not absolutely
2–4 · Weight, rank & chooseAnchoring — valuations tethered to irrelevant reference points

The headline: we make errors in a systematic and predictable way. That is the whole thesis of behavioral economics — and it is precisely what makes our irrationality studiable and, unfortunately, exploitable.

Extra · Procrastination

A closely related failure worth flagging: procrastination is the hot–cold gap applied to time. In a cold state we set sensible long-term goals (start the essay early, exercise, save); in the hot present, immediate gratification wins and we postpone. Ariely's research on procrastination shows a practical fix — externally imposed deadlines beat self-set intentions. Students who were given (or forced to commit to) evenly spaced deadlines outperformed those told simply to “hand everything in by the end.” Structure protects us from our present-biased selves; pure willpower usually doesn't.

Part Four

4.The paradox of choice

4.0  The core paradox

Part 3 attacked the assumption that we choose well. Part 4 attacks the deeper cultural assumption from §1.3 — that more choice is always better. This is Barry Schwartz's territory.

The assumption on trial

“The more choices, the better, because it maximises freedom; and the more freedom, the more prosperity and welfare, because people can pick what's best for them.” This chain of reasoning underlies modern consumer societies. Schwartz argues it is only true up to a point — after which extra choice starts to hurt.

The paradox has two distinct negative effects, and it's important not to blur them:

  • Paralysis — with too many options, people struggle to choose at all, and often don't choose — even when not choosing is clearly against their interest.
  • Discontent — even when people do manage to choose, having faced many options makes them less satisfied with what they picked.

4.1  Paralysis

A · Big choices — retirement savings (the 401(k) study)

A U.S. 401(k) is a workplace retirement-savings plan: money you put in is tax-advantaged and often partly matched by your employer — meaning that not joining is like refusing free money. It is close to a financial no-brainer.

Study · participation vs. number of funds offered
Participants
~800,000 employees across 657 organisations.
Independent variable
The number of investment fund options offered by the plan (ranged from about 2 to 60).
Dependent variable
Whether employees enrolled at all.

Result: the more funds a plan offered, the fewer people joined. Every additional ~10 funds measurably reduced participation. Faced with an overwhelming menu, many people froze and defaulted to doing nothing — turning down free employer money and tax breaks. More options produced less of the beneficial behaviour.

This is paralysis at its most costly: the stakes are enormous (retirement security) and the “safe” default of inaction is actually the worst outcome.

B · Small choices — the jam experiment (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000)

The most famous demonstration in this whole literature, precisely because the stakes are tiny — it isolates the effect of choice-quantity alone.

Setting
A promotional tasting stand in a large upscale supermarket.
Participants
Adult shoppers (middle class, 62% women).
Independent variable
Extensive choice: 24 exotic jam flavours  vs.  limited choice: 6 flavours.
Dependent variables
How many shoppers stopped to look; how many tasted; how many bought.

The striking result: the big display attracted more attention (24 jams is eye-catching), and people tasted about the same amount in both. But at the checkout the effect reversed completely — shoppers at the 6-jam stand were about ten times more likely to actually buy (30% vs. 3%). The large assortment drew people in and then paralysed them; they walked away empty-handed rather than commit.

Conclusion & management implication

Limited choice = more purchases. Attracting interest and converting it into a decision are two different things, and more options helps the first while sabotaging the second.

Business model example — “Bongo box” gift boxes. A smart way to offer variety without paralysis is to pre-bundle and categorise. Instead of one giant menu, the customer first picks a manageable theme — Staying in nature (14), Culture & entertainment (19), Eating & drinking (24), Wellness (7), City break (17), Active experience (13), Surprise (8), a coupon code (2). Each box still contains many underlying options, but the buyer only ever faces a short, digestible menu at each step. Structure the choice; don't dump it.

Discussion
  • Where do you see paralysis in practice — streaming catalogues, restaurant menus, dating apps, university course catalogues, insurance and phone tariffs? Which businesses succeed precisely by curating down the options for you?

4.2  Discontent

Paralysis is about failing to choose. Discontent is the second, subtler cost: even a successful choice tastes worse when it was drawn from a large set.

How to read this table — it's the heart of §4

Look at the tension. The large assortment felt more enjoyable and exciting at the moment of choosing (“pleasant” 6.02 vs 4.72) — this is the seductive appeal that draws us in. But it was simultaneously more overwhelming and frustrating, took nearly three times as long (24s vs 9s), and — the punchline — produced the lowest actual satisfaction with the chocolate finally eaten (5.46 vs 6.28).

Two more crucial details. First, anticipated satisfaction was identical (5.67) across conditions — people cannot predict that more choice will let them down, so they keep seeking it. Second, and remarkably, the 6-option choosers ended up more satisfied (6.28) than even the no-choice group (4.92) — so the answer is not “no choice.” It's a moderate, curated amount of choice. Choice follows an inverted-U: too little is disappointing, too much is worse, and a modest middle is best.

Why does extensive choice breed discontent? Three mechanisms: (1) higher expectations — with 30 options, surely one is perfect, so anything less feels like a failure; (2) opportunity costs — every rejected option's attractive features nag at you, diluting the appeal of the one you picked; (3) self-blame — with so many options, a disappointing outcome must be your fault. These reappear in §4.3–4.4.

4.3  The underlying processes (Schwartz)

In his TED talk, Barry Schwartz names why abundant choice backfires. These are the psychological processes that turn “more options” into “less happiness”:

  • Regret & anticipated regret. With many alternatives, it's easy to imagine a better outcome you missed — so you regret your choice, and even dread that regret in advance, which makes deciding harder.
  • Opportunity costs. Every option you reject has attractive features, and those subtract from your satisfaction with the winner. More options = more foregone attractions haunting you.
  • Escalation of expectations. When options are limitless, you expect perfection. A good outcome no longer produces a pleasant surprise — it merely meets the sky-high bar, so the best you can feel is “not disappointed.”
  • Self-blame. If you had only two options and were let down, you blame the world. With a hundred options, a poor result is your fault — you should have chosen better. Abundant choice converts external disappointment into internal, well-being-corroding self-criticism.

Schwartz's sharpest claim links this to modern unhappiness and even clinical depression: a culture of unlimited choice and sky-high expectations sets people up to feel that any shortfall is a personal failing.

4.4  Consequences

A · Individual level — how you choose matters

The costs of choice don't hit everyone equally; they depend on your decision style. Schwartz's questionnaire probes it — do statements like these sound like you?

  • I channel-surf even when I'm trying to follow one programme; I switch radio stations to check nothing better is on, even when I'm happy with what's playing.
  • Buying a gift for a friend is hard — I worry about getting it exactly right.
  • No matter how satisfied I am with my job, I keep an eye out for better opportunities.
  • Relationships are like clothing: I expect to “try on” a lot before finding the perfect fit.
  • I love ranked lists — best films, best novels, best athletes — and I fantasise about quite different ways of living.

The key distinction: maximizers vs. satisficers

A maximizer searches every option “down to the bone,” determined to make the single best possible choice. Nothing less than optimal will do.

A satisficer (satisfy + suffice) has standards, but as soon as an option is good enough to meet them, takes it and moves on.

Counter-intuitively, maximizers often obtain objectively better outcomes (a higher-paying job, a cheaper price) — yet they feel worse. The exhaustive search is exhausting, it maximises exposure to regret and opportunity costs, and it guarantees they'll always wonder whether a better option was still out there. Which aspect is most problematic? Research points to one component in particular — the compulsive search for alternatives and inability to stop looking.

The problem isn't wanting good things — it's the endless, restless searching that never lets you settle.

Process 1 · Regret

Maximizers carry more regret over decisions already made, precisely because they can always picture the road not taken. Regret is the emotional tax on refusing to let a choice be final.

Process 2 · Social comparison

Much of our (dis)satisfaction comes not from the choice itself but from comparing our outcome to other people's — the relativity of §3.1 applied to our whole lives.

What we learn: maximizers are far more reactive to social comparison. An upward comparison sinks their self-rated performance and spikes negative feelings much more sharply than it does for satisficers; and even a downward comparison gives them less of a lift. Because maximizers are permanently scanning for the best, they are permanently exposed to someone, somewhere, who did better — a recipe for chronic dissatisfaction.

B · The organizational field — but note the opposite failure mode

Everything above warns against over-deliberating. In organisations there is a mirror-image danger: decision-deficiency syndrome (Michael Hyatt) — managers who delay decisions indefinitely or never decide at all.

Not deciding is itself a choice — usually a bad one. Refuse to decide and you become “the plaything of circumstances,” drifting into a negative spiral. Making a decision, by contrast, releases energy: it creates momentum, direction and new challenges.

The reassuring part, echoing Dave Ramsey's “I make a decision; and if it's wrong, I make another,” is that most decisions are reversible. You always keep the freedom to review or adjust a wrong choice. Employees respect a manager who decides and occasionally errs — then owns it and re-chooses — far more than an eternal doubter. “What you decide often matters less than that you decide.”

Reconciling the two halves of Part 4

There is no contradiction. Satisficing is not indecision. The satisficer decides promptly once “good enough” is met — the healthy middle. Both the maximizer (who can't stop searching) and the decision-deficient manager (who can't start choosing) are stuck at the extremes of the same inverted-U. The goal is to set clear standards, choose the first option that meets them, then commit and stop looking.

Applied exercise · the BBQ packages

You're organising a BBQ for 4 people. Which package will the average customer most likely choose — and which will be most satisfying for the guests?

PackageContentsPrice
A4 ribs, 4 steaks, 4 chicken breasts, 4 chipolatas (16 items, 4 kinds — exactly enough)€40
B2 each of ribs, steaks, chicken breasts, chipolatas, scampi, marinated steaks, farmer's sausages, chicken legs (16 items, 8 kinds — much more variety)€60
CSame 8-kind variety as B, plus mayonnaise (a near-identical “decoy” for B)€70

How to think it through with this chapter:

  • What the average customer chooses: variety is seductive (§4.2 — the wide assortment felt more pleasant), so B and C tempt strongly. C is a classic decoy (§3.1): it's clearly worse value than B (basically B + mayo for €10 more), and its job is to make B look like the smart buy. So the relativity/decoy machinery pushes the average customer toward Package B — the many-options, higher-priced middle.
  • What actually satisfies the guests: for 4 people, Package A gives everyone a full, generous portion of proven crowd-pleasers at the lowest price. B and C spread the same 16 pieces across 8 kinds — only 2 of each — so guests face the paradox of choice at the grill, more items run out or get picked over, and you paid €20–30 more for the privilege. The variety that looks more attractive in the shop delivers less satisfaction at the table.
  • The lesson: the package that maximises perceived value at the point of sale (B, propped up by decoy C) is not the one that maximises actual enjoyment (A). This is the whole chapter in one barbecue: the appeal of abundance at the moment of choosing diverges from the satisfaction it delivers.
Chapter takeaways — the five things to remember
  1. The rational ideal (Homo economicus) assumes full information, perfect logic and stable preferences — and concludes more choice is always good.
  2. Behavioral economics shows we break that model systematically and predictably: relativity, anchoring, hot/cold emotions, and refusing to close options.
  3. The paradox of choice shows abundance itself backfires — paralysis (we don't choose) and discontent (we like our choice less).
  4. Choice is an inverted-U: some beats none, but a curated middle beats overwhelming abundance. Maximizing correlates with lower well-being; the damage is in the endless search, not in high standards.
  5. The practical fix works at both levels: as a chooser, satisfice — set standards, take the first good-enough option, commit, stop looking. As a designer/manager, curate and structure options, and decide rather than drift.
