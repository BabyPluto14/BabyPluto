Topic 1 · Options

Options are powerful financial contracts that give the holder a right without an obligation. Understanding them unlocks both risk management in finance and strategic thinking in corporate investment.

1.1 · What is a Financial Option?

A financial option is a derivative contract — meaning its value is derived from an underlying asset, most commonly a stock. The option gives the option holder (buyer) the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell the underlying asset at a predetermined price called the strike price (or exercise price), on or before a specific date called the expiry date.

In return for granting this right, the holder pays an upfront cost called the option premium. The person who sells (writes) the option is called the option writer or counterparty. The writer collects the premium but takes on the obligation to fulfill the contract if the holder exercises their right.

💡
Everyday Analogy — The Concert Ticket Imagine you pay €20 for the option to buy a festival ticket at €100 any time in the next 3 months. If the festival sells out and resale prices hit €200, you exercise your option and profit. If the festival gets cancelled and tickets drop to €40, you simply don't exercise — you lose only the €20 premium. This is the core logic of every option: limited downside, asymmetric upside.

Options are used for two broad purposes: hedging (reducing existing risk) and speculation (taking a position on future price direction).

🛡️ Hedging — Risk Reduction

A company already exposed to price risk uses options to limit the damage if prices move against them. The cost (premium) is like paying for insurance.

Example: An airline that needs to buy jet fuel in 6 months buys a call option on oil. If oil prices spike, the option profits offset the higher fuel costs.

📈 Speculation — Directional Bets

A trader with no underlying exposure uses options to bet on price direction, using less capital than buying the asset outright (this is called leverage).

Example: A trader who believes Tesla stock will rise buys call options rather than the actual shares, risking only the premium while keeping upside potential.

1.2 · Calls and Puts — The 2 Products, 4 Positions

There are only two types of options — call options and put options. Each can be held from two sides (buyer or seller), creating four fundamental positions.

📞 CALL Option — The Right to BUY

A call gives the holder the right to purchase the underlying asset at the strike price. You benefit when the market price rises above the strike price.

Profit formula (at expiry):
Payoff = max(ST − K, 0) − Premium

Where ST = stock price at expiry, K = strike price

📤 PUT Option — The Right to SELL

A put gives the holder the right to sell the underlying asset at the strike price. You benefit when the market price falls below the strike price.

Profit formula (at expiry):
Payoff = max(K − ST, 0) − Premium

A long put is essentially portfolio insurance against price declines.

Position Action You profit when… Max Loss Max Gain Typical Use
Long Call Buy a call option Price rises above strike + premium Premium paid Unlimited Hedge rising input costs / bullish speculation
Short Call Write/sell a call option Price stays flat or falls Unlimited Premium received Generate income on existing stock holdings
Long Put Buy a put option Price falls below strike − premium Premium paid Strike price − premium Hedge against falling asset prices / bearish speculation
Short Put Write/sell a put option Price stays flat or rises Strike price − premium Premium received Willing to buy asset cheaply if it falls
⚠️
Risk asymmetry is the most important concept in options When you buy an option (long position), your downside is always capped at the premium you paid. When you write an option (short position), you collect a small premium but face potentially massive — even unlimited — losses. This is why large financial institutions have strict position limits for option writers.
Option Moneyness

The relationship between the current stock price and the strike price is called moneyness:

✅ In-the-Money (ITM)

Call: Stock price above strike (S > K)
Put: Stock price below strike (S < K)

Has intrinsic value — immediate profit if exercised now.

➖ At-the-Money (ATM)

Stock price equals (or is very close to) the strike price (S ≈ K)

Intrinsic value ≈ 0. Value consists only of time value.

❌ Out-of-the-Money (OTM)

Call: Stock price below strike (S < K)
Put: Stock price above strike (S > K)

No intrinsic value. Cheaper — but requires a larger price move to profit.

🔗
Combination: The Long Straddle Buying a call AND a put simultaneously on the same asset, same strike, same expiry. You profit from large moves in either direction. Used by import/export companies to hedge currency risk when they do not know whether their home currency will strengthen or weaken. The short straddle (selling both) bets on low volatility and earns both premiums — but faces losses in both directions if prices move significantly.
1.3 · The Black-Scholes-Merton Model — Pricing an Option

Before 1973, there was no agreed-upon way to price options. In 1973, economists Fischer Black and Myron Scholes published a formula that solved this problem, earning Myron Scholes (and colleague Robert Merton) the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1997. The Black-Scholes model gives the theoretical fair price of a European call option on a non-dividend-paying stock.

📖
European vs American Options A European option can only be exercised on the exact expiry date. An American option can be exercised any time up to and including the expiry date. Black-Scholes is only correct for European options. American options require more complex models (like the binomial tree) because the "early exercise" feature adds value.
C = S · N(d₁) − K · e−rT · N(d₂)

d₁ = [ ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2) · T ] / (σ · √T) d₂ = d₁ − σ · √T

───────────────────────────────────────────── S = current stock price K = strike price (the agreed purchase price) T = time to expiry (in years, e.g. 6m = 0.5) r = risk-free interest rate (e.g. gov't bond rate) σ = volatility (annualised standard deviation of returns) N(·) = cumulative standard normal distribution e = Euler's number ≈ 2.718 (used for discounting) ─────────────────────────────────────────────

Intuition behind the formula:

The formula can be read as two parts. The first part, S · N(d₁), is the expected benefit of receiving the stock (the upside). The second part, K · e−rT · N(d₂), is the present value of what you pay (the strike price), adjusted by the probability you'll actually exercise. The option's value is the difference between what you expect to gain and what you expect to pay.

The 5 Value Drivers of an Option
VariableDescriptionEffect on Call PriceEffect on Put PriceWhy?
S — Stock Price ↑ Current market price of underlying ↑ Increases ↓ Decreases Call moves closer to or into the money
K — Strike Price ↑ The agreed exercise price ↓ Decreases ↑ Increases Higher hurdle to beat (call); easier to exceed (put)
T — Time to Expiry ↑ More time remaining on the contract ↑ Increases ↑ Increases More time = more chance of a favourable price move (time value)
r — Risk-Free Rate ↑ Government bond yield ↑ Increases ↓ Decreases Higher rates reduce the PV of the strike price you'd pay

🌪️
Volatility (σ) is the single most important driver — and the most surprising Unlike a direct stock investor who fears volatility, the option holder loves volatility. If the stock moves wildly upward, the call option profits enormously. If it moves wildly downward, the call holder simply doesn't exercise — losing only the small premium. This asymmetry means volatility only creates upside for the option holder. In markets, this measure of implied future volatility is traded directly — it is called the VIX (Volatility Index), often nicknamed the "fear gauge."
Option Greeks — Sensitivity Measures

Traders use the Greeks to measure how sensitive an option's price is to changes in each input:

Δ Delta

How much the option price moves for each €1 move in the stock. Range 0–1 for calls. Delta = 0.6 means: stock rises €1 → option rises €0.60.

Θ Theta

The daily time decay — how much value the option loses each day as it approaches expiry, all else equal. Always negative for buyers.

V Vega

Sensitivity to volatility. How much the option price rises for a 1% increase in σ. High vega = highly sensitive to changes in market uncertainty.

ρ Rho

Sensitivity to changes in the risk-free interest rate. Generally a small effect compared to delta and vega.

1.4 · Real Options — Embedding Flexibility into Investment Projects

The same logic that makes financial options valuable applies directly to corporate investment decisions. A real option is the right, but not the obligation, to take a specific managerial action regarding a real (non-financial) asset. Just as a call option lets you buy a stock at a fixed price in the future, a real option lets a company — for example — delay a factory investment, or expand a product line, or abandon a failing project.

✈️
The Boeing 737 MAX Case Study In 2019, Boeing's 737 MAX aircraft was grounded worldwide after two fatal crashes. Boeing faced three distinct real options: (1) Option to abandon — stop all 737 MAX production permanently; (2) Option to wait/delay — halt production, wait for regulatory clarity, and resume later; (3) Option to expand — invest heavily in fixing all issues and scale up production to meet backlogged orders. Standard NPV analysis would struggle to capture these choices. Real options analysis revealed that the option to wait and eventually resume had substantial positive value — which is broadly what Boeing chose.
Why Standard NPV Falls Short

The Net Present Value (NPV) rule says: invest if the present value of future cash flows exceeds the initial cost. This is the correct framework, but the standard version has a critical flaw — it assumes managers are passive. It is a "one-shot decision": commit now, observe outcomes, do nothing to adapt. Real-world managers are not passive. They observe new information and adjust course. Standard NPV systematically undervalues projects that contain embedded flexibility.

🔑
The expanded NPV formula Expanded NPV = Standard NPV + Value of Real Options
A project with a negative standard NPV can still be worth undertaking if the embedded real options (e.g. the option to expand if successful) have positive value that more than compensates. This is why many early-stage investments — R&D projects, pilot programmes, small exploration wells — have value even when their expected cash flows alone do not justify the cost.
The Six Types of Real Options

⏰ Option to Delay (Wait)

Defer a capital commitment until uncertainty is reduced. Like holding a financial option before expiry — you preserve the right to invest later without having to commit now.

Example: An oil company holds land rights but delays drilling until oil prices recover above a profitable threshold.

📈 Option to Expand

If a pilot project or Phase 1 succeeds, scale up by investing more. The initial investment "buys" the option to expand.

Example: A retailer opens one store in a new country as a pilot. Success triggers a roll-out of 50 stores.

📉 Option to Scale Down

If conditions deteriorate, reduce scale to limit losses rather than continue at full speed.

Example: A semiconductor manufacturer reduces production runs when chip prices fall.

🚪 Option to Abandon

Exit the project entirely and salvage remaining value — like exercising a put option on the project.

Example: A pharmaceutical company abandons a drug in Phase 2 trials after poor efficacy results, recovering remaining R&D budget.

🔄 Option to Switch

Alter the use of assets or inputs in response to changing prices or market conditions.

Example: A power plant designed to burn either gas or coal can switch fuels based on which is cheaper at any given time.

🌱 Growth Option

Early investments "plant a seed" that creates the option for future, larger investments. Common in technology and life sciences.

Example: A tech startup's Series A investment buys the option to raise Series B if the product achieves user growth targets.

Decision Tree Analysis — The Meghan Flea Market Example

A decision tree is a visual tool that maps out all future decisions and uncertain outcomes. It contains two types of nodes: decision nodes (squares) where managers choose, and chance nodes (circles) where nature resolves uncertainty.

Meghan wants to rent a flea market booth for €500. There is a 75% chance of good weather (profit €1,500) and 25% chance of bad weather (loss €100).

❌ Naive Approach — No Flexibility

Meghan commits regardless and goes in all weather.

E[Value] = (0.75 × €1,500) + (0.25 × −€100) − €500
= €1,125 − €25 − €500
= €600

In 25% of cases she loses €600 total (€100 loss + €500 booth).

✅ Real Option Approach — Wait for Forecast

She waits for the weather forecast. Only goes if sunny.

E[Value] = (0.75 × €1,500) − €500
= €1,125 − €500
= €625

Option value = €625 − €600 = +€25

She avoids the €600 bad-weather loss entirely.

🗝️ The Manager's Real Options Framework — 3 Steps
1

Identify the key uncertainty

Ask: "What is the single biggest unknown in this project?" Frame it explicitly. In Meghan's case it is the weather. In a drug trial it is clinical efficacy. In a new market entry it is consumer demand. Once named, you can design around it.

2

Design interim decision points (staging)

Break the investment into stages so management can react once uncertainty resolves. Do not commit all capital upfront. Each stage "buys" information and the option to proceed or stop. This is standard practice in venture capital, R&D, and oil exploration.

3

Recalculate NPV — add the option value

Revised NPV = Standard NPV + Value of Real Options. Use decision tree analysis or option pricing models. A project that looked marginal or even negative under standard NPV may become clearly attractive once real option value is recognised.

🔗
The surprising connection between financial and real options Both share the same fundamental property: the higher the uncertainty, the more valuable the option. A volatile stock → a more valuable financial option. An uncertain market → a more valuable real option to delay, expand, or abandon. This is the counterintuitive insight: uncertainty is an asset when you hold an option. Managers who recognise this avoid over-committing early and instead actively preserve flexibility.
🏆
Topic 1 Core Takeaway

Options — financial or real — give you asymmetric payoffs: limited downside, unlimited (or large) upside. Their value comes from flexibility in the face of uncertainty. Black-Scholes tells us the fair price of a financial option using 5 inputs, with volatility being the most important. Real options tell corporate managers to build flexibility into projects, stage investments, and never treat a capital decision as irreversible.

Topic 2 · The Efficient Market Hypothesis

The EMH is one of the most tested and debated theories in economics. It asks a simple but profound question: can you consistently make more money in the stock market than the average investor, by being smarter?

2.1 · The Random Walk — Why Stock Charts Look Like Coin Flips

A classic experiment asks experienced traders to distinguish between two charts: one showing 5 years of actual S&P 500 prices, and one generated by flipping a coin (heads = price up, tails = price down). Subjects consistently fail to tell them apart. This is not a coincidence — it is the signature of a random walk.

🎲
The self-destroying pattern — the logic of market efficiency Suppose someone discovers a reliable pattern in past stock prices: "every time stock X rises 5 days in a row, it falls on day 6." What happens? Rational traders immediately start short-selling the stock on day 5, pushing the price down earlier. This trading continues until the pattern no longer produces excess profit — at which point the pattern has destroyed itself. In equilibrium, no exploitable pattern can survive. Prices must reflect all available information, and future moves must be unpredictable.

This is the essence of the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH), formalized by Nobel laureate Eugene Fama in 1970. The EMH states that asset prices fully and instantly reflect all available information. Any attempt to earn above-average returns using publicly available information will fail because the information is already priced in.

🔗
Connection to the Law of One Price and Arbitrage Market efficiency is enforced by arbitrage — the simultaneous buying and selling of equivalent assets to profit from price discrepancies. In efficient markets, arbitrage opportunities are exploited so quickly by professional traders that they disappear almost instantly. This rapid arbitrage is what keeps prices aligned with fundamental value.
2.2 · The Three Forms of Market Efficiency

Fama identified three distinct "levels" of efficiency, each corresponding to a different information set being reflected in prices. They are nested — each stronger form includes all the information of weaker forms.

Weak Form Level 1
Prices reflect all historical market data — past prices, trading volumes, and returns.
  • Technical analysis (chart patterns, moving averages, momentum indicators) cannot generate consistent excess returns. If past patterns predicted the future, they would already be traded away.
  • Fundamental analysis (studying earnings, balance sheets, valuations) may still yield superior returns because it uses information not captured by price history alone.
  • Evidence: Strong empirical support in developed markets. Most academic studies confirm that past price trends do not reliably predict future returns after accounting for transaction costs.
Semi-Strong Form Level 2
Prices reflect all publicly available information — financial statements, earnings announcements, news, economic data, analyst reports.
  • Neither technical analysis NOR fundamental analysis can consistently outperform the market. By the time a public announcement is made, the price has already moved to reflect it.
  • Studies of event studies (e.g. earnings surprises, M&A announcements) consistently show that prices adjust to new public information within minutes or even seconds.
  • Implication: Passive index investing (which makes no attempt to pick stocks) is likely to outperform the average active manager after fees.
  • Evidence: Broadly supported in developed markets, though some anomalies persist (see Section 2.3).
Strong Form Level 3
Prices reflect all information — including private, non-public (insider) information.
  • Even corporate insiders with access to confidential information about their own company cannot consistently earn abnormal returns.
  • This form is considered too strong to hold in practice. Empirical evidence shows that insiders do earn abnormal returns on their trades — which is exactly why insider trading laws exist in most countries.
  • The existence of insider trading regulations is itself an implicit acknowledgement that markets are not strong-form efficient.
✅
Academic Consensus Most financial economists agree that developed markets (e.g. NYSE, London Stock Exchange, Euronext) are at least weak-form efficient and largely semi-strong-form efficient. Strong-form efficiency is generally rejected. Emerging markets tend to be less efficient — offering more potential opportunities but also more risk.
2.3 · Market Anomalies — Where Efficiency Breaks Down

Despite its theoretical elegance, the EMH has been challenged by documented anomalies — patterns that appear to offer abnormal returns and persist even after they become well-known. These anomalies are important because they reveal the limits of purely rational market models.

📅 The January Effect

Stocks — especially small-cap stocks — tend to earn higher returns in January than in any other month. Likely explanation: investors sell losing stocks in December to realize tax losses, then reinvest in January. The predictable selling pressure depresses prices in December and the buying creates a January rebound.

Challenge to EMH: If this is predictable, why hasn't arbitrage eliminated it? Possibly transaction costs and limits to arbitrage prevent full correction.

🏠 Home Bias

Investors systematically overweight their domestic stocks and underweight international stocks, even though international diversification would reduce portfolio risk without reducing expected returns. This violates the rational, utility-maximizing investor assumption at the core of EMH.

Cause: Familiarity bias, perceived currency risk, differential information costs.

📤 The Disposition Effect

Investors tend to sell winning stocks too early (to lock in gains) and hold losing stocks too long (to avoid realizing losses). This is irrational — tax considerations actually argue for the opposite. Described by behavioral economists Shefrin and Statman, it stems from loss aversion.

📊 Momentum Effect

Stocks that have performed well over the past 3–12 months tend to continue outperforming over the next few months (price momentum). This directly contradicts weak-form EMH, which says past prices cannot predict future returns. First documented by Jegadeesh and Titman (1993), it remains one of the most robust anomalies.

🎮 GameStop Short Squeeze (2021)

Retail investors on Reddit's WallStreetBets coordinated to buy heavily shorted stocks like GameStop (GME), forcing short-sellers to buy back shares at rising prices (a short squeeze). GME shares rose 1,700% in two weeks — far beyond any fundamental justification. A clear case of a price driven by coordination and narrative, not information.

🏗️ Evergrande & China Real Estate

Evergrande, China's second-largest property developer, accumulated over $300bn in liabilities. Rating agencies, auditors, and investors missed or ignored the systemic risk for years. When the crisis broke in 2021, contagion spread rapidly. A potential example of semi-strong inefficiency — publicly available leverage data was not correctly priced.

🤖
AI and Market Efficiency — Current Debate (2025–26) A major concern in financial markets today: AI-related stocks (NVIDIA, Palantir, Microsoft) have been trading at extremely high valuation multiples. Some analysts draw parallels to the dot-com bubble of 1999-2000 — when "story stocks" disconnected from fundamentals. The question is whether this is rational pricing of genuinely transformative technology, or a speculative bubble. This is an EMH test playing out in real time.
2.4 · Active vs Passive Investing — The Practical Debate

The EMH has a direct, practical implication for investors: if prices reflect all available information, it is impossible to consistently "beat the market." This is the intellectual foundation for passive investing (index funds). The opposing view — that skilled analysis can identify mispriced securities — underpins active investing.

🎯 Active Investing

  • Fund managers select individual securities and try to beat a benchmark (e.g. S&P 500)
  • Requires belief that markets contain mispricing that skilled analysts can identify
  • Higher management fees (typically 0.5%–2% p.a.)
  • Higher portfolio turnover = higher transaction costs and tax drag
  • Most studies show ~85% of active funds underperform their benchmark after fees over 10 years (S&P SPIVA report)
  • Surviving funds suffer from survivorship bias — underperformers are merged/closed

📊 Passive Investing (Index Funds / ETFs)

  • Mechanically replicates the composition of a market index (e.g. S&P 500, MSCI World)
  • No stock selection, minimal portfolio turnover
  • Very low fees (e.g. 0.03%–0.20% p.a.)
  • By definition captures the market return (beta) before fees; beats the average active fund after fees
  • Championed by Jack Bogle (Vanguard founder) and Warren Buffett for retail investors
  • $10+ trillion now held in US index funds (2025)
⚠️
The Passive Investing Paradox — Is Success Making Markets Less Efficient? Here is the irony: passive investing works because active investors keep prices efficient. But as more money flows into passive funds (which do no price analysis), there are fewer active traders doing the price discovery work. Research from the LSE (2024) and others suggests that high passive fund ownership increases price co-movement, reduces idiosyncratic price discovery, and may amplify market-wide sell-offs. Paradoxically, the more successful passive investing becomes, the more opportunity it creates for the remaining active investors — until equilibrium is found.
2.5 · Beyond the EMH — Alternative Theories

🧠 Behavioral Finance

Founded by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky (Kahneman received the Nobel Prize in 2002), and extended by Richard Thaler (Nobel 2017), behavioral finance documents that humans are systematically irrational in predictable ways. Key biases:

  • Overconfidence bias — we overestimate the accuracy of our forecasts
  • Loss aversion — losses feel ~2.5× more painful than equivalent gains feel good (Prospect Theory)
  • Anchoring — we fixate on arbitrary reference points (e.g. the price we paid for a stock)
  • Herding — we mimic others' behavior, amplifying market movements
  • Representativeness bias — we over-extrapolate recent trends (e.g. assuming past winners will keep winning)
  • Mental accounting — treating money differently depending on its source or intended use

🦎 Adaptive Market Hypothesis (AMH)

Proposed by MIT Professor Andrew Lo (2004), the Adaptive Market Hypothesis reconciles the EMH with behavioral finance by applying evolutionary biology. Key ideas:

  • Markets are ecosystems populated by competing financial species (retail investors, hedge funds, pension funds)
  • These agents learn, adapt, and compete — like organisms in a biological environment
  • Market efficiency is not a fixed state — it varies over time as conditions change and strategies evolve
  • Behavioral biases are not errors — they are heuristics that evolved to work in certain environments and fail in others
  • Strategies that earned excess returns can stop working as competitors copy them — and then become profitable again later (evolutionary cycle)
  • In stable periods, markets approach EMH. In crises, fear and herding dominate

Implication for managers: Don't ask "is the market efficient?" — ask "how efficient is this market right now, and in which asset class?" The answer determines your investment strategy.

🏆
Topic 2 Core Takeaway

Markets are not perfectly efficient, but they are competitive enough that consistently beating them is very difficult. The practical lesson: be deeply skeptical of anyone claiming a reliable way to predict stock prices — if it were truly reliable and risk-free, rational traders would already have arbitraged it away. The best evidence-based strategy for most investors is low-cost diversification via index funds, while recognising that behavioral biases and market conditions periodically create real opportunities for disciplined active managers.

Topic 3 · Debt Policy & Capital Structure

How should a firm finance itself — with equity, debt, or some combination? This fundamental question in corporate finance connects daily financial decisions to macroeconomic stability.

3.1 · Setting the Scene — The Financing Decision

Recall the fundamental distinction in corporate finance between the investment decision (which projects to undertake) and the financing decision (how to fund those projects). The investment decision determines what value is created. The financing decision determines how that value is divided between debt holders and equity holders — but does it also affect the total value created?

A firm's capital structure is the mix of financial instruments used to finance its assets — principally debt (bonds, bank loans) and equity (shares). The ratio of debt to total capital is called leverage or gearing.

🏛️ Equity Financing

  • Issue new shares to raise capital
  • No obligation to repay (permanent capital)
  • Shareholders receive dividends (discretionary)
  • Shareholders bear the residual risk — last in line if firm fails
  • Cost = cost of equity (re) — typically highest

📋 Debt Financing

  • Borrow via bonds, loans, or credit lines
  • Legal obligation to make fixed interest and principal payments
  • Interest payments are tax-deductible (key advantage)
  • Debt holders are senior to equity — lower risk, lower return
  • Cost = cost of debt (rd) — lower than equity

🔄 WACC

The blended cost of all financing sources is the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC):

WACC = re×(E/V) + rd×(1−tc)×(D/V)

Where V = D + E (total firm value). WACC is the firm's hurdle rate for investment decisions.

3.2 · Modigliani & Miller Proposition I — Capital Structure is Irrelevant (in a Perfect World)

In 1958, economists Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller published what became one of the most influential papers in financial economics, earning them both Nobel Prizes. Their MM Proposition I states:

⚖️
MM Proposition I (No Taxes) In perfect capital markets (no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, no transaction costs, symmetric information, efficient markets), the total value of a firm is independent of its capital structure. A firm worth €1 billion cannot increase or decrease that value simply by rearranging how it is financed.

VU = VL (Value of Unlevered firm = Value of Levered firm)

The intuitive explanation — "Homemade Leverage": Suppose you own shares in an all-equity firm. If the firm issues debt to repurchase shares (increasing leverage), does that change your wealth? No — because you can undo any leverage decision the firm makes by borrowing (or lending) on your own account. This ability to create homemade leverage means investors will not pay a premium for a leveraged firm, since they can replicate the same effect themselves. Any mispricing would be eliminated by arbitrage.

🔓
Why MM I matters in practice MM I does NOT say "financing decisions don't matter in the real world." It says: "if financing decisions do matter, it must be because one or more of the perfect market assumptions is violated." This tells you exactly where to look — taxes, financial distress, information asymmetry. Each violated assumption is a potential source of value from financing decisions.

With the introduction of corporate taxes, MM I is modified. Interest payments on debt are tax-deductible, meaning the government effectively subsidizes debt financing:

VL = VU + PV(Tax Shield)
Tax Shield per year = tc × rd × D

tc = corporate tax rate (e.g. 25%) rd = interest rate on debt D = total debt outstanding PV(Tax Shield) = tc × D (if debt is permanent)

Example: A firm with €10 million in permanent debt at 5% interest, with a 30% tax rate, saves €150,000 per year in taxes. The present value of this perpetual saving = 30% × €10m = €3 million in additional firm value — just from the tax benefit of debt.

3.3 · Modigliani & Miller Proposition II — Leverage Raises Shareholder Returns (and Risk)

MM Proposition II is a direct consequence of Proposition I. If total firm value is unchanged by leverage, but debt holders get a fixed, low return, then equity holders must earn a higher return to compensate. Leverage is like using a physical lever — it amplifies both force and risk.

re = ra + (ra − rd) × (D/E)

re = required return on equity (what shareholders demand) ra = return on firm's assets (unlevered cost of capital) rd = cost of debt D/E = debt-to-equity ratio

As D/E increases, re increases linearly. This makes perfect sense from a risk-return perspective: shareholders of a levered firm face financial risk on top of business risk, and they rationally demand higher compensation.

Leverage Magnification — Worked Example

Consider a €100,000 investment that either increases to €125,000 (+25%) or falls to €75,000 (−25%):

Scenario Financing Own Capital Invested Outcome: +25% ROE: +25% Outcome: −25% ROE: −25%
No Leverage 100% equity €100,000 €125,000 +25% €75,000 −25%
50% Leverage €50k debt + €50k equity €50,000 €75,000 → repay €50k debt → €25,000 equity +50% €25,000 → repay €50k → equity = €25,000 loss −50%
90% Leverage €90k debt + €10k equity €10,000 €35,000 → repay €90k → equity = −€55,000 (default!) Loss €(15,000) → repay debt → deep default Bankrupt
⚔️
Debt is a double-edged sword — micro and macro At the individual level, leverage multiplies both gains and losses. At the macro level, the 2008 Global Financial Crisis illustrated catastrophic systemic risk from excessive leverage throughout the financial system. US households took on subprime mortgages they could not afford; banks held these mortgages with 30:1 leverage via complex mortgage-backed securities (MBS) and collateralized debt obligations (CDOs); investors around the world bought these instruments. When US house prices fell just 5–10%, the leveraged amplification turned a housing correction into a global financial crisis.
3.4 · The Trade-Off Theory — Finding the Optimal Capital Structure

The trade-off theory of capital structure says that firms choose their debt level by weighing the benefits of debt against the costs of debt. The optimal capital structure is at the point where marginal benefit equals marginal cost.

✅ Benefits of Debt — Rising with D

  • Tax shield: Interest payments are deducted before tax. Every euro of interest reduces taxable income by €1, saving tc×€1 in tax. This is a direct government subsidy on debt financing.
  • Discipline effect: Debt forces managers to generate cash flows to service interest — reducing wasteful spending of free cash flow (agency cost reduction).
  • Signal of confidence: Taking on debt signals that management believes future cash flows are strong enough to service the obligations.

❌ Costs of Debt — Rising with D

  • Direct financial distress costs: Legal fees, restructuring costs, and administrative expenses if the firm cannot meet its debt obligations. Can represent 10–20% of firm value.
  • Indirect financial distress costs: Lost business — customers avoid a firm that may not honour warranties; suppliers demand cash up front; talented employees leave for safer employers.
  • Agency costs of debt: Shareholders may take excessive risks ("asset substitution") or pass up positive NPV projects ("debt overhang") when a firm is in distress — harming debt holders.

Who uses more debt? Trade-Off Theory predictions Firms with stable, predictable cash flows and tangible assets (utilities, real estate, infrastructure) can safely use more debt — lower distress risk, more tax shield benefit. Firms with volatile cash flows, many intangible assets, or high growth options (tech startups, biotech) should use less debt — distress costs are higher and tax shield is worth less when taxable income is uncertain.
3.5 · The Pecking Order Theory — Financing Follows a Hierarchy

Proposed by Stewart Myers and Nicholas Majluf (1984), the pecking order theory says that firms do not have a target optimal capital structure. Instead, they follow a fixed hierarchy of financing preferences driven by information asymmetry — the fact that managers know more about the firm's true value than outside investors.

💭
Why Information Asymmetry Matters Imagine you are buying a used car from someone who knows its complete service history and you don't. You'd worry they're only selling because it has hidden problems — so you'd discount your offer price. The same logic applies when a firm issues new equity: outside investors worry that management would only issue shares if they believe the shares are overvalued. This concern means new equity issuance is always received skeptically by markets — typically, stock prices fall 2–3% when a new share issuance is announced. This "adverse selection" cost makes equity expensive.
The Pecking Order Hierarchy
1st

Internal Funds — Retained Earnings & Depreciation Cash Flows

Cheapest source of capital — no new investors, no information asymmetry cost, no transaction costs. Firms always prefer to fund investment from internally generated cash first. The cost is the opportunity cost — the return shareholders could have earned by investing the cash themselves (i.e. the cost of equity).

2nd

External Debt — Bank Loans, Bonds

When internal funds are exhausted, firms turn to debt. Debt involves less information asymmetry than equity — creditors only care about whether the firm can make fixed payments, not about future upside. Rating agencies and covenants provide transparency. The cost is higher than internal funds but much lower than equity issuance.

3rd

External Equity — New Share Issues

Used only as a last resort. The adverse selection discount (investors assume overvaluation), plus underwriting fees, makes equity the most expensive source of external capital. A firm that announces a new equity issue typically sees its share price fall immediately — a direct empirical confirmation of pecking order logic.

🔑
Pecking Order Theory — Key Predictions
  • Profitable firms use less debt — they can self-fund
  • There is no well-defined "target" debt ratio — leverage fluctuates based on investment needs vs. available internal funds
  • Firms issue equity rarely, and only when debt capacity is exhausted or when shares are clearly overvalued
  • Large, profitable firms (e.g. Apple, Microsoft) accumulate huge cash reserves — consistent with preferring internal funds
3.6 · Trade-Off vs. Pecking Order — Side-by-Side Comparison
Dimension Trade-Off Theory Pecking Order Theory
Core driver Tax shield vs. financial distress costs Information asymmetry & cost of capital hierarchy
Target leverage? Yes — firms have an optimal D/E ratio No — leverage is a residual of financing needs
Profitable firms use… More debt (higher taxable income to shield) Less debt (fund internally, less need to borrow)
Equity issuance Done when leverage is above target Last resort — signals overvaluation

🌏
Neither theory is complete — the real world needs both In practice, firms' capital structure decisions are influenced by both frameworks simultaneously, plus additional factors: market timing (issuing equity when share prices are high), managerial preferences, industry norms, and rating agency requirements. A truly complete capital structure theory does not yet exist — this remains one of the most actively researched areas in corporate finance.
🏆
Topic 3 Core Takeaway

MM I proved that in a perfect, frictionless world, how you finance a firm is completely irrelevant — only the investment decisions matter. The real world breaks this irrelevance via taxes (creating the tax shield benefit of debt), bankruptcy costs (creating the cost of financial distress), and information asymmetry (creating the pecking order). Trade-off theory finds a value-maximizing optimal debt level. Pecking order theory says firms follow a cost hierarchy. Both offer genuine insights, and the right answer depends on the firm's specific situation. The overarching lesson: debt is always a double-edged sword — powerful when used carefully, dangerous when misused.

Key Terms Glossary

All highlighted terms from this guide, defined concisely for quick revision.

Adaptive Market Hypothesis (AMH)
Markets as evolutionary ecosystems where agents adapt; efficiency varies over time.
Agency Cost
Cost arising from conflicts of interest between principals (shareholders) and agents (managers/debt holders).
Arbitrage
Risk-free profit from exploiting price discrepancies; its pursuit enforces market efficiency.
At-the-Money (ATM)
An option whose strike price equals the current market price of the underlying asset.
Behavioral Finance
Study of how systematic psychological biases affect financial decisions and asset prices.
Black-Scholes Model
Mathematical formula for pricing European call/put options on non-dividend-paying stocks.
Call Option
Contract giving the holder the right (not obligation) to buy an asset at the strike price.
Capital Structure
The mix of debt and equity a firm uses to finance its assets.
Cost of Equity (re)
The return required by shareholders; increases with financial leverage (MM II).
Decision Tree
Graphical tool mapping future decisions (squares) and chance events (circles) in a project.
Delta (Δ)
Option Greek measuring option price sensitivity to a €1 change in the underlying stock price.
Derivative
A financial contract whose value is derived from an underlying asset, index, or rate.
Disposition Effect
Investors' tendency to sell winners too early and hold losers too long.
Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)
Asset prices fully and instantly reflect all available information; excess returns are not achievable.
European Option
An option exercisable only on its expiry date (vs. American, exercisable any time).
Financial Distress
State where a firm cannot (or may not be able to) meet its debt obligations; incurs direct and indirect costs.
Free Cash Flow
Operating cash flow minus capital expenditure; cash available to return to investors.
Greeks
Sensitivity measures for option prices: Delta, Gamma, Theta, Vega, Rho.
Hedging
Using financial instruments (options, futures) to reduce or eliminate an existing risk exposure.
Herding
Behavioral bias where investors follow the crowd rather than independent analysis.
Homemade Leverage
An investor's ability to replicate a firm's leverage decision using personal borrowing.
Information Asymmetry
Situation where managers have more/better information about the firm than outside investors.
In-the-Money (ITM)
Call: S > K. Put: S < K. Exercising immediately would generate a profit.
Intrinsic Value (option)
The immediate exercise value of an option: max(S−K, 0) for a call.
Leverage / Gearing
The use of debt to amplify potential returns (and risks) on equity capital.
Long Position
Buying an option (or asset) — you benefit from favourable price moves, maximum loss = premium paid.
Loss Aversion
Behavioral bias: losses feel approximately 2.5× more painful than equivalent gains feel good (Prospect Theory).
MM Proposition I
Firm value is independent of capital structure in perfect markets. VU = VL.
MM Proposition II
Cost of equity increases linearly with leverage; total WACC unchanged (in no-tax world).
Moneyness
The relationship between an option's strike price and the current price of the underlying asset.
NPV (Net Present Value)
Sum of discounted future cash flows minus initial investment; invest if NPV > 0.
Option Premium
The price paid by the option buyer to the writer for granting the right.
Option to Abandon
Real option giving management the right to exit a project and recover salvage value.
Option to Delay
Real option giving management the right to defer an investment decision until uncertainty resolves.
Option to Expand
Real option giving management the right to increase scale of a project if it succeeds.
Out-of-the-Money (OTM)
Call: S < K. Put: S > K. Immediate exercise has no value.
Pecking Order Theory
Firms finance investment using internal funds first, then debt, then equity — driven by information asymmetry costs.
Put Option
Contract giving the holder the right (not obligation) to sell an asset at the strike price.
Random Walk
Theory that stock price movements are unpredictable; future moves are independent of past moves.
Real Option
The right (not obligation) to take a specific managerial action on a real investment project.
Semi-Strong Form EMH
Prices reflect all publicly available information; fundamental analysis cannot consistently beat the market.
Short Position
Selling/writing an option — you collect the premium but take on obligation; limited gain, large/unlimited loss.
Short Squeeze
Rapid price rise forcing short-sellers to buy back stock, further driving up the price (e.g. GameStop 2021).
Strike Price (K)
The agreed price at which an option holder can buy (call) or sell (put) the underlying asset.
Strong Form EMH
Prices reflect all information including private/insider information. Widely rejected in practice.
Tax Shield
The tax saving from deducting interest payments: tc × rd × D per year.
Theta (Θ)
Option Greek measuring daily time decay — how much value the option loses per passing day.
Time Value (option)
The portion of option premium attributable to time remaining — the chance something good happens before expiry.
Trade-Off Theory
Optimal capital structure balances the tax shield benefit of debt against financial distress costs.
VIX (Volatility Index)
Market-implied forecast of S&P 500 volatility over the next 30 days; called the "fear gauge."
Vega (V)
Option Greek measuring price sensitivity to a 1% change in implied volatility.
Volatility (σ)
Standard deviation of an asset's returns; the key driver of option value in Black-Scholes.
WACC
Weighted Average Cost of Capital — blended cost of all financing; the firm's investment hurdle rate.
Weak Form EMH
Prices reflect all historical trading data; technical analysis cannot generate consistent excess returns.

One-Page Exam Cheat Sheet

Everything you need to remember, on one page.

📊 Options

Financial: Call = right to buy. Put = right to sell. Long = buy (limited loss). Short = write (unlimited risk).

BS inputs: S↑→Call↑ · K↑→Call↓ · T↑→both↑ · σ↑→both↑ (most important)

Real options: Delay, Expand, Scale down, Abandon, Switch, Growth

Key rule: Higher uncertainty → more valuable real option. Expanded NPV = Standard NPV + Option value.

📉 Market Efficiency

Weak: Prices reflect historical data → technical analysis fails.

Semi-strong: Prices reflect all public info → fundamental analysis also fails.

Strong: Even insiders can't beat it → rejected in practice (insider laws exist for a reason).

Anomalies: January effect, home bias, momentum, disposition effect, bubbles.

AMH: Markets adapt like ecosystems — efficiency varies by time and market.

🏦 Debt Policy

MM I: VU = VL in perfect markets. Only investments create value.

MM I + taxes: VL = VU + tcD (tax shield adds value).

MM II: re = ra + (ra−rd)×D/E. More debt → higher equity return AND risk.

Trade-off: Optimal debt = max(Tax shield − Distress costs).

Pecking order: Internal funds → debt → equity. No target ratio; driven by info asymmetry.
