A. Introduction — the firm and its decisions

A firm is a web of decisions. This unit zooms in on two of them — what price to set and how much to produce — and shows they depend on demand (how customers respond to price) and on production costs.

The firm's decisions

The firm's many choices feed into each other and ultimately into production, sales and profit:

What this unit is about

  • Key firm decisions: what price to set and what quantity to produce.
  • These depend on the demand function (how responsive quantity sold is to price — its price elasticity) and the cost function (how costs change with output).
  • We model firms that produce differentiated products and therefore have some market power (they face a downward-sloping demand curve, unlike price-takers).
  • We measure the gains from trade (surplus) and ask how policy can affect its division between firms and customers.
The core trade-off in one line Setting a lower price raises quantity sold but lowers revenue per unit; a higher price does the reverse. Whether a price change raises or lowers revenue depends on the elasticity of demand. Whether it raises profit also depends on the cost of producing the extra unit (marginal cost). Profit maximization is about balancing these.

B. Production & costs — key concepts

To choose price and output well, managers must know how costs vary with the quantity produced. That relationship is the cost function.

Fixed, variable and total cost

Definitions
  • Cost function C(Q) — shows how total production cost varies with quantity Q produced.
  • Fixed costs — do not vary with output (e.g. opportunity cost of capital, R&D). They can depend on the planning horizon (long run vs short run). “Fixed” means not varying with the production rate, not literally unchangeable.
  • Variable costs — increase with output (e.g. wages, materials).

Average cost, marginal cost, and how they relate

Two cost measures you must not confuse
  • Average cost (AC) = cost per unit produced = C(Q)/Q. Graphically it is the slope of the ray from the origin to a point on the total-cost curve.
  • Marginal cost (MC) = the effect on total cost of producing one additional unit. Graphically it is the slope of the total-cost curve (its tangent) at a point.
The MC–AC rule (learn this cold)
  • If AC > MC → average cost is decreasing (the cheap extra unit pulls the average down).
  • If AC < MC → average cost is increasing (the expensive extra unit pulls the average up).
Consequence: the MC curve always cuts the AC curve at the AC curve's lowest point.

Advantages of being large: economies of scale

Many sectors are dominated by a few large firms. A key explanation: average costs fall as output rises.

Definitions — don't confuse these two
  • Economies of scale — average cost of production decreases as output increases. (Large firms get a cost advantage.) Newspaper industry (strong scale economies) vs the restaurant sector (weak).
  • Economies of scope — cheaper to produce two products jointly in one firm than separately in two, usually because a common input is shared (e.g. internet + cable TV; insurance + banking).

Economies of scale come from two broad sources — technological advantages and cost advantages.

1. Technological advantages → returns to scale

Costs are fundamentally linked to the production process. Scaling inputs up can raise output more or less than proportionally:

Returns to scaleOutput vs inputsEffect on average cost
Increasing returnsOutput rises more than proportionally to inputs (inputs rise less than proportionally to output)AC decreasing
Constant returnsOutput rises proportionally with inputsAC constant
Decreasing returnsOutput rises less than proportionally to inputs (inputs rise more than proportionally to output)AC increasing
Sources of increasing returns
  • Specialization & indivisibilities in the production process
  • Engineering reasons — e.g. a gas pipeline's material cost scales with its circumference, but capacity with its cross-sectional area; container ships, tankers
Sources of decreasing returns
  • Coordination becomes harder at scale
  • Supervision / management overheads grow

2. Cost advantages

  • Fixed costs — when fixed costs are large, spreading them over more units lowers AC as scale rises. (Fixed = doesn't vary with the production rate: R&D, IT, advertising. A pharma firm's R&D can vary year to year, but not with how many pills it makes.)
  • Bargaining power — large firms buy inputs on more favourable terms (e.g. the Ahold–Delhaize merger).

Worked example: how SpaceX makes space travel cheaper

Fixed development cost F and constant variable cost per launch v give a textbook economy of scale:

TC(q) = F + v·q   ⇒   AC(q) = v + Fq   (decreases as q rises)

C. Demand, elasticity & revenue (bridging — slides not in upload)

Why this section is here The profit-maximization material in Part 2 constantly uses the demand curve, price elasticity and marginal revenue. These were on the missing middle slides, so here is the standard treatment you need to make Section D make sense.

The demand curve & willingness to pay

Definition — Demand curve Shows, for each price, the quantity customers will buy (equivalently, for each quantity, the highest price at which it can all be sold). A firm with market power (a differentiated product) faces a downward-sloping demand curve: to sell more it must lower the price. Each point on the curve is a customer's willingness to pay (WTP) — its reservation price.

Price elasticity of demand

Definition — Price elasticity of demand (ε) The percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price (taken as a positive number):
ε = − % change in quantity% change in price = − ΔQ / QΔP / P
  • ε > 1 → demand is elastic (quantity very responsive; cutting price raises revenue).
  • ε < 1 → demand is inelastic (quantity barely responds; cutting price lowers revenue).
  • ε = 1 → revenue is at a maximum.

Total & marginal revenue

Definitions
  • Total revenue R = P·Q.
  • Marginal revenue (MR) = the change in total revenue from selling one more unit = dR/dQ.
Why MR is below the price To sell one extra unit a firm with market power must lower the price on all units. So MR = (price of the new unit) − (revenue lost on the units it could have sold dearer). That “lost revenue” term makes MR < P whenever demand slopes down. For a linear (straight-line) demand curve, the MR curve has the same intercept and is exactly twice as steep.

This last fact is used repeatedly in Section D, so keep it handy: linear inverse demand P = a − bQ ⇒ R = aQ − bQ² ⇒ MR = a − 2bQ.

D. Pricing & production decisions — profit maximization

Now bring demand and cost together to find the profit-maximizing price and quantity. There are two equivalent methods, and (obviously) they give the same answer:

  1. Isoprofit curves + demand curve — the “constrained optimization under scarcity” picture.
  2. Marginal revenue = marginal cost — the marginal picture.

Method 1 — Isoprofit curves

Definitions
  • (Economic) profit = total revenue − total cost:   π = PQ − TC(Q) = Q(P − AC).
  • Isoprofit curve — all price–quantity combinations that give the same profit (the firm's “indifference curve”).
  • The zero-profit curve is the AC curve (where P = AC, so π = 0).
  • Isoprofit curves above the AC curve = positive profits; curves further from the origin = higher profit.
Slope of an isoprofit curve
slope = − P − MCQ
When Q rises by one unit, profit changes by (P − MC); to hold profit constant, price must change by −(P − MC)/Q.
  • If MC > P (MC above the isoprofit curve) → isoprofit curve slopes up (need a higher price to offset the costly extra unit).
  • If MC < P (MC below) → isoprofit curve slopes down.

Method 1 as constrained optimization (MRS = MRT)

This is exactly the consumer's problem from Unit 3, with new labels:

Consumer (Unit 3)Firm (Unit 7)
Indifference curvesIsoprofit curves — slope = MRS
Budget constraint / feasible frontierDemand curve — the feasible frontier; slope = MRT
Optimum where MRS = MRT (tangency)Profit max where MRS = MRT — highest isoprofit curve tangent to demand

Profit-maximizing price: the markup rule

Setting MRS = MRT and doing the algebra gives one of the most important results in the course — the Lerner markup rule:

P − MCP  =  1ε
Slope of isoprofit (MRS)Slope of demand (MRT)Set MRS = MRT ⇒
− (P − c)/Q − P/(εQ) (P−c)/Q = P/(εQ)  ⇒  (P−c)/P = 1/ε
Reading the markup rule (here c = MC) The price markup (price-cost margin) equals the inverse of the demand elasticity. More elastic demand (larger ε) ⇒ smaller markup ⇒ lower optimal price. A firm whose customers are very price-sensitive cannot charge much above marginal cost. This single equation links market power (Section F) to elasticity.

Method 2 — Marginal revenue = marginal cost

The MR = MC rule To maximize profit, produce at the scale where marginal revenue = marginal cost.
  • If MR > MC → sell more (the extra unit adds more revenue than cost).
  • If MR < MC → sell less.
  • Algebra: π = PQ − TC(Q); the first-order condition dπ/dQ = 0 gives MR = MC.
Then read the price off the demand curve at that quantity. One can show this price also satisfies (P−MC)/P = 1/ε — the two methods agree.

Worked numerical example

Solve it step by step

Demand: Q = 12 − 2P, so inverse demand P = 6 − 0.5Q. Marginal cost MC = 2.

  1. Total revenue: R = PQ = (6 − 0.5Q)Q = 6Q − 0.5Q².
  2. Marginal revenue: MR = dR/dQ = 6 − Q (straight line, twice as steep as demand — same 6 intercept).
  3. Set MR = MC: 6 − Q = 2 ⇒ Q* = 4.
  4. Price from demand: P* = 6 − 0.5(4) = 4.

Answer: Q* = 4, P* = 4. (Check: price 4 > MC 2, as market power implies.)

E. Gains from trade (bridging — slides not in upload)

About this section Part 2 ends on the title slide “Gains from Trade”; its content slides weren't in the upload. Here is the standard CORE/KU Leuven treatment of surplus and the welfare cost of market power, which is where the unit heads next.
Definitions — measuring the gains from trade
  • Consumer surplus (CS) — total willingness to pay minus what customers actually pay. Area below the demand curve, above the price.
  • Producer surplus (PS) — revenue minus variable cost of production. Area above the MC curve, below the price.
  • Total surplus = CS + PS — the total gains from trade.
  • Deadweight loss (DWL) — surplus that is lost because trades that would benefit both sides don't happen (e.g. when a firm restricts output to keep price high).

F. Competition, differentiation & market power (bridging)

Definition — Market power The ability of a firm to set its price above marginal cost without losing all its customers. It comes from producing a differentiated product (brand, quality, location) so the firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve rather than being a price-taker.
How it all ties together The markup rule (P − MC)/P = 1/ε is the bridge:
  • Strong differentiation → less elastic demand (small ε) → large markup, high price (much market power).
  • More competition / closer substitutes → more elastic demand (large ε) → small markup, price near MC (little market power).
In the limit of perfect competition, ε → ∞, the markup → 0, and price = marginal cost (no deadweight loss).

Policy angle: because market power creates deadweight loss, competition policy, regulation and lowering barriers to entry aim to push ε up (more substitutes) and shrink the markup — redistributing surplus toward customers and improving efficiency.

Summary

  1. Costs. The cost function splits into fixed and variable costs. AC = C/Q (slope of ray from origin); MC = slope of the cost curve. MC cuts AC at AC's minimum.
  2. Economies of scale (AC falls with output) come from technological advantages (increasing returns to scale) and cost advantages (spreading fixed costs, bargaining power). Don't confuse with economies of scope.
  3. Demand & revenue. A firm with market power faces a downward-sloping demand curve; MR < P, and for linear demand MR is twice as steep.
  4. Profit maximization. Two equivalent methods: highest isoprofit curve tangent to demand (MRS = MRT), or MR = MC. Both give price via the demand curve, with P > MC and (if profitable) P > AC.
  5. Markup rule. (P − MC)/P = 1/ε: markup = inverse elasticity. More elastic demand → lower price.
  6. Gains from trade & market power. Surplus = CS + PS; market power raises price above MC, creating deadweight loss. Competition and policy shrink the markup toward the efficient outcome.

Self-test questions

1. A firm's AC is 3,600 and its MC is 4,600 at the current output. Is average cost rising or falling? What does this imply about where you are relative to minimum AC?

MC (4,600) > AC (3,600), so AC is rising → you are to the right of the minimum-AC point. Producing a bit less would lower average cost.

2. Demand is Q = 20 − 2P and MC = 3. Find the profit-maximizing quantity and price.

Inverse demand: P = 10 − 0.5Q. R = 10Q − 0.5Q² ⇒ MR = 10 − Q. Set MR = MC: 10 − Q = 3 ⇒ Q* = 7. Price: P* = 10 − 0.5(7) = 6.5. (Check P > MC.)

3. Two firms have the same MC. Firm A's customers are very price-sensitive (ε = 4); Firm B's are loyal (ε = 1.5). Which charges the higher markup, and why?

Markup = 1/ε. Firm A: 1/4 = 25%. Firm B: 1/1.5 ≈ 67%. Firm B charges the higher markup — less elastic demand (more market power) lets it price further above MC.

4. Why does making rocket boosters reusable only lower average cost per launch if the number of launches is large?

Reusability raises fixed development cost F but lowers variable cost v, so AC = v + F/q. At small q the larger F/q dominates and AC is higher; only once q is large enough does the lower v outweigh the spread-out fixed cost, so the reusable AC curve dips below the conventional one.

5. At the profit-maximizing point, is price above or below marginal cost? Above or below average cost? What does each tell you?

Price > MC always for a firm with market power (that gap is the markup). Price vs AC determines profitability: P > AC means positive economic profit; P = AC means zero profit; P < AC means a loss.
