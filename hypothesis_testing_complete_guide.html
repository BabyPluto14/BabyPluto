The Story — Statistics as a Courtroom

Every single test in this chapter — one mean, two means, ANOVA, correlation, chi-square — is the same courtroom drama wearing different costumes. If you understand the trial, you understand the whole chapter. So before any formulas, here is the story.

The trial Imagine you're a juror. On trial is a claim about the world — say, "this new medicine lowers blood pressure." In court there is a golden rule: the defendant is presumed innocent until proven guilty. The prosecution must prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt. If they can't, the defendant walks free — not because they're proven innocent, but because there wasn't enough evidence to convict.

Statistics runs the exact same trial. The roles map perfectly:

CourtroomHypothesis testMeaning
Presumption of innocenceH₀ (null hypothesis)The "boring" default: no effect, no difference, no relationship. We assume it's true until the data forces us to abandon it.
The accusationH₁ (alternative)The interesting claim we're trying to establish: there is an effect / difference / relationship.
The evidenceThe data (test statistic)What we actually observed, boiled down to a single number (a T, F, or χ²).
"Beyond reasonable doubt"Significance level α (usually 0.05)How much doubt we tolerate before convicting. 5% = we accept a 1-in-20 chance of convicting an innocent H₀.
Strength of evidencep-valueIf H₀ really were innocent, how surprising is evidence this extreme? A tiny p-value = very surprising = convict.
"Guilty"Reject H₀Evidence was strong enough. We conclude H₁.
"Not guilty"Do not reject H₀Not enough evidence. Note: this is not "proven innocent."
The single most important idea in the whole chapter: "Do not reject H₀" never means "H₀ is true." A jury that acquits hasn't proven innocence — it just didn't see enough evidence for guilt. Likewise, failing to reject H₀ only means your data didn't provide enough evidence against it. This is the "weak conclusion" and it trips up everyone at least once.
Two ways to get it wrong A jury can make two kinds of mistake. It can convict an innocent person (Type I error — a "false alarm"), or it can let a guilty person go free (Type II error — a "miss"). You can't drive both to zero at once: a jury that never convicts anyone never jails the innocent but also never catches the guilty. Statistics faces the same trade-off, and α = 0.05 is our chosen tolerance for false alarms.

Rather than one story, this chapter throws a series of little cases at you. To keep them straight, meet the cast you'll see again and again — each one is really the same trial with different evidence:

How to read the rest of this guide: whenever you meet a new test, don't ask "what's the new formula?" Ask "who's the defendant (H₀), what's the evidence (test statistic), and what counts as reasonable doubt (the distribution & p-value)?" The costume changes; the trial never does.

0. The Universal 5-Step Recipe MEMORISE

Because every test is the same trial, every test follows the same five steps. Learn this skeleton once and you only ever have to swap in the details.

1
State the hypotheses. Write H₀ (the boring default) and H₁ (your research claim). Decide one-sided (< or >) or two-sided (≠) from the question's wording, before looking at the data.
2
Explore & check assumptions. Look at the data (histogram, boxplot, scatterplot, contingency table). Verify the conditions the test needs — normality, equal variances, independence, expected counts, etc.
3
Compute the test statistic. Squeeze all the evidence into one number (T, F, or χ²) that measures "how far is the data from what H₀ predicts?"
4
Find the p-value. Ask: if H₀ were true, how likely is a statistic this extreme? Read it off the reference distribution (t, F, or χ²) with the right degrees of freedom and the right tail(s).
5
Decide & conclude in words. If p ≤ α → reject H₀. If p > α → do not reject H₀. Then translate back into a sentence about the real-world question.
The only things that change between tests are: the formula in Step 3, the distribution in Step 4, and the assumptions in Step 2. Steps 1 and 5 are literally always the same shape. That's the whole secret of this chapter.

0b. p-values, α, and the Two Errors — Really Understanding Them KEY

These three ideas power every conclusion you'll ever write, so it's worth slowing down.

What a p-value actually is

Definition
p-value = P( evidence at least this extreme │ H₀ is true )

Read it as a conditional: "Assuming the defendant is innocent, how often would pure chance hand me data this damning?" If the answer is "almost never" (small p), then either an extraordinary coincidence happened, or H₀ is false. We bet on the latter.

What a p-value is NOT: it is not the probability that H₀ is true. H₀ is either true or false; it has no probability. The p-value is a property of the data given H₀, not of H₀ given the data. Saying "p = 0.03, so there's a 3% chance H₀ is true" is the classic blunder.

The decision rule

SituationVerdictWording
p ≤ α (e.g. ≤ 0.05)Reject H₀"There is significant evidence that … (H₁)."
p > αDo not reject H₀"There is insufficient evidence that … (H₁)."

The two errors — and why α is exactly the false-alarm rate

H₀ really trueH₀ really false
We reject H₀Type I error (false alarm) — probability = αCorrect! (power = 1−β)
We don't reject H₀Correct!Type II error (miss) — probability = β

Choosing α = 0.05 literally sets the Type I error rate at 5%: over many trials where H₀ is true, we'll wrongly "convict" 1 time in 20. Lowering α (say to 0.01) makes false alarms rarer but makes misses (β) more common — the courtroom trade-off again.

Power (= 1 − β) is the test's ability to catch a real effect. It goes up with larger sample size, larger true effect, and smaller noise. This is why "collect more data" is the universal fix for a test that can't detect anything: bigger n → more power.

One-sided vs two-sided — decide from the question

One-sided
The question points in a direction: "is it larger than…", "does it lower…", "is it less than…". H₁ uses > or <. All of α goes in one tail — easier to reach significance in that direction.
Two-sided
The question is just "is there a difference?", with no prior direction. H₁ uses ≠. α is split between both tails (2.5% each), so the two-sided p-value is double the one-sided one.
Golden rule: pick the direction from the research question before seeing the data. Choosing one-sided after peeking at which way the data leans is cheating (it secretly doubles your false-alarm rate).
H1.1 Part 1

Test for One Mean (σ Unknown) — the t-test

🏢 The case of the Leuven apartments You have a €350,000 budget for a two-bedroom flat in Leuven. A friend insists "forget it, the average is way above that." You can't measure every apartment in the city — but you can sample 23 of them and put the claim on trial. This is the simplest trial of all: one sample, one mean, one number to beat.

1. Why a New Test? The σ Problem

Earlier in the course you tested a mean μ when the population standard deviation σ was known, using the Z-test:

Z-test (σ known)
Z = (X̄ − μ₀) / (σ/√n) ~ N(0,1) under H₀

But here's the catch that makes this whole section necessary: in real life you almost never know σ. If you don't know the true mean μ, how would you know the true spread σ? You wouldn't. All you have is the sample standard deviation s.

The fix seems trivial — just swap σ for s. And that's exactly what we do. But swapping in an estimate injects extra uncertainty: s itself jumps around from sample to sample. Because of that extra wobble, the standardised statistic no longer follows the tidy N(0,1) curve. It follows a slightly heavier-tailed cousin: the t-distribution.

T-test (σ unknown — the realistic case)
T = (X̄ − μ₀) / (s/√n) ~ tn−1 under H₀
Everything else is unchanged. The 5-step recipe is identical. Only three things differ from the Z-test: (1) use s instead of σ, (2) use the t-distribution instead of N(0,1), (3) degrees of freedom = n − 1.

2. The t-Distribution KEY

The t-distribution has a single dial: degrees of freedom (df) = n − 1. Intuitively, df counts "how much independent information you have to estimate the spread." More data → more df → less wobble.

  • Same shape family as N(0,1) — symmetric, bell-shaped, centred at 0.
  • Fatter tails than the normal — this is the "extra uncertainty tax" for estimating σ with s. Fatter tails mean extreme values are a bit more likely, so you need more evidence to reject H₀.
  • As df → ∞ the tails thin out and tn−1 → N(0,1). For n ≥ 30 they're already almost identical.
df (= n−1)t critical value (95%, two-sided)vs z = 1.96

Takeaway: smaller n → more uncertainty → heavier tails → larger critical values → harder to reject H₀. That's the distribution protecting you from over-claiming on thin data.

3. Worked Example 1 — Apartment Prices EXAM

n = 23 Leuven 2-bedroom apartments. X̄ = €377,557, s = €106,947. Is the mean price larger than €350,000? α = 0.05.

1
Hypotheses. H₀: μ = 350,000   H₁: μ > 350,000  (one-sided right — the claim is "larger than")
2
Assumptions. Random sample ✓; σ unknown → t-test; n = 23 < 30 so we need prices to be roughly normal (check histogram/QQ-plot — reasonable for prices). → use t22.
3
Test statistic. SE = s/√n = 106,947/√23 = 22,298.
T = (377,557 − 350,000)/22,298 = 27,557/22,298 = 1.236.
4
P-value. Right-sided: p = 1 − T.DIST(1.236; 22; TRUE) = 0.115.
5
Conclusion. p = 0.115 > 0.05 → do not reject H₀. "There is insufficient evidence that the mean Leuven apartment price exceeds €350,000." Note the courtroom logic: we did NOT prove prices are below €350k — our 23-flat sample simply wasn't damning enough.
Reading it: T = 1.236 never reaches the critical value t_c = 1.717. The tail area beyond it (11.5%) is far bigger than 5%. Not guilty.

Worked Example 2 — Ketchup Bottles EXAM

🍅 The case of the underfilled bottles The label promises 750 ml. A consumer watchdog suspects the factory is quietly shorting customers. They grab 40 bottles off the shelf. Same trial, opposite direction — and this time the evidence is overwhelming.

Label = 750 ml. Suspect underfilling. n = 40, X̄ = 742 ml, s = 4 ml. α = 0.05.

1
Hypotheses. H₀: μ = 750   H₁: μ < 750  (one-sided left — suspect "less than")
2
Assumptions. Random sample ✓; σ unknown → t-test; n = 40 ≥ 30 so the CLT covers normality regardless of shape. → t39.
3
Test statistic. SE = 4/√40 = 0.6325. T = (742 − 750)/0.6325 = −12.65.
4
P-value. Left-sided: p = T.DIST(−12.65; 39; TRUE) ≈ 0.000 (essentially zero).
5
Conclusion. p ≈ 0 < 0.05 → reject H₀. "There is very strong evidence that bottles contain less than 750 ml on average." Guilty.
Why so different from the apartments? Same test, but here the signal (8 ml short) is huge relative to the noise (SE ≈ 0.63 ml). A 20-standard-error gap is unmissable. The apartment gap was barely one standard error — buried in noise.

4. Confidence Interval & Excel Functions EXAM

The CI changes the same way the test did: σ → s and z* → t*.

CI for μ — σ unknown (t-interval)
CI = X̄ ± t* · s/√n, where t* = T.INV.2T(α; n−1)
Apartment 95% CI: t* = T.INV.2T(0.05; 22) = 2.074. ME = 2.074 × 22,298 = 46,246.
CI = [377,557 ∓ 46,246] = [331,311 ; 423,803]. This interval contains 350,000 — perfectly consistent with "do not reject H₀."
CI ↔ test always agree. A two-sided 95% CI contains μ₀ ⟺ the two-sided test at α = 0.05 fails to reject H₀. The CI is just the test viewed from the other side.
What you needExcelUse for
P-value (left)=T.DIST(t; df; TRUE)H₁: μ < μ₀
P-value (right)=1-T.DIST(t; df; TRUE)H₁: μ > μ₀
P-value (two-sided)=T.DIST.2T(ABS(t); df)H₁: μ ≠ μ₀
t* two-sided=T.INV.2T(alpha; df)CI half-width
t_c one-sided right=T.INV(1-alpha; df)critical value
Common mistakes: using NORM.DIST instead of T.DIST · df = n instead of n−1 · putting σ in the formula when you only have s · using T.DIST.2T for a one-sided test (it already doubles the tail).
H1.2 Part 2

Tests for Normality — the prerequisite check

🍷 The case of the wine prices Before the t-test can testify in court, we have to make sure it's a reliable witness. The t-test's testimony is only trustworthy if the data is roughly bell-shaped (or n is large). So H1.2 is a background check on the witness: is this data normal enough? A sommelier hands you 45 bottle prices and asks whether they follow a normal distribution.

5. Why Test Normality — and the Sneaky Reversed Hypotheses

The t-test needs data that is normal or n ≥ 30. When n is small, you must verify normality first. Two toolkits:

Visual / graphical
  • Histogram — bell-shaped?
  • Boxplot — symmetric, no outliers?
  • Normal QQ-plot — points on a straight line?
Fast, but subjective
Formal tests ← this section
  • Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS)
  • Shapiro–Wilk (SW)
Objective, give a p-value
Normality test hypotheses — always these
H₀: the variable IS normally distributed
H₁: the variable is NOT normally distributed
The plot twist: here H₀ is the thing you're hoping for. When checking a t-test assumption, you want a large p-value (do not reject H₀) — it means "no evidence against normality, carry on." This is the opposite of most tests, where you hope to reject. Recall the courtroom: acquitting normality ("not guilty of being non-normal") is not proof it's perfectly normal — which is exactly why you always pair the formal test with the plots.

6. Step 1 — Always Look First

Before any formal test, eyeball the three standard diagnostics:

PlotNormal looks like…Non-normal looks like…
HistogramSymmetric bellSkewed, bimodal, flat
BoxplotMedian centred, equal whiskersOutlier dots, lopsided box
QQ-plotPoints hug the diagonalS-curve (heavy tails), bending

7. Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) Test

The idea: compare two "climbing" curves — the cumulative distribution functions (CDFs).

  • Empirical CDF F̂(x) — a staircase built from your actual data.
  • Theoretical CDF F(x) — the smooth S-curve of a normal with the same mean and SD as your sample.

If the data really is normal, the staircase should hug the smooth curve. The test measures the single biggest vertical gap between them:

KS statistic
D = max │ F̂(x) − F(x) │
🍷 Wine example: X̄ = 17.7298, s = 8.97385.
F(10.29) = NORM.DIST(10.29; 17.7298; 8.97385; TRUE) = 0.2035 → if prices were truly normal, 20.35% of bottles should cost ≤ €10.29. Compare that to the real fraction ≤ €10.29 to get one gap; D is the biggest such gap over all x.

The Lilliefors correction — a subtle but examinable point

We cheated slightly: we estimated μ and σ from the very same data we're testing. That makes the normal curve fit "too well," so the plain KS p-value is too lenient (it under-rejects). The Lilliefors correction replaces the reference distribution with a simulated one that accounts for this, giving an honest p-value.

In practice: SPSS applies Lilliefors automatically for a normality KS test — you just read the corrected "Sig." Reject H₀ if p ≤ 0.05 (large D = data strays from the curve = evidence against normality).

8. Shapiro–Wilk (SW) Test HIGHER POWER

The idea: the SW test asks "how straight is the normal QQ-plot?" Its statistic is essentially the correlation between your sorted data and the values a perfect normal would produce. Straight line = normal.

Shapiro–Wilk statistic
SW ≈ correlation between observed data and expected normal quantiles
  • Under H₀ (normal): QQ-plot is a straight line → SW ≈ 1.
  • Reject H₀ if SW is meaningfully below 1 (points bend off the line).
Prefer Shapiro–Wilk. It usually has higher power — it's better at catching genuine non-normality. When SPSS prints both, lead with the SW result.
Kolmogorov–Smirnov (+ Lilliefors)Shapiro–Wilk
Based onMax CDF gapQQ-plot linearity
StatisticD (small under H₀)SW (≈1 under H₀)
Reject H₀ ifD large, p ≤ 0.05SW ≪ 1, p ≤ 0.05
PowerLowerHigher — preferred

9. The n Nuances Everyone Forgets EXAM

Large n → the test almost always rejects. With huge samples, even trivial, harmless deviations become "significant." But for n ≥ 30 the CLT already makes X̄ normal, so you don't need the assumption anyway. Don't let a significant normality test on n = 500 scare you off a t-test.
Small n → the test barely rejects anything. For n < 15 the test has low power; "not significant" doesn't mean "normal." Lean on the plots.
The practical rule:
  • n ≥ 30: don't worry — CLT handles it, use the t-test freely.
  • 15 ≤ n < 30: check plots and run the test; both should support normality.
  • n < 15: be cautious — plots + Shapiro–Wilk; if in doubt, use a non-parametric test.

SPSS path: Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Explore → Plots → tick "Normality plots with tests." Read the Shapiro–Wilk row's Statistic and Sig.

H2 Part 3

Comparing Two Means

💊 The case of the two groups So far we compared one sample to a fixed number. Now the questions get more interesting: does a medicine change patients? Do men and women differ? These pit two means against each other — H₀: μ₁ = μ₂. But there's a fork in the road that decides everything: are the two measurements linked to the same subjects, or from separate groups?

10. The Big Decision — Paired or Unpaired?

Paired data
  • One group of subjects
  • Two measurements each (X and Y)
  • X and Y are dependent — same person/object
  • before/after, left/right, two treatments on the same unit
Unpaired (independent) data
  • Two separate groups
  • One measurement per subject
  • Groups are independent — different people
  • treatment group vs control group
The one question that decides it: "Is each observation in group 1 naturally matched to a specific observation in group 2?" Before/after on the same person → paired. Two different sets of people → unpaired.
Shoe soles, Experiment 1 (Unpaired)Shoe soles, Experiment 2 (Paired)
Design50 people wear old soles, 50 others wear new100 people each wear one old + one new sole
X↔YIndependent — different feetDependent — same person's two feet
AdvantageSimplerCancels between-person differences → more powerful

11. Paired t-Test — the "Reduce to One Sample" Trick KEY

The elegant move: for paired data, don't wrestle with two means. Instead compute each pair's difference D = X − Y, then run a one-sample t-test (H1.1!) on those differences against 0.

The equivalence that makes it work
H₀: μX = μY ⟺ H₀: μX − μY = 0 ⟺ H₀: μD = 0
Paired t-statistic
T = (D̄ − 0) / (s_D/√n) ~ tn−1

where D̄ and s_D are the mean and SD of the n differences.

Why pairing is powerful: subtracting X − Y for each person deletes all the between-person variation (some people just naturally run high). What's left in D is only the within-person treatment effect — a cleaner, smaller-noise signal. Same reason a before/after weight-loss study beats comparing two random groups.
💊 Captopril worked example 15 patients have diastolic blood pressure measured before (X) and after (Y) treatment. Does the drug lower it? α = 0.05.
1
Hypotheses. D = before − after. H₀: μ_D = 0   H₁: μ_D > 0 (if the drug works, before > after → D > 0)
2
Compute D for each of the 15 patients; find D̄ and s_D.
3
Assumptions. Paired ✓; n = 15 < 30 → check that the differences D are roughly normal (histogram/QQ of D).
4
Statistic. T = D̄ / (s_D/√15) ~ t14.
5
P-value. Right-sided: p = 1 − T.DIST(t; 14; TRUE). If p ≤ 0.05 → "Captopril significantly reduces diastolic blood pressure."
SPSS — direct
Analyze → Compare Means → Paired-Samples T-Test → put before & after as the pair.
SPSS — manual
Transform → Compute D = before − after, then One-Sample T-Test on D with test value 0.
Direction matters: define D so that H₁ points the way your research question does. If you write D = after − before but H₁ says "before > after," your sign flips and you'll misread the p-value.

12. Unpaired t-Tests & the Variance Question KEY

For two independent groups, the statistic is built on the difference of sample means X̄₁ − X̄₂:

General form (σ unknown)
T = (X̄₁ − X̄₂ − a) / √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂)

where a is the hypothesised difference — usually 0 (H₀: μ₁ = μ₂), but not always:

Non-zero a: "SlimDrink claims you lose at least 5 kg." You're sceptical. H₀: μ_before − μ_after = 5 vs H₁: μ_before − μ_after < 5 → here a = 5.

But there are two versions of the unpaired test, depending on whether the two populations have equal spread:

Case 1: σ₁ = σ₂ (equal — pooled)
Combine both groups' spread into one pooled estimate:
s²_p = [(n₁−1)s₁² + (n₂−1)s₂²]/(n₁+n₂−2)
T = (X̄₁−X̄₂−a)/(s_p·√(1/n₁+1/n₂))

df = n₁ + n₂ − 2
Case 2: σ₁ ≠ σ₂ (unequal — Welch)
Keep the variances separate:
T = (X̄₁−X̄₂−a)/√(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂)
df = Welch's messy formula (SPSS computes it)

Conservative shortcut: df = min(n₁−1, n₂−1) — safe, gives a larger p-value.

Levene's test — the pre-test that picks the version EXAM

Which column do you read? A little test-before-the-test decides. Levene's test checks equal variances:

Levene's hypotheses
H₀: σ₁² = σ₂² vs H₁: σ₁² ≠ σ₂²
Levene's pVerdict on variancesWhich SPSS row?
p > 0.05Assume equal"Equal variances assumed" (pooled)
p ≤ 0.05Significantly different"Equal variances not assumed" (Welch)
Two-step SPSS reading: (1) look at Levene's Sig. to choose the row; (2) read t, df, and Sig. (2-tailed) from that chosen row. Don't skip step 1.
🧪 Calcium worked example Calcium (CAMMOL) for males (n₁ = 91, s₁ = 0.1217) vs females (n₂ = 86, s₂ = 0.1403). H₀: μ₁ = μ₂ vs H₁: μ₁ ≠ μ₂ (two-sided). α = 0.05.
1
Hypotheses. H₀: μ₁ = μ₂   H₁: μ₁ ≠ μ₂ (no prior direction → two-sided).
2
Explore. Two histograms & boxplots on the same scale; roughly symmetric, no wild outliers. n₁+n₂ = 177 ≥ 40 → CLT fine.
3
Levene. Suppose p > 0.05 → equal variances. s²_p = [90·0.1217² + 85·0.1403²]/175 = 0.01718 → s_p = 0.131.
4
Statistic. SE = 0.131·√(1/91 + 1/86) = 0.0197. T = (X̄₁−X̄₂)/0.0197 = −3.841, df = 175.
5
P-value & conclusion. Two-sided: p = T.DIST.2T(3.841; 175) = 0.000171 < 0.05 → reject H₀. "Mean calcium differs significantly between males and females (p < 0.001)."
Welch cross-check: T = −3.825, df = 168.4, p = 0.000184 — virtually identical. When groups are large, pooled and Welch usually agree, so the Levene decision rarely changes the story here.
CI ↔ test, again: if the 95% CI for μ₁ − μ₂ excludes 0, the two-sided test rejects H₀. Pooled CI uses s_p and df = n₁+n₂−2; Welch CI uses separate variances and Welch df.
H3 Part 4

ANOVA & Post-Hoc Tests

👕 The case of the four stores A clothing chain runs four shops — Antwerp, Brussels, Ghent, Hasselt. Head office asks: "do these stores sell differently, or is it all random week-to-week wobble?" Now there are four means, not two. Your first instinct — "just run a t-test on every pair" — is a trap, and understanding why is the whole point of ANOVA.

13. Why Not Just Do Many t-Tests?

Four groups make 6 pairwise comparisons. Each t-test carries a 5% false-alarm risk. Run 6 of them and the chance that at least one fires falsely balloons:

Family-wise error with k independent tests
P(≥ 1 false positive) = 1 − (1 − 0.05)^k

For k = 6: 1 − 0.95⁶ = 26.5%. More than one in four "findings" would be pure noise. That's the courtroom equivalent of holding 6 trials and calling it a win if any jury convicts.

ANOVA's fix: one single F-test asks "are these groups all the same?" while holding the overall false-alarm rate at exactly 5%. Only after it rejects do we go hunting for which pairs differ — with a corrected threshold (post-hoc).

14. The Core Idea — Between vs Within Variability KEY

ANOVA's insight is beautiful: to tell if group means truly differ, compare two kinds of spread.

Between-group variability
How far apart are the group means?

Large → groups really differ.
This is the signal.
Within-group variability
How much do individuals scatter around their own group mean?

Large → noisy even inside one group.
This is the noise.
F-statistic — signal-to-noise ratio
F = MS_between / MS_within = s²_between / s²_within
  • Under H₀ (all means equal): signal ≈ noise → F ≈ 1.
  • Reject H₀ when F is much bigger than 1 (signal drowns out noise).
  • Always one-sided right — only large F counts as evidence.

4 stores × 5 weeks. Means: Antwerp 14.6, Brussels 27.2, Ghent 20.0, Hasselt 13.4. N = 20. α = 0.05.

What the F-test does NOT tell you: it says "at least one store differs," not "all differ" and not "which one." That's the cliffhanger — and post-hoc tests are the sequel.

17. Post-Hoc — Which Groups Differ? (Bonferroni) KEY

Once ANOVA rejects, we do all g(g−1)/2 pairwise comparisons — for g = 4 that's 6. Each is a t-test with two twists:

Twist 1 — borrow the pooled variance from ALL groups

Instead of pooling only the two groups being compared, use MS_within from the full ANOVA (built on all g groups). More data behind the estimate → more power.

Post-hoc t-statistic (groups i, j)
T = (X̄ᵢ − X̄ⱼ) / √(MS_within/nᵢ + MS_within/nⱼ),   df = N − g
df = N − g, not nᵢ+nⱼ−2. This is the single most-missed detail in post-hoc tests.
Antwerp vs Brussels: MS_within = 10.2, n = 5 each. SE = √(10.2/5 + 10.2/5) = √4.08 = 2.02. T = (14.6 − 27.2)/2.02 = −6.24, df = 16. Two-sided raw p = 2·P(T ≤ −6.24) = 1.186 × 10⁻⁵.

Twist 2 — the Bonferroni correction EXAM

Running k tests inflates the family-wise error (the 26.5% problem). Bonferroni simply splits the budget: test each pair at α/k.

Bonferroni threshold per test
α_Bonf = α / k = 0.05 / (g(g−1)/2)
Sales (g=4, k=6): α_Bonf = 0.05/6 = 0.00833. Antwerp vs Brussels: raw p = 1.2×10⁻⁵ < 0.00833 → significantly different ✓
SPSS shortcut: SPSS's Bonferroni "Sig." is already the adjusted p-value (raw p × k). So you compare it directly to 0.05 — don't divide α yourself. Both routes agree: (raw p < α/k) ⟺ (k·raw p < α).

Results across all 6 pairs (means: Brussels 27.2 > Ghent 20.0 > Antwerp 14.6 > Hasselt 13.4):

ComparisonDiffConclusion (α=0.05)

Brussels − Ghent+7.2Different

The story's punchline: Brussels is the star — significantly ahead of all three others. Antwerp and Hasselt are near-twins (14.6 vs 13.4) and statistically indistinguishable. That nuance is exactly what the single F-test could never have told you.

Post-hoc CIs: CI = (X̄ᵢ−X̄ⱼ) ± t*·√(MS_within/nᵢ + MS_within/nⱼ) with t* = T.INV.2T(α/k; N−g). If a CI excludes 0 → that pair differs at the family-wise 5% level.

19. Pearson Correlation Test H4.1

Two quantitative measurements per observation. The sample gives you r (−1 ≤ r ≤ 1); you want to know about the population ρ (rho).

r — sample correlation
r > 0 positive · r < 0 negative · |r|→1 strong · |r|→0 weak
ρ — population correlation
The truth we're after. We see r, we test ρ.
🎬 Movies 100 movies. X = age (years since release), Y = length (minutes). r = −0.5091 — a moderate negative link in the sample. Is ρ < 0 in the whole population, or is −0.51 just luck?
Hypotheses (only ρ = 0 is examinable)
H₀: ρ = 0 (no linear relationship)   H₁: ρ < 0 / > 0 / ≠ 0
H₀: ρ = 0 means no LINEAR relationship — a curved relationship could still exist with r ≈ 0. Always look at the scatterplot first. (Testing H₀: ρ = 0.5 needs Fisher's z — not in this course.)
Test statistic (rescales r into a t)
T = r·√(n−2) / √(1−r²) ~ tn−2 under H₀
Movies: r = −0.5091, n = 100, df = 98. T = (−0.5091·√98)/√(1−0.5091²) = −5.039/0.8607 = −5.86. H₁: ρ < 0 → left-sided. p = P(T ≤ −5.86) ≈ 3.2 × 10⁻⁸ ≪ 0.05 → reject H₀. "Strong evidence that older movies are shorter (negative linear relationship)."
H₁P-valueExcel
ρ < 0P(T ≤ t)=T.DIST(t; n-2; TRUE)
ρ > 0P(T ≥ t)=1-T.DIST(t; n-2; TRUE)
ρ ≠ 02·P(T ≥ |t|)=T.DIST.2T(ABS(t); n-2)
Assumptions: bivariate normality — X normal, Y normal, and the scatterplot roughly elliptical (not banana or funnel shaped). For n > 50 with no severe skew/outliers, the test is robust.
Outliers are lethal to r. A single extreme point can manufacture or destroy a correlation. Always inspect the scatterplot before trusting r.

SPSS: Analyze → Correlate → Bivariate → tick Pearson, choose one/two-tailed. Read r, N, and Sig.

20. Chi-Square Test of Independence H4.2

Both variables categorical — no means, no correlation. We test whether the distribution of one depends on the other, i.e. whether they're independent.

🌐 Internet providers X = provider (Belgacom / Telenet / Other), Y = employment (Blue collar / White collar / Self-employed), n = 100. Is provider choice independent of job type?
Hypotheses (always these)
H₀: X and Y are independent   H₁: X and Y are associated
Chi-square only detects whether an association exists — never its direction or strength. For that, read the table proportions or a stacked bar chart.

The test statistic KEY

Chi-square
χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E   (over all cells) ~ χ²(r−1)(k−1)
  • Under H₀: O ≈ E → χ² ≈ 0.
  • Reject H₀ when χ² is large (observed table strays far from independence).
  • Always one-sided right — χ² ≥ 0 by construction.
  • df = (r−1)(k−1); for a 3×3 table, df = 2×2 = 4.
Internet providers: χ² = 3.195, df = 4. p = CHISQ.DIST.RT(3.195; 4) = 0.526 ≫ 0.05 → do not reject H₀. "No significant evidence of an association between provider and employment status."

The one sentence to remember Every test in this chapter is the same trial: presume H₀, weigh the evidence, and only convict (reject) when the data would be genuinely surprising under innocence. Master that, and the formulas are just paperwork.
