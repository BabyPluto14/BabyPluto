P1.1 Frequentist Probability

Random Experiments & Sample Spaces

A random experiment is any process whose outcome is uncertain before it happens. Examples: rolling a die, inspecting a truck, checking whether a client files an insurance claim. No matter how carefully you control conditions, you cannot predict the exact outcome in advance.

The sample space Ω (capital omega) is the set of all possible outcomes. An event is any subset of Ω — a collection of outcomes you care about.

Example — Rolling a Die

Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. The event A = "rolling an even number" = {2, 4, 6}.

The event B = "rolling more than 4" = {5, 6}.

The Frequentist Definition

Suppose you repeat a random experiment n times and event B occurs k times. The relative frequency of B is k/n. As n grows very large, this ratio stabilises:

Frequentist (Long-Run) Definition
P(B) = lim[n→∞] (number of times B occurs) / n

This is an empirical definition — probability is something you can (in principle) measure by repeating the experiment many times. In practice we use observed frequencies as estimates: the truck factory's 10% defect rate is our best estimate of P(defective), not a definition carved in stone.

Key insight: Probability as a population proportion. Imagine the universe of all trucks this factory will ever produce. The probability of a defect = the proportion of defective trucks in that infinite population. The 40/400 from today is a sample estimate of that population proportion. This connects probability directly to the proportions you already know from descriptive statistics.

Probability as Population Proportion

This equivalence is powerful: whenever you have a large population and you pick one member at random, the probability that the member has property X equals the proportion of the population that has property X. So:

  • P(a randomly chosen Dutch adult is left-handed) ≈ 0.13 (13% of Dutch adults are left-handed)
  • P(a randomly chosen insurance claim exceeds €5,000) = fraction of claims above €5,000 in the historical data

No abstract philosophy needed — just a proportion of a (possibly hypothetical) population.

P1.2 Counting Rules
📖 Monday Afternoon — "How many combinations are there?" A client wants to insure a fleet of cars. Each car can have 3 engine sizes, 4 colour options, and 2 trim levels. "How many distinct configurations do we need pricing for?" Sam realises the answer isn't 3+4+2 = 9. You don't choose one feature OR another — you choose one AND another AND another. Sam needs the product rule.

The Product Rule (Multiplication Principle)

If a process has k stages and stage i has nᵢ choices (independent of other stages), the total number of outcomes is:

Product Rule
Total = n₁ × n₂ × · · · × nₖ

Sam's fleet: 3 × 4 × 2 = 24 configurations. Choosing an outfit from 7 shirts, 3 trousers, and 2 shoes gives 7 × 3 × 2 = 42 outfits.

Factorials

How many ways can you arrange n distinct objects in a row? The first slot has n choices, the second n-1, …, the last 1. That gives n! ("n factorial").

Factorial
n! = n × (n-1) × (n-2) × · · · × 2 × 1
Special rule: 0! = 1

Example: 5! = 5×4×3×2×1 = 120. The number of ways to arrange 5 books on a shelf is 120.

Does Order Matter? The Central Question

Almost every counting problem hinges on one question: does order matter?

Permutations — Order Matters

Choose k items from n, and the order of selection matters (ABC ≠ BAC).

Permutations
P(n, k) = n! / (n−k)!
Example — Race Podium

8 runners, 3 podium places (gold, silver, bronze). Order matters because gold ≠ silver.

P(8, 3) = 8! / 5! = 8 × 7 × 6 = 336 distinct podium outcomes.

Combinations — Order Does Not Matter

Choose k items from n, and the order does not matter ({A,B,C} = {C,B,A}).

Combinations (Binomial Coefficient)
C(n, k) = n! / [k! × (n−k)!] also written (n choose k) or ⁿCₖ
Example — Concert Tickets

4 friends, 2 tickets, no assigned seats — who goes? {Alice, Bob} is the same as {Bob, Alice}.

C(4, 2) = 4! / (2! × 2!) = 24/4 = 6 combinations.

Example — The Lottery (Why You Won't Win)

Dutch lottery: choose 6 numbers from 49. Order doesn't matter.

C(49, 6) = 49!/(6! × 43!) = 13,983,816 ≈ 14 million possible tickets. Your one ticket has probability 1/13,983,816 ≈ 0.0000000715.

Choosing from Two Groups

Sometimes you select from two distinct groups simultaneously (e.g. 3 men and 2 women from a board of 10 men and 8 women). The rule is to multiply the separate combination counts:

Two-Group Selection
C(n₁, k₁) × C(n₂, k₂)

A committee of 3 men and 2 women from 10 men and 8 women: C(10,3) × C(8,2) = 120 × 28 = 3,360 committees.

P1.3 Kolmogorov Axioms & Probability Rules
📖 Tuesday Morning — "Can I just make up any numbers?" Sam's colleague claims: "I think there's a 60% chance a client renews their policy and a 70% chance they don't." Sam instinctively knows something is wrong — those numbers add up to 130%. But why exactly is that forbidden? The answer lies in the three axioms that define what a "valid" probability is.

The Three Kolmogorov Axioms

In 1933, Andrei Kolmogorov formalised probability with three simple rules that any valid probability assignment must satisfy:

Axiom 1 — Non-negativity

P(A) ≥ 0 for every event A. Probabilities cannot be negative.

Axiom 2 — Normalisation

P(Ω) = 1. Something in the sample space must happen — certainty has probability 1.

Axiom 3 — Additivity

If A and B are disjoint (mutually exclusive, A∩B = ∅), then P(A∪B) = P(A) + P(B).

Everything else in probability theory follows from these three axioms. Let's derive the most useful results:

The Complement Rule

Complement Rule
P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A)

Why: A and Aᶜ are disjoint (can't both happen) and together cover all of Ω. By Axioms 2 and 3: P(A) + P(Aᶜ) = P(Ω) = 1.

Sam's colleague's error: if P(renews) = 0.60 then P(doesn't renew) = 1 − 0.60 = 0.40, not 0.70.

Monotonicity

If A ⊆ B (A is contained in B), then P(A) ≤ P(B). Intuitively: B can happen in all the ways A can, plus more. This also confirms 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1 for any event A.

The General Addition Rule

For any two events A and B (not necessarily disjoint):

General Addition Rule
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
Special case (disjoint): P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)

The subtraction of P(A∩B) corrects for double-counting — the outcomes where both A and B occur get counted twice if you just add P(A) + P(B).

Example — Pets (Venn Diagram)

In a neighbourhood: 40% of households have a dog, 30% have a cat, 15% have both.

P(dog or cat) = P(D) + P(C) − P(D∩C) = 0.40 + 0.30 − 0.15 = 0.55

P(neither) = 1 − 0.55 = 0.45

The Rule of Laplace (Equally Likely Outcomes)

When all outcomes in Ω are equally likely (e.g., a fair die, random sampling), probability simplifies to counting:

Laplace's Rule
P(A) = (number of outcomes in A) / (total number of outcomes in Ω)
Warning — Equally Likely Required! Laplace's rule only works when all outcomes are equally likely. Don't apply it to a loaded die, a biased coin, or real-world populations where some outcomes are more common than others.
P1.4 Conditional Probability & Independence
📖 Tuesday Afternoon — "Knowing one thing changes everything" Sam is pricing health insurance. The probability that a random client files a claim next year is 20%. But Sam learns that this particular client is a 70-year-old smoker. Suddenly 20% feels completely wrong. Knowing information about the client changes the probability estimate. This is conditional probability — and it's the engine behind almost all of insurance.

Conditional Probability

P(A|B) — read "the probability of A given B" — is the probability of A once you know that B has already occurred. You've restricted your universe from all of Ω to just the part where B is true.

Conditional Probability
P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) [valid when P(B) > 0]
Example — Two Dice (Full 6×6 Grid)

Roll two fair dice. Define A = "sum is 8" and B = "first die shows 4".

There are 36 equally-likely outcomes. Outcomes where B holds: {(4,1),(4,2),(4,3),(4,4),(4,5),(4,6)} — six outcomes. Of these, A∩B = {(4,4)} — only one outcome gives sum 8.

P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B) = (1/36) / (6/36) = 1/6 ≈ 0.167

Compare with P(A) = 5/36 ≈ 0.139. Knowing the first die is 4 raises the probability of a sum of 8.

The General Multiplication Rule

Rearranging the conditional probability formula gives a useful rule for computing intersection probabilities:

General Multiplication Rule
P(A ∩ B) = P(B) · P(A | B) = P(A) · P(B | A)

Statistical Independence

A and B are statistically independent if knowing B tells you nothing about A:

Independence (Three Equivalent Definitions)
P(A | B) = P(A) P(B | A) = P(B) P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B) ← the most useful form

When events are independent, intersection probability is simply the product of individual probabilities. This is what Sam's colleague needed for the truck example — but independence is an assumption that must be justified, not just assumed.

The Prosecutor's Fallacy — Sally Clark Case (1999)
Sally Clark was convicted of murdering her two infant sons after a paediatrician testified that the probability of two children dying of SIDS in the same family was 1 in 73 million (1/8,500 × 1/8,500, assuming independence). This was catastrophically wrong for two reasons:
  1. Independence was unjustified — SIDS has genetic and environmental components, so a second SIDS death in the same family is more likely than in the general population. You cannot simply multiply.
  2. The Prosecutor's Fallacy: P(two SIDS deaths | innocent) ≠ P(innocent | two SIDS deaths). The relevant question for the jury was the latter, which also requires knowing the base rate of double infant murder.
Sally Clark was eventually acquitted after three years in prison. The lesson: always question the assumption of independence.

Discrete Random Variables

Moving from events to numbers: a random variable assigns a numerical value to each outcome. Part II covers probability distributions, expected value, variance, and the all-important Binomial distribution.

📖 Wednesday — "How do I summarise an entire distribution in two numbers?" Sam's new task: a car dealership wants flood insurance. Sam needs to model how many cars a dealership sells per week. The dealership says: "Sometimes 0, usually 1 or 2, rarely 3." Sam needs a way to capture this whole story mathematically — a probability distribution. And then to compress that distribution down to two key numbers: the average and the spread.
P2.1 Probability Distributions

What is a Random Variable?

A random variable X is a function that assigns a number to each outcome in Ω. It converts a random experiment into a number we can calculate with. X is discrete if it can only take a countable set of values (0, 1, 2, …).

Probability Distribution

The probability distribution of a discrete RV lists all possible values and their probabilities, usually in a table:

x (number of cars sold)0123
P(X = x)0.100.350.55?
Wait — where is P(X=3)? Since all probabilities must sum to 1: P(X=3) = 1 − (0.10 + 0.35 + 0.55) = 1 − 1.00 = 0. There are only 0, 1, or 2 cars sold. This is how you find a missing probability from a table.

Validity Rules for a Probability Distribution

  • All probabilities are between 0 and 1: 0 ≤ P(X = k) ≤ 1 for all k
  • All probabilities sum to exactly 1: Σ P(X = k) = 1
  • All listed outcomes are mutually exclusive (each trial has exactly one outcome)

Visualising with a Needle (Spike) Graph

Discrete distributions are visualised with vertical spikes (needles) at each value, with height equal to the probability. Unlike a bar chart, the spikes have no width — reminding us that only specific integer values are possible, not anything in between.

Example — Russian Roulette Geometry

A theoretical (morbid but instructive) example: a revolver has 5 empty chambers and 1 bullet. Players take turns. Let Y = the turn on which the bullet fires. P(Y = k) = (5/6)^(k-1) × (1/6). This is a geometric distribution — each trial is independent with the same probability of "success" (firing).

P(Y=1) = 1/6 ≈ 0.167  |  P(Y=2) = 5/36 ≈ 0.139  |  P(Y=3) = 25/216 ≈ 0.116  …

P2.2 Expected Value & Variance

Expected Value E(X) — The Long-Run Average

The expected value (or population mean, μ) is the weighted average of all possible values, where the weights are the probabilities:

Expected Value
E(X) = μ = Σ k · P(X = k)

The expected value is the value you'd predict on average if the experiment were repeated infinitely many times. It need not be a value X can actually take — a fair die has E(X) = 3.5, which you can never roll.

Example — Cars per Week

Using our dealership distribution (assuming P(X=2)=0.35 as the corrected table for illustration):

With the corrected table {P(0)=0.10, P(1)=0.35, P(2)=0.55, P(3)=0}: E(X) = 0×0.10 + 1×0.35 + 2×0.55 + 3×0 = 0 + 0.35 + 1.10 + 0 = 1.45 cars/week

Variance and Standard Deviation

Variance Var(X) = σ² measures how spread out the distribution is around the mean. The shortcut formula is easier to compute than the definition:

Variance (Shortcut Formula)
Var(X) = E(X²) − [E(X)]² where E(X²) = Σ k² · P(X = k) σ = √Var(X) (standard deviation)
Example — Two Dice Sum

Sum S of two fair dice. E(S) = 7 (by symmetry — the distribution is symmetric around 7).

E(S²) = Σ s² × P(S=s) = 4×(1/36) + 9×(2/36) + … + 49×(6/36) + … ≈ 54.83

Var(S) = 54.83 − 7² = 54.83 − 49 = 5.83, so σ ≈ 2.42

Special Cases: Bernoulli Distribution

A Bernoulli random variable takes only two values: 1 (success, probability p) or 0 (failure, probability 1−p). It models a single yes/no trial.

Bernoulli(p)
E(X) = p Var(X) = p(1−p) [Variance is maximised at p=0.5 and equals 0 at p=0 or p=1]
Insight — The Bridge from Sample to Population. When you compute a sample mean x̄ and sample variance s², you're estimating the population quantities E(X) and Var(X). The probability distribution of X is the population — and E(X) is the population mean you're trying to estimate with your sample.
P2.3 Binomial Distribution
📖 Wednesday Afternoon — "What are the chances exactly 7 of our 50 clients will claim?" Sam is modelling a portfolio of 50 health insurance clients, each with probability 0.13 of filing a claim. "What's the probability that exactly 7 clients claim?" Sam can't just multiply — the clients claiming in different combinations all lead to the same count of 7. This is exactly what the Binomial distribution handles.

When to Use Binomial

X ~ Bin(n, p) when ALL four conditions hold:

  • Fixed number of trials n (decided before the experiment)
  • Each trial has exactly 2 outcomes: success or failure
  • Constant probability p of success on every trial
  • Trials are independent (outcome of one doesn't affect others)

Binomial Probability Formula

Binomial PMF
P(X = k) = C(n,k) · pᵏ · (1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ for k = 0, 1, 2, …, n E(X) = np Var(X) = np(1−p) σ = √[np(1−p)]

The formula has three parts: C(n,k) counts the number of ways to get exactly k successes in n trials; pᵏ is the probability of k successes; (1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ is the probability of the n−k failures.

Example — Left-Handed Students, X ~ Bin(50, 0.13)

13% of people are left-handed. In a class of 50, what's the probability exactly 7 are left-handed?

P(X=7) = C(50,7) × 0.13⁷ × 0.87⁴³ = 99,884,400 × 0.0000006… × 0.0025… ≈ 0.1189 (≈ 12%)

E(X) = 50 × 0.13 = 6.5 students  |  Var(X) = 50 × 0.13 × 0.87 = 5.655  |  σ ≈ 2.38

Excel: BINOM.DIST

Excel Functions
=BINOM.DIST(k; n; p; FALSE) → P(X = k) [exact probability] =BINOM.DIST(k; n; p; TRUE) → P(X ≤ k) [cumulative probability] To get P(X ≥ k): use 1 − BINOM.DIST(k−1; n; p; TRUE) To get P(a ≤ X ≤ b): use BINOM.DIST(b;n;p;TRUE) − BINOM.DIST(a−1;n;p;TRUE)

Shape of the Binomial Distribution

p = 0.5 → Symmetric

Equal probability of success and failure. The distribution is perfectly balanced around np.

p < 0.5 → Right-Skewed

Successes are rare, so the distribution clusters near 0 with a long right tail.

p > 0.5 → Left-Skewed

Successes are common, so the distribution clusters near n with a long left tail.

Large n → Bell-Shaped

As n grows (and np ≥ 5, n(1−p) ≥ 5), the binomial approaches a normal distribution.

Part III

Continuous Random Variables

When variables can take any value in an interval — heights, weights, waiting times, temperatures — discrete distributions no longer work. Part III introduces density functions and how to compute probabilities as areas.

📖 Thursday Morning — "The coffee machine problem" Sam is pricing insurance for a chain of coffee shops. One risk: the automatic coffee machines sometimes pour too much or too little coffee, angering customers. The fill volume isn't one of a handful of values — it's continuous. Sam needs to model the probability that a cup gets less than 240 ml (minimum customer tolerance) or more than 260 ml (overflow) — using a density function.
P3.1 Density Functions & Continuous Probabilities

From Histogram to Density Curve

Imagine collecting data on fill volumes: 240.3 ml, 251.7 ml, 248.1 ml, … No two measurements are exactly the same. As you collect more data and make the histogram bins narrower, the histogram smooths out into a density curve.

Key Insight: Probability = Area

For a continuous random variable, probability is area under the density curve, not the height. The height f(x) is the density — it tells you where the probability is concentrated, not how much of it there is at any exact point.

Core Rule for Continuous RVs
P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = area under f(x) between a and b P(X = a) = 0 for any single point a P(X ≤ a) = P(X < a) [strict vs. non-strict inequalities are equal!]
f(x) CAN exceed 1! Density is not probability. The only constraint is that the total area under f(x) equals 1, and that f(x) ≥ 0 everywhere. A density function can spike above 1 over a narrow region as long as the total area stays 1.

The Uniform Distribution U[a, b]

The simplest continuous distribution: all values in [a, b] are equally likely. The density is flat (constant).

Uniform U[a, b]
f(x) = 1/(b−a) for a ≤ x ≤ b, 0 elsewhere P(c ≤ X ≤ d) = (d−c) / (b−a)
Example — Metro Arrival U[0, 10]

You arrive at a metro station. The next train comes uniformly in [0, 10] minutes. X ~ U[0, 10].

P(wait < 3 min) = (3−0)/(10−0) = 0.30

P(3 ≤ wait ≤ 7) = (7−3)/(10−0) = 0.40

P3.2 Mean & Standard Deviation of Continuous RVs

Expected Value — The Balance Point

For a continuous RV, expected value is defined by an integral (the continuous analogue of the discrete weighted sum):

Expected Value (Continuous)
E(X) = ∫ x · f(x) dx (integral over all x)

Geometrically, E(X) is the balance point of the density curve — the x-value where the distribution would balance if it were a physical shape cut out of uniform material.

Shortcut: Symmetric Distributions

For any symmetric distribution, E(X) = median = centre of symmetry. No integration needed! This is why E(X) = (a+b)/2 for U[a,b] and E(X) = μ for the Normal distribution.

Uniform Distribution Formulas

U[a, b] — Mean and Variance
E(X) = (a + b) / 2 [midpoint of the interval] Var(X) = (b − a)² / 12 [spread depends on width] σ = (b − a) / √12 = (b − a) / (2√3)
Example — Metro Wait U[0, 10]

E(X) = (0 + 10)/2 = 5 minutes

Var(X) = (10−0)²/12 = 100/12 ≈ 8.33  |  σ = √8.33 ≈ 2.89 minutes

The Normal Distribution

The bell curve that appears everywhere in nature, measurement, and statistics. Part IV covers the normal distribution's properties, Excel calculations, Z-scores, and how to assess normality.

📖 Thursday Afternoon — "IQ scores, pregnancies, and coffee packs" Sam has three insurance problems in the afternoon: (1) A client insures a school — how many students score above 120 on IQ tests? (2) An obstetrics clinic wants coverage for premature births. (3) A supermarket chain wants to ensure their coffee-pack machine fills correctly. All three involve the same shape: a bell curve. Sam needs to master the Normal distribution.
P4.1 The Normal Distribution N(μ, σ)

Properties of the Normal Distribution

X ~ N(μ, σ) (note: σ is the standard deviation, not variance) has these key properties:

  • Bell-shaped and perfectly symmetric around μ
  • Extends infinitely in both directions (though probabilities become negligible beyond ±4σ)
  • Completely determined by just two parameters: mean μ and standard deviation σ
  • E(X) = μ (the mean equals the centre of symmetry)
  • Var(X) = σ², SD = σ

The 68-95-99.7 Rule (Empirical Rule)

68-95-99.7 Rule
P(μ − σ ≤ X ≤ μ + σ) ≈ 68% [within 1 SD] P(μ − 2σ ≤ X ≤ μ + 2σ) ≈ 95% [within 2 SD] P(μ − 3σ ≤ X ≤ μ + 3σ) ≈ 99.7% [within 3 SD]

Excel: Computing Normal Probabilities

Excel Functions for N(μ, σ)
=NORM.DIST(x; μ; σ; TRUE) → P(X ≤ x) [CDF at x] =NORM.INV(p; μ; σ) → x such that P(X ≤ x) = p [quantile] P(X > x) = 1 − NORM.DIST(x; μ; σ; TRUE) P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = NORM.DIST(b;μ;σ;TRUE) − NORM.DIST(a;μ;σ;TRUE)
Example — IQ Scores, X ~ N(100, 15)

IQ scores are approximately normal with mean 100, SD 15.

P(X > 120) = 1 − NORM.DIST(120; 100; 15; TRUE) ≈ 9.1% (about 1 in 11 people)

P(80 ≤ X ≤ 120) = NORM.DIST(120;100;15;TRUE) − NORM.DIST(80;100;15;TRUE) ≈ 81.8%

P(X < 85) = NORM.DIST(85; 100; 15; TRUE) ≈ 15.9% (roughly 1 SD below the mean)

Example — Pregnancy Length, X ~ N(266, 16) days

Human gestation is approximately N(266, 16) days. A birth before 250 days is considered premature.

P(premature) = P(X < 250) = NORM.DIST(250; 266; 16; TRUE) ≈ 15.9%

The 5th percentile: NORM.INV(0.05; 266; 16) ≈ 239.7 days (about 34 weeks)

P4.2 Standardizing, Z-Scores & QQ-Plots

The Z-Score: Standardizing a Normal Variable

Any normal variable X ~ N(μ, σ) can be converted to a standard normal Z ~ N(0, 1) by subtracting the mean and dividing by the SD:

Z-Score (Standardization)
Z = (X − μ) / σ Z tells you: "how many standard deviations is X away from the mean?" Z > 0 → above the mean Z < 0 → below the mean

Z-scores let you compare values from different normal distributions. An IQ of 130 (Z = +2) and a coffee pack at 260g when μ=250, σ=5 (also Z = +2) are both equally extreme relative to their distributions.

Example — Z-Score Exercises with X ~ N(μ, σ)

Q: If X ~ N(50, 8), find P(X > 62).

Z = (62 − 50)/8 = 12/8 = 1.5.    P(Z > 1.5) = 1 − Φ(1.5) ≈ 1 − 0.9332 = 0.0668

Or directly: 1 − NORM.DIST(62; 50; 8; TRUE) = 0.0668.

Working Backwards: Finding μ from a Target Probability

Sam's coffee machine problem: the machine fills packs with SD σ = 5g. We want P(underfill, i.e., X < 250) ≤ 1%. What should we set the mean fill μ to?

Calibration Formula
P(X < c) = p → c = μ + σ · z_p → μ = c − σ · z_p where z_p = NORM.INV(p; 0; 1) is the p-th quantile of N(0,1)
Coffee Machine Calibration

We want P(X < 250) = 0.01. The 1st percentile of N(0,1): z₀.₀₁ = NORM.INV(0.01; 0; 1) ≈ −2.326.

μ = 250 − 5 × (−2.326) = 250 + 11.63 = 261.63g

Or using the specific value in the study guides: μ ≈ 253.49g when using a 5% underfill tolerance.

Interpretation: set the machine mean to 253.49g so that only 5% of packs are under 250g.

Assessing Normality: QQ-Plots

A QQ-plot (quantile-quantile plot) compares the quantiles of your data against the theoretical quantiles of a normal distribution. If the data is normal, the points fall on a straight diagonal line.

QQ-Plot PatternWhat It MeansAction
Straight diagonal lineData is approximately normalNormal methods valid
Curves up at high endRight skew (long right tail)Consider log transform
Curves down at low endLeft skew (long left tail)Consider reflection + log
S-shape (both ends deviate)Heavy tails (more extreme values than normal)Use robust methods
Inverted S-shapeLight tails (data more clustered than normal)Often fine for large n

The Log Transformation

For right-skewed data, taking logarithms often produces a near-normal distribution. If log(X) ~ N(μ, σ), then X follows a lognormal distribution. Insurance claim amounts are a classic example: a few very large claims create a long right tail, but log(claim amount) tends to be approximately normal.

Tip: In Excel: if your data is right-skewed, create a new column with =LN(data), check if that is more normally distributed on a QQ-plot, and perform your normal-distribution calculations on the log-transformed values.

Sums, Averages & the Central Limit Theorem

What happens when you add or average many random variables? The answers unlock why the normal distribution is so universal — and why statistics works at all.

📖 Friday — "The most important theorem in statistics" Sam's final challenge of the week: ActuRisk insures an office building with 24 air conditioners. Each AC unit draws a random amount of power. "What's the probability the total power draw exceeds 120 kW?" No single distribution governs the total — but Sam has one powerful tool: the Central Limit Theorem. And it doesn't matter what distribution the individual units follow.
P5.1 Sums & Averages of Random Variables

Setting Up: Independent Identically Distributed (i.i.d.)

Suppose X₁, X₂, …, Xₙ are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) — each has the same distribution with mean μ and variance σ². Define:

  • Sum: S = X₁ + X₂ + … + Xₙ
  • Sample average: X̄ = S/n = (X₁ + … + Xₙ)/n

Properties of the Sum S

Sum S = X₁ + X₂ + … + Xₙ
E(S) = n · μ Var(S) = n · σ² SD(S) = √n · σ

Variance adds linearly for independent RVs. Standard deviation grows as √n — not as n.

Properties of the Sample Average X̄

Average X̄ = S / n
E(X̄) = μ [unbiased: the average of averages = the population mean] Var(X̄) = σ² / n SD(X̄) = σ / √n [also called the Standard Error of the Mean]
The √n effect: To halve the standard deviation of X̄ (i.e., halve the uncertainty in your estimate), you must quadruple the sample size. Going from n=100 to n=400 halves SD(X̄). This is why large samples are so valuable — and why there are diminishing returns to increasing sample size.

Critical Distinction: W = X₁ + … + X₅ vs. 5X

Common Mistake: W = X₁ + X₂ + X₃ + X₄ + X₅ (sum of FIVE DIFFERENT trials) is NOT the same as 5X (ONE trial multiplied by 5).
QuantityE(·)Var(·)SD(·)
X (single trial)μσ²σ
5X (1 trial × 5)5μ25σ²5σ
W = X₁+X₂+X₃+X₄+X₅ (5 separate trials)5μ5σ²√5 · σ

The means are the same but the variances are very different. W has much less variance than 5X because the five independent draws partially cancel each other out. 5X just scales up the randomness.

Example — Air Conditioning Units (ActuRisk)

24 AC units, each drawing power X ~ (some distribution) with μ = 4.8 kW, σ = 1.4 kW. Total power S = X₁ + … + X₂₄.

E(S) = 24 × 4.8 = 115.2 kW

Var(S) = 24 × 1.4² = 24 × 1.96 = 47.04  |  SD(S) = √47.04 ≈ 6.86 kW

By the CLT (n=24 is reasonably large): S ≈ N(115.2, 6.86)

P(S ≥ 120) = 1 − NORM.DIST(120; 115.2; 6.86; TRUE) ≈ 1 − 0.7580 ≈ 0.242 ≈ 24.2%

P5.2 The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

Statement of the CLT

Central Limit Theorem
If X₁, X₂, …, Xₙ are i.i.d. with mean μ and finite variance σ², then: X̄ ≈ N(μ, σ/√n) for large n (rule of thumb: n ≥ 30) S ≈ N(nμ, √n · σ) for large n This holds REGARDLESS of the original distribution of Xᵢ.

This is arguably the most important result in all of statistics. It says: no matter how weird the distribution of individual observations is, the average of many observations follows a bell curve.

How Does it Work? A Simulation Story

Three Key Scenarios

Exactly Normal (any n)

If each Xᵢ ~ N(μ, σ), then X̄ is exactly N(μ, σ/√n) for any sample size n. No approximation needed.

Approximately Normal (n ≥ 30)

For any distribution with finite variance, X̄ is approximately normal when n ≥ 30. The approximation improves with n. More skewed distributions need larger n.

Binomial Special Case

If Xᵢ ~ Bernoulli(p), then S = X₁+…+Xₙ ~ Bin(n,p) exactly. The CLT gives the normal approximation: Bin(n,p) ≈ N(np, √np(1−p)) when np ≥ 5 AND n(1−p) ≥ 5.

Checking CLT Conditions

1. Observations are independent
2. Observations are identically distributed (from the same population)
3. n ≥ 30 (or exact normality, or large-n Binomial)

Example — GPA Averages (CLT Applied)

University GPA has μ = 6.5, σ = 1.2 (on a 10-point scale). A random sample of n = 36 students.

By CLT: X̄ ~ N(6.5, 1.2/√36) = N(6.5, 0.2)

P(X̄ > 7.0) = 1 − NORM.DIST(7.0; 6.5; 0.2; TRUE) = 1 − Φ(2.5) ≈ 0.0062 (0.62%)

P(6.2 ≤ X̄ ≤ 6.8) = NORM.DIST(6.8; 6.5; 0.2; TRUE) − NORM.DIST(6.2; 6.5; 0.2; TRUE) ≈ 86.6%

Example — Binomial Normal Approximation

X ~ Bin(200, 0.40). Check: np = 80 ≥ 5 ✓; n(1−p) = 120 ≥ 5 ✓. CLT applies.

X ≈ N(μ=80, σ=√(200×0.4×0.6)) = N(80, √48) = N(80, 6.93)

P(X ≤ 90) ≈ NORM.DIST(90; 80; 6.93; TRUE) ≈ 0.926

(Exact binomial gives 0.928 — excellent approximation!)

Why does the CLT matter so much? Most statistical tests (t-tests, ANOVA, regression coefficients) rely on the sampling distribution of averages or sums being approximately normal. The CLT guarantees this for large enough n — regardless of the underlying data distribution. This is why parametric tests are so broadly applicable.
