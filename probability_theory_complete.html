1. The Story — A Week at ActuRisk Insurance

The Setting

Sam just started as a junior actuary at ActuRisk Insurance Co., a firm that prices risk for everything from car fleets to cargo ships. On the first day, Sam's manager drops a thick manual on the desk: "Before you can price anything, you need to think like a probabilist." Over the next five days, every concept in this guide arrives through a real problem Sam has to solve.

Monday — "What does 'probability' even mean?" (P1.1–P1.2)

First task: a toy-truck factory produces 400 trucks per day and 40 come out defective. P(defect) = 40/400 = 0.10 — simple enough. But then: "How many insurance configurations do we need to price if each truck can have 3 engine sizes, 4 colours, and 2 trim levels?" Sam writes 3+4+2 = 9 and gets corrected immediately. It's 3 × 4 × 2 = 24. The product rule and combinations become essential tools before lunch.

Tuesday — "Can I just make up any probability numbers?" (P1.3–P1.4)

A colleague claims: "I think there's a 60% chance a client renews and a 70% chance they don't." Sam instinctively knows something is wrong — those sum to 130%. The answer lies in Kolmogorov's axioms. Then the afternoon brings the most important warning in the chapter: the Prosecutor's Fallacy. The Sally Clark case (1999) shows how assuming independence where none exists can send an innocent person to prison.

Wednesday — "How do I summarise a whole distribution in two numbers?" (P2)

Sam models how many cars a dealership sells per week and needs to compress the whole distribution into two numbers: the expected value (long-run average) and the standard deviation (spread). Then comes the Binomial distribution: in a class of 50 students, 13% are left-handed — what's the probability exactly 7 are? The answer isn't a simple proportion; it requires C(50,7) × 0.13⁷ × 0.87⁴³.

Thursday — "The coffee machine problem" (P3)

Sam now models coffee-pack fill volumes — a continuous variable where no two measurements are exactly the same. Probability becomes area under a density curve, and the normal distribution governs almost everything: IQ scores (μ=100, σ=15), pregnancy lengths (μ=266, σ=16 days), pack weights. The afternoon's puzzle: calibrate a coffee machine so only 1% of packs are underfilled below 250g. Answer: set μ = 250 − σ × NORM.INV(0.01; 0; 1) ≈ 261.6g.

Friday — "The most important theorem in statistics" (P4)

ActuRisk insures an office building with 24 air conditioners. What's the probability total power draw exceeds 120 kW? No single distribution governs the sum — but the Central Limit Theorem guarantees that when you add or average many independent observations, the result is approximately normal, regardless of what distribution each unit follows. This theorem is why almost all of statistics works. Sam ends the week understanding why the bell curve shows up everywhere.

The thread running through all five days: probability starts as counting, grows into formal axioms, becomes a tool for modelling individual random outcomes (distributions), and finally — through the CLT — explains why averages always converge to the normal distribution. Each concept builds directly on the last.
P1.1 Frequentist Probability & Sample Spaces

2. Random Experiments, Outcomes & Events

A random experiment is any process whose outcome is uncertain before it happens — rolling a die, measuring a truck, observing whether a client files an insurance claim. Three building blocks:

  • Sample space Ω — the set of all possible outcomes. Rolling a die: Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.
  • Event A — any subset of Ω you care about. A = "even number" = {2, 4, 6}.
  • Complement Aᶜ — everything in Ω that is not in A. Aᶜ = "odd number" = {1, 3, 5}.
Frequentist (Long-Run) Definition
P(B) = lim[n→∞] (number of times B occurs) / n

As you repeat the experiment more and more times, the relative frequency stabilises at the true probability. The truck factory's 40/400 = 10% defect rate is a sample estimate of P(defective) in the infinite population of all trucks this factory could ever produce.

Probability = population proportion. Whenever you pick one member at random from a large population, the probability that the member has property X equals the proportion of the population with property X. This connects probability directly to the proportions from descriptive statistics — there is no philosophical gap between them.
P1.2 Counting Rules — Product Rule, Permutations & Combinations

3. The Product Rule (Multiplication Principle)

If a process has k independent stages with n₁, n₂, …, nₖ choices at each stage, the total number of outcomes is their product:

Product Rule
Total = n₁ × n₂ × · · · × nₖ

Outfit example: 7 shirts × 3 trousers × 2 shoes = 42 outfits. The truck insurance: 3 × 4 × 2 = 24 configurations.

Factorials

The number of ways to arrange n distinct objects in a sequence is n! ("n factorial") — the first slot has n choices, the second n−1, down to 1.

Factorial
n! = n × (n−1) × · · · × 2 × 1     (special case: 0! = 1)

4. Does Order Matter? KEY

The central question for every counting problem:

Permutations — order matters
P(n, k) = n! / (n−k)!

Example — Race podium: 8 runners, 3 medals (gold ≠ silver ≠ bronze).
P(8, 3) = 8 × 7 × 6 = 336 arrangements

Combinations — order doesn't matter
C(n, k) = n! / [k! × (n−k)!]

Example — Concert tickets: 4 friends, 2 tickets, no assigned seats.
C(4, 2) = 4!/(2!×2!) = 6 combinations

The Lottery — Why You Won't Win: Dutch lottery: choose 6 from 49. C(49, 6) = 49!/(6!×43!) = 13,983,816 ≈ 14 million possible tickets. Your one ticket has probability 1/13,983,816 ≈ 0.000000072.

Choosing from Two Groups

Select k₁ items from group 1 (size n₁) AND k₂ from group 2 (size n₂): multiply the separate combination counts.

Two-Group Selection
C(n₁, k₁) × C(n₂, k₂)

A committee of 3 men + 2 women from 10 men and 8 women: C(10,3) × C(8,2) = 120 × 28 = 3,360 committees.

Excel: =COMBIN(n; k) for combinations  |  =PERMUT(n; k) for permutations  |  =FACT(n) for n!
P1.3 Kolmogorov Axioms, Complement & Addition Rule

5. The Three Axioms — What Makes a Valid Probability?

In 1933, Andrei Kolmogorov gave probability its mathematical foundation with three axioms that any valid probability assignment must satisfy. Everything else in probability theory is derived from these three rules alone.

Axiom 1 — Non-negativity

P(A) ≥ 0 for every event A. Probabilities cannot be negative.

Axiom 2 — Normalisation

P(Ω) = 1. Something in the sample space must happen — certainty has probability 1.

Axiom 3 — Additivity

If A and B are disjoint (mutually exclusive), then P(A∪B) = P(A) + P(B).

Sam's colleague's error: "60% chance a client renews, 70% chance they don't." These sum to 130% — impossible. By Axioms 2 and 3, the two complementary events must sum to exactly 1. The correct numbers: P(renews) = 0.60, P(doesn't renew) = 0.40.

6. Derived Rules

Complement Rule
P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A)

Why: A and Aᶜ are disjoint and together cover all of Ω. By Axioms 2 and 3: P(A) + P(Aᶜ) = 1.

Monotonicity
If A ⊆ B, then P(A) ≤ P(B)

B can occur in all the ways A can, plus more. This also proves 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1.

General Addition Rule
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) Special case (disjoint events): P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)

The subtraction of P(A∩B) corrects for double-counting: outcomes where both A and B occur get counted twice if you simply add P(A) + P(B).

Pets example: 40% of households have a dog, 30% have a cat, 15% have both.
P(dog or cat) = 0.40 + 0.30 − 0.15 = 0.55  |  P(neither) = 1 − 0.55 = 0.45

Rule of Laplace (Equally Likely Outcomes Only)

Laplace's Rule — use only when all outcomes are equally likely
P(A) = (number of outcomes in A) / (total outcomes in Ω)
Laplace's rule fails for loaded dice, biased coins, or any real-world population where some outcomes are more common than others. Always verify equal likelihood before applying it.
P1.4 Conditional Probability & Independence

7. Conditional Probability — Updating with New Information KEY

P(A | B) — "the probability of A given B" — is the probability of A once you know B has occurred. You restrict your universe from all of Ω to just the part where B is true.

Conditional Probability
P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)     [requires P(B) > 0]
Two-dice example: Roll two fair dice. Let A = "sum is 8", B = "first die shows 4".
Outcomes where B holds: {(4,1),(4,2),(4,3),(4,4),(4,5),(4,6)} — 6 out of 36.
Outcomes where A∩B holds: {(4,4)} — 1 out of 36.
P(A | B) = (1/36) / (6/36) = 1/6 ≈ 0.167   vs.   P(A) = 5/36 ≈ 0.139.
Knowing the first die is 4 raises the probability of a sum of 8 — the information matters.
General Multiplication Rule (rearranging the formula)
P(A ∩ B) = P(B) · P(A | B) = P(A) · P(B | A)

8. Statistical Independence KEY

A and B are statistically independent if knowing B provides no information about A:

Three Equivalent Definitions of Independence
P(A | B) = P(A) P(B | A) = P(B) P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B)   ← the most useful form for calculations
Independence must be justified physically or by context — it cannot simply be assumed because it would make calculation convenient.

9. The Prosecutor's Fallacy — A Deadly Error EXAM

Sally Clark Case (UK, 1999) — A cautionary tale about independence

Sally Clark was convicted of murdering her two infant sons after a paediatrician testified that the probability of two SIDS deaths in the same family was 1 in 73 million (= 1/8,500 × 1/8,500, assuming independence). This was catastrophically wrong for two reasons:
  1. Independence was unjustified. SIDS has genetic and environmental components. A second SIDS death in the same family is far more likely than in the general population. You cannot simply multiply.
  2. The Prosecutor's Fallacy: P(evidence | innocent) ≠ P(innocent | evidence). The jury needed the second probability, not the first. The relevant comparison is: "How likely is this family to be guilty given the deaths?" — which also requires the base rate of double murder.
Sally Clark was acquitted after three years in prison. The paediatrician was subsequently struck off. Never assume independence without evidence for it.
P2.1 Discrete Probability Distributions

10. Random Variables & Probability Distributions

A random variable X is a function that assigns a number to each outcome in Ω. It converts a random experiment into something we can calculate with. X is discrete if it can only take a countable set of values (0, 1, 2, …).

The probability distribution lists all possible values and their probabilities:

x (cars sold per week)0123
P(X = x)0.100.350.55?
Finding a missing probability: All probabilities must sum to 1. P(X=3) = 1 − (0.10 + 0.35 + 0.55) = 1 − 1.00 = 0. There are only 0, 1, or 2 cars sold.

Validity Rules — Three Requirements

  • All probabilities are non-negative: 0 ≤ P(X = k) ≤ 1 for all k
  • All probabilities sum to exactly 1: Σ P(X = k) = 1
  • All listed outcomes are mutually exclusive (every trial has exactly one outcome)
Visualisation — Needle (Spike) Graph: Discrete distributions use vertical spikes at each value with height equal to the probability. Unlike a bar chart, the spikes have no width — emphasising that only specific integer values are possible, not anything in between.
P2.2 Expected Value & Variance

11. Expected Value E(X) — The Long-Run Average

Expected Value (Population Mean μ)
E(X) = μ = Σ k · P(X = k)

The expected value is the weighted average of all possible values, where the weights are the probabilities. It equals the value you'd predict on average if the experiment were repeated infinitely. It need not be a value X can actually take — a fair die has E(X) = 3.5.

Cars per week example: With P(0)=0.10, P(1)=0.35, P(2)=0.55:
E(X) = 0×0.10 + 1×0.35 + 2×0.55 = 0 + 0.35 + 1.10 = 1.45 cars/week

12. Variance & Standard Deviation

Variance — Shortcut Formula
Var(X) = E(X²) − [E(X)]² where E(X²) = Σ k² · P(X = k) σ = √Var(X) (standard deviation)
Two-dice sum example: S = sum of two fair dice.
E(S) = 7 (by symmetry)  |  E(S²) ≈ 54.83  |  Var(S) = 54.83 − 49 = 5.83  |  σ ≈ 2.42

Bernoulli Distribution — Special Case

Bernoulli(p) — Single 0/1 Trial
E(X) = p     Var(X) = p(1−p) [Maximum variance at p = 0.5; zero variance at p = 0 or p = 1]
The bridge from sample to population: When you compute x̄ and s² from data, you're estimating E(X) and Var(X) of the underlying population. The probability distribution of X is the population.
P2.3 The Binomial Distribution

13. When to Use Binomial KEY

X ~ Bin(n, p) when ALL four conditions hold:

  • Fixed number of trials n (decided before the experiment starts)
  • Each trial has exactly 2 outcomes: success (probability p) or failure (probability 1−p)
  • Constant probability p of success on every trial
  • Trials are independent (one outcome doesn't affect the next)
If any condition fails, the Binomial does not apply. Sampling without replacement from a small population violates independence. Non-binary outcomes violate condition 2.

14. Binomial Probability Formula & Excel EXAM

Binomial PMF
P(X = k) = C(n,k) · pᵏ · (1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ   for k = 0, 1, …, n E(X) = np     Var(X) = np(1−p)     σ = √[np(1−p)]

The formula has three parts: C(n,k) counts the ways to get exactly k successes; pᵏ is the probability of k successes; (1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ is the probability of the remaining n−k failures.

Left-handed students — X ~ Bin(50, 0.13):
P(X = 7) = C(50,7) × 0.13⁷ × 0.87⁴³ ≈ 11.9%
E(X) = 50 × 0.13 = 6.5  |  Var(X) = 50 × 0.13 × 0.87 = 5.655  |  σ ≈ 2.38
What you needExcel formula
Exact probability P(X = k)=BINOM.DIST(k; n; p; FALSE)
Cumulative P(X ≤ k)=BINOM.DIST(k; n; p; TRUE)
P(X ≥ k)=1 − BINOM.DIST(k−1; n; p; TRUE)
P(a ≤ X ≤ b)=BINOM.DIST(b;n;p;TRUE) − BINOM.DIST(a−1;n;p;TRUE)

Shape of the Binomial

p = 0.5 → Symmetric

Perfectly balanced around np.

p < 0.5 → Right-skewed

Successes are rare; long right tail.

p > 0.5 → Left-skewed

Successes are common; long left tail.

P3.1 Density Functions & Continuous Probabilities

15. From Histogram to Density Curve

When a variable can take any value in an interval (heights, fill volumes, waiting times), the discrete framework breaks down. As you collect more data and narrow the histogram bins, the histogram smooths into a continuous density curve. The fundamental shift:

Core Rule for Continuous RVs
P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = area under f(x) between a and b P(X = a) = 0 for any exact point a P(X ≤ a) = P(X < a) [strict vs. non-strict are equal]
f(x) can exceed 1! Density is not probability. f(x) tells you where probability is concentrated, not how much is at a single point. The only constraints are f(x) ≥ 0 everywhere and total area = 1.

16. The Uniform Distribution U[a, b]

The simplest continuous distribution: all values in [a, b] are equally likely. The density is flat (constant).

Uniform U[a, b]
f(x) = 1/(b−a) for a ≤ x ≤ b, 0 elsewhere P(c ≤ X ≤ d) = (d−c) / (b−a)
Metro wait example — X ~ U[0, 10]:
P(wait < 3 min) = 3/10 = 0.30  |  P(3 ≤ wait ≤ 7) = 4/10 = 0.40
P3.2 Mean & Standard Deviation of Continuous RVs

17. Expected Value — The Balance Point

Expected Value (Continuous) — integral version of the discrete sum
E(X) = ∫ x · f(x) dx (integral over all x)

Geometrically, E(X) is the balance point of the density curve — the x-value where the distribution would balance if cut from uniform material.

Shortcut: Symmetric Distributions

For any symmetric distribution, E(X) = median = centre of symmetry. No integration needed. This is why E(X) = (a+b)/2 for U[a,b] and E(X) = μ for the normal distribution.

Uniform U[a, b] — Mean and Variance
E(X) = (a + b) / 2 [midpoint of the interval] Var(X) = (b − a)² / 12 [spread depends on the width] σ = (b − a) / √12
Metro wait U[0, 10]:
E(X) = (0+10)/2 = 5 minutes  |  Var(X) = 100/12 ≈ 8.33  |  σ = √8.33 ≈ 2.89 minutes
P3.3 The Normal Distribution N(μ, σ)

18. Properties of the Normal Distribution

The normal distribution is the most important distribution in statistics. X ~ N(μ, σ) where σ is the standard deviation (not variance):

  • Bell-shaped and perfectly symmetric around μ
  • Completely determined by just two parameters: mean μ and standard deviation σ
  • Extends infinitely in both directions (probabilities become negligible beyond ±4σ)
  • E(X) = μ  |  Var(X) = σ²  |  SD = σ
The 68-95-99.7 Empirical Rule
P(μ − σ ≤ X ≤ μ + σ) ≈ 68% [within 1 SD of mean] P(μ − 2σ ≤ X ≤ μ + 2σ) ≈ 95% [within 2 SD of mean] P(μ − 3σ ≤ X ≤ μ + 3σ) ≈ 99.7% [within 3 SD of mean]

19. Excel Functions for the Normal Distribution

What you needExcel formula
P(X ≤ x) — cumulative from the left=NORM.DIST(x; μ; σ; TRUE)
P(X > x) — right tail=1 − NORM.DIST(x; μ; σ; TRUE)
P(a ≤ X ≤ b) — between two values=NORM.DIST(b;μ;σ;TRUE) − NORM.DIST(a;μ;σ;TRUE)
Quantile: find x such that P(X ≤ x) = p=NORM.INV(p; μ; σ)
IQ scores — X ~ N(100, 15):
P(X > 120) = 1 − NORM.DIST(120; 100; 15; TRUE) ≈ 9.1%
P(80 ≤ X ≤ 120) = NORM.DIST(120;100;15;TRUE) − NORM.DIST(80;100;15;TRUE) ≈ 81.8%
Pregnancy length — X ~ N(266, 16) days:
P(premature, X < 250) = NORM.DIST(250; 266; 16; TRUE) ≈ 15.9%
5th percentile = NORM.INV(0.05; 266; 16) ≈ 239.7 days
P3.4 Z-Scores, Calibration & QQ-Plots

20. The Z-Score — Standardizing Any Normal Variable

Z-Score (Standardization)
Z = (X − μ) / σ ~ N(0, 1) Z > 0 → X is above the mean Z < 0 → X is below the mean |Z| = 2 → X is 2 standard deviations from the mean

Z-scores let you compare values from completely different normal distributions. An IQ of 130 (Z = +2) and a coffee pack at 260g when μ=250, σ=5 (also Z = +2) are equally extreme relative to their respective distributions.

Z-score exercise: X ~ N(50, 8). Find P(X > 62).
Z = (62 − 50)/8 = 1.5  |  P(Z > 1.5) = 1 − Φ(1.5) ≈ 1 − 0.9332 = 0.0668
Or directly: =1 − NORM.DIST(62; 50; 8; TRUE)

21. Working Backwards — Calibration Formula EXAM

Sam's coffee machine: SD = 5g, and we want P(X < 250g) ≤ 1%. What mean μ should we set the machine to?

Calibration — Find μ to Achieve a Target Probability
P(X < c) = p → μ = c − σ · NORM.INV(p; 0; 1) where NORM.INV(p; 0; 1) is the p-th quantile of N(0,1)
1
Target: P(X < 250) = 0.01, σ = 5
2
Find z₀.₀₁: NORM.INV(0.01; 0; 1) ≈ −2.326
3
Solve: μ = 250 − 5 × (−2.326) = 250 + 11.63 = 261.6g
Set the machine mean to 261.6g so that only 1% of packs fall below the 250g minimum. The machine fills slightly more than the label to protect against underfilling complaints.

22. Assessing Normality — QQ-Plots & Log Transformation

A QQ-plot (quantile-quantile plot) compares observed data quantiles against theoretical normal quantiles. If data is normal, the points fall on a straight diagonal line.

QQ-Plot PatternInterpretationAction
Straight diagonal lineApproximately normalNormal methods valid
Curves up at high endRight skew (long right tail)Consider log transform
Curves down at low endLeft skew (long left tail)Consider reflection + log
S-shape (both ends deviate)Heavy tailsUse robust methods
Log transformation: For right-skewed data (insurance claims, incomes, prices), apply =LN(x) and check if the transformed data is approximately normal on a new QQ-plot. If log(X) ~ N(μ, σ), then X follows a lognormal distribution — common in insurance and finance.
P4.1 Sums & Averages of Random Variables

23. Properties of the Sum S and Average X̄ KEY

Let X₁, X₂, …, Xₙ be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with mean μ and variance σ². Define the sum S = X₁ + … + Xₙ and the sample average X̄ = S/n.

Sum S = X₁ + X₂ + … + Xₙ
E(S) = n · μ Var(S) = n · σ² SD(S) = √n · σ
Average X̄ = S / n
E(X̄) = μ Var(X̄) = σ² / n SD(X̄) = σ / √n [Standard Error]
The √n effect: To halve the standard deviation of X̄ (halve the uncertainty in your estimate), you must quadruple the sample size. Going from n=100 to n=400 halves SD(X̄). This is why there are diminishing returns to increasing sample size.

24. Critical Distinction — W = X₁ + … + X₅ vs. 5X EXAM

W = X₁+X₂+X₃+X₄+X₅ (sum of FIVE DIFFERENT trials) is NOT the same as 5X (ONE trial multiplied by 5). They have the same expected value but very different variances.
QuantityE(·)Var(·)SD(·)
X (single trial)μσ²σ
5X (1 trial scaled up)5μ25σ²5σ
W = X₁+…+X₅ (5 independent trials)5μ5σ²√5 · σ

W has much less variance than 5X because the five independent draws partially cancel each other out. 5X just scales up all the randomness from a single trial.

Air conditioner example: 24 AC units, each drawing X ~ (any dist.) with μ = 4.8 kW, σ = 1.4 kW. Total S = X₁ + … + X₂₄.
E(S) = 24 × 4.8 = 115.2 kW  |  Var(S) = 24 × 1.96 = 47.04  |  SD(S) ≈ 6.86 kW
P4.2 The Central Limit Theorem

25. The Central Limit Theorem KEY

Central Limit Theorem (CLT)
If X₁, …, Xₙ are i.i.d. with mean μ and finite variance σ², then: X̄ ≈ N(μ, σ/√n) for large n [rule of thumb: n ≥ 30] S ≈ N(nμ, √n · σ) for large n This holds REGARDLESS of the original distribution of each Xᵢ.

This is arguably the most important result in all of statistics: no matter how skewed, heavy-tailed, or unusual the distribution of individual observations is, the average of many observations follows a bell curve.

26. How the CLT Works — The Simulation Picture

Exactly Normal (any n)

If each Xᵢ ~ N(μ, σ), then X̄ is exactly N(μ, σ/√n) for any n — no approximation needed.

CLT Approximation (n ≥ 30)

For any distribution with finite variance, X̄ ≈ N(μ, σ/√n) when n ≥ 30. Skewed distributions may need larger n.

Binomial Special Case

Bin(n,p) ≈ N(np, √np(1−p)) when np ≥ 5 AND n(1−p) ≥ 5.

27. Worked CLT Examples EXAM

GPA example: University GPA has μ = 6.5, σ = 1.2. Random sample n = 36.
By CLT: X̄ ~ N(6.5, 1.2/√36) = N(6.5, 0.2)
P(X̄ > 7.0) = 1 − NORM.DIST(7.0; 6.5; 0.2; TRUE) = 1 − Φ(2.5) ≈ 0.62%
P(6.2 ≤ X̄ ≤ 6.8) = NORM.DIST(6.8;6.5;0.2;TRUE) − NORM.DIST(6.2;6.5;0.2;TRUE) ≈ 86.6%
Binomial approximation: X ~ Bin(200, 0.40). Check: np = 80 ≥ 5 ✓, n(1−p) = 120 ≥ 5 ✓.
X ≈ N(80, √48) = N(80, 6.93)
P(X ≤ 90) ≈ NORM.DIST(90; 80; 6.93; TRUE) ≈ 0.926 (exact: 0.928 — excellent!)
Why the CLT matters so much: Every major statistical test — t-tests, ANOVA, regression coefficients — relies on the sampling distribution of averages or sums being approximately normal. The CLT guarantees this for large enough n, regardless of the underlying data distribution. This is why parametric tests are so broadly applicable, and why the normal distribution appears everywhere in statistics even when the original data is not normal.
