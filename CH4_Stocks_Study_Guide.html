1 · The Big Picture — Why Do Stocks Matter?

A stock (share / equity) represents a fractional ownership claim on a company. When you buy a share of Apple, you own a tiny slice of its factories, patents, cash, and future profits.

Global equity markets

Combined global stock market capitalisation exceeds $100 trillion. The US market alone (~NYSE + NASDAQ) accounts for roughly 44% of that total.

Long-run track record

US equities have delivered ~7% real returns per year over the past century — far outpacing bonds and inflation. But the path is bumpy.

⚠️

No free lunch: Higher long-run returns come packaged with higher short-run volatility. The VIX index (the "fear index," published by the CBOE) measures expected 30-day volatility using S&P 500 option prices. A reading above ~20 signals elevated uncertainty. On 5 March 2026 it stood at 24.28.

"In the short run, the market is a voting machine but in the long run, it is a weighing machine."
— Benjamin Graham, father of value investing
📖

2 · Key Terminology

Primary vs. Secondary Markets

Primary Market

New shares are issued for the first time — the company raises fresh capital. Example: IPO (Initial Public Offering).

Secondary Market

Existing shares are traded between investors. The company gets no new money; only ownership changes hands. Example: daily trading on NYSE.

Types of Stock Exchanges

Auction Market

Buyers and sellers matched directly. Prices determined by competing bids/offers. Examples: NYSE, LSE

Dealer Market

Market makers quote a bid price (what they buy for) and an ask price (what they sell for). Spread = their profit. Example: NASDAQ

OTC Market

No centralised exchange. Traded directly between parties. Common for penny stocks, derivatives (options, futures, CDOs, CDSs).

Growth Stocks vs. Value Stocks

Value Stocks

Trade at a discount relative to fundamentals (low P/E or P/B). Often mature, dividend-paying companies. Examples: utility companies, banks

Low P/E Higher dividend

3 · Market Efficiency — Do Prices Tell the Truth?

Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)

The EMH (Eugene Fama, Nobel 2013) says stock prices already incorporate all available information, so you can't consistently beat the market through stock picking or timing.

Weak Form

Prices reflect all past trading data. Technical analysis cannot generate excess returns.

Semi-Strong Form

Prices reflect all public information. Fundamental analysis cannot generate excess returns.

Strong Form

Prices reflect all information, including insider knowledge. Even insiders cannot beat the market.

Challenges to EMH

Adaptive Market Hypothesis

Andrew Lo (MIT): markets evolve like ecosystems. Efficiency varies over time as participants adapt. Opportunities exist but disappear when exploited.

Behavioral Finance

Kahneman & Thaler: humans are irrational. Biases (overconfidence, loss aversion, herding) create mispricings and bubbles.

Financial Instability Hypothesis (Minsky)

Periods of stability breed complacency and leverage. Debt builds up until a sudden "Minsky moment" collapse. Explains the 2008 crash.

💡

The 2010 Flash Crash (6 May 2010) saw the Dow Jones drop ~1,000 points in minutes due to algorithmic high-frequency trading feedback loops — then recover most losses within the hour. It illustrated how e-trading can amplify market dislocations.

4 · Dividend Discount Model (DDM) — The Core Idea

The DDM is built on one simple principle: a stock is worth the present value of all the cash it will ever pay you. Those cash flows are dividends.

Value = PV of all future dividends

The three ingredients of any valuation exercise (from Chapter 0):

1. Future Cash Flows

Expected dividends each period.

2. Timing

When exactly each dividend is paid.

3. Discount Rate (r)

The required return — must match the risk of the investment (opportunity cost).

5 · DDM — One-Period Setting

If you plan to hold a stock for exactly one year, you will receive one dividend and then sell the share. Your total return is:

Expected Return
r = (Div₁ + P₁ − P₀) / P₀
= Dividend Yield + Capital Gain Rate

Rearranging, today's price is the present value of next year's dividend and price:

Today's Price
P₀ = (Div₁ + P₁) / (1 + r)
Worked Example — Proximus

Proximus is selling for €9.00 today. Expected price in one year: €9.50. Expected dividend in one year: €0.60.

1r = (0.60 + 9.50 − 9.00) / 9.00
2r = 1.10 / 9.00
3r = 12.2% expected return

That's 6.7% dividend yield + 5.6% capital gain.

💡

We can also run this backwards: given investors' required return r and a forecast of Div₁ and P₁, we calculate what today's fair price P₀ should be.

🔢

6 · DDM — Multi-Period Extension

Substitute next year's price using the same one-period formula, then the year after, and so on. As the holding period stretches to infinity, the terminal price term shrinks to zero, leaving:

General DDM Formula
P₀ = Div₁/(1+r)¹ + Div₂/(1+r)² + Div₃/(1+r)³ + ··· + Divₙ/(1+r)ⁿ
Stock price = present value of ALL future dividends (to infinity)
Worked Example — Multi-Period

Proximus expected dividends: $1.20, $0.60, $0.60 over years 1–3. Expected sale price at end of year 3: $11.00. Required return: 8%.

1PV(Div₁) = 1.20 / 1.08 = $1.111
2PV(Div₂) = 0.60 / 1.08² = $0.514
3PV(Div₃) = 0.60 / 1.08³ = $0.476
4PV(P₃) = 11.00 / 1.08³ = $8.732
5P₀ = $10.83
📌

As the horizon H → ∞, the PV of the terminal price → 0. The stock value is then entirely driven by the stream of dividends — which is why dividends are the fundamental unit of stock value in theory.

0️⃣

7 · Special Case 1 — Zero Growth DDM (Perpetuity)

What if a company pays the exact same dividend forever? This is the simplest special case — a perpetuity.

Zero Growth DDM
P₀ = Div / r
Where Div = constant dividend per year, r = required return
Example

A stock pays a fixed dividend of $5 per year forever. Required return = 10%.

1P₀ = 5 / 0.10 = $50
💡

This is used as a benchmark "no-growth" valuation. If a company pays out all its earnings as dividends (Div = EPS), then P₀ = EPS/r. Any value above this must come from growth — which is what PVGO measures (see Section 10).

📈

8 · Special Case 2 — Constant Growth DDM (Gordon-Shapiro)

What if dividends grow at the same rate g every year forever? This is the Gordon-Shapiro model (also called the Gordon Growth Model).

Constant Growth DDM — Gordon-Shapiro
P₀ = Div₁ / (r − g)
Div₁ = next year's expected dividend
r = required return (must be > g)
g = constant annual dividend growth rate
⚠️

The formula only works when r > g. If g ≥ r, the denominator becomes zero or negative and the price would be infinite — economically unrealistic. In practice g is usually tied to long-run nominal GDP growth (~3-5%).

Rearranging for Required Return r

Market Capitalisation Rate
r = Div₁/P₀ + g
= Dividend Yield + Expected Dividend Growth Rate

Where Does g Come From? — The Plowback Formula

A firm grows dividends by reinvesting some earnings back into profitable projects. The key identities:

Payout Ratio

Fraction of earnings paid out as dividends.
Payout = Div / EPS

Plowback Ratio

Fraction of earnings retained.
Plowback = 1 − Payout

Growth Rate g

g = ROE × Plowback ratio
More retained earnings + higher ROE → higher g

Worked Example — Finding r

Stock price = $41.67, next dividend = $1.49, analysts forecast g = 5.1%.

1r = Div₁/P₀ + g = 1.49/41.67 + 0.051
2r = 0.0357 + 0.051 = 8.67%

Alternative: payout = 62%, ROE = 10% → g = 10% × (1−0.62) = 3.8% → r = 1.49/41.67 + 0.038 = 7.4%

The Dividend-Growth Trade-off

⚖️

Paying a higher dividend today reduces retained earnings → lower g → lower future dividends. The optimal payout decision depends on whether reinvesting capital earns more than r. If ROE > r, plowing back earnings increases stock price.

🌱

9 · PVGO — Present Value of Growth Opportunities

Any stock's price can be decomposed into two parts:

Stock Price Decomposition
P₀ = EPS / r + PVGO
EPS/r = no-growth benchmark (zero-growth perpetuity)
PVGO = value added by future reinvestment opportunities

Classic PVGO Example

Company pays 100% of earnings as dividend → EPS = Div = $8.33. Required return r = 15%.

1No plowback price: P₀ = 8.33 / 0.15 = $55.56

Now the company decides to plowback 40% of earnings at ROE = 25%:

2g = ROE × Plowback = 25% × 40% = 10%
3New Div₁ = EPS × Payout = 8.33 × 60% = $5.00
4Growth price: P₀ = 5.00 / (0.15 − 0.10) = $100.00
5PVGO = $100.00 − $55.56 = $44.44

The reinvestment strategy creates $44.44 of additional value per share because ROE (25%) > r (15%) — each dollar reinvested is worth more than $1.

📌

Key takeaway: PVGO is only positive when ROE > r (the firm earns more than its cost of capital). If ROE = r, PVGO = 0 and growth adds no value. If ROE < r, growth actually destroys value — paying out all earnings would be better!

10 · Special Case 3 — Multistage DDM

Real companies don't grow at a constant rate forever. A typical pattern: high growth phase → transition → stable growth phase. The multistage DDM handles this.

Stage 1
High growth
e.g. 15% × 5 years
→
Stage 2
Stable growth
e.g. 3% forever

Method

Step 1 — Calculate Stage 1 dividends

Project each dividend during the high-growth phase using the high growth rate.

Step 2 — Terminal value at end of Stage 1

Apply Gordon-Shapiro using the stable growth rate to get the stock price at start of Stage 2 (terminal value).

Step 3 — Discount everything back

PV all Stage 1 dividends + PV of the terminal value back to today.

Worked Example — Adidas

Last dividend paid: €3.30. Next 5 years growth: 15%/yr. After year 5: stable growth at 3%/yr. Required return: 8%.

1Div₁ = 3.30 × 1.15 = €3.795
2Div₂ = 3.795 × 1.15 = €4.364
3Div₃ = €5.019 | Div₄ = €5.772 | Div₅ = €6.638
4Terminal value P₅ = Div₆ / (r − g) = (6.638 × 1.03) / (0.08 − 0.03) = €136.86
5P₀ = PV(Div₁…Div₅) + PV(P₅) at 8%
6P₀ = 3.51 + 3.25 + 3.00 + 2.78 + 4.52 + 93.10 ≈ €110.16

11 · Business Valuation in Practice

The goal is to estimate the intrinsic value per share (Vₑ) to support investment decisions: buy/hold/sell advice, long/short positions, IPO pricing, M&A benchmarking.

Absolute Valuation (Discounting)

Models that explicitly project and discount future cash flows. More rigorous but data-intensive.

DDM DCF Residual Income

Relative Valuation (Multiples)

Compare to peer firms using ratios. Fast and practical but relies on comparables being fairly priced.

P/E P/S P/B

Why DCF Is Often Preferred Over DDM

Many companies don't pay dividends (especially growth companies), making DDM inapplicable. DCF models use free cash flows instead — what the company could pay if it chose to.

DCF — Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFe)

FCFe Model
Vₑ = FCFe₁/(1+r)¹ + FCFe₂/(1+r)² + ···
Discount rate = cost of equity

FCFe = Net Income + Depreciation − CapEx − ΔNWC + Net new borrowing

DCF — Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFf / WACC model)

FCFf (Enterprise Value) Model
EV = FCFf₁/(1+WACC)¹ + FCFf₂/(1+WACC)² + ···
Discount at WACC (weighted avg. cost of debt+equity)

Then: Equity Value = EV − Net Debt. Divide by shares outstanding to get price per share.

Relative Valuation — Multiples

MultipleFormulaWhat it tells youCaution
P/E Ratio Price / EPS How much investors pay per €1 of earnings. "Growth premium" shows up here. Earnings can be manipulated; negative earnings → meaningless P/E
Shiller CAPE Price / 10-yr avg. real EPS Smooths out business cycle volatility; widely used for market-level valuation. Can remain "overvalued" for years
P/B Ratio Price / Book Value per share Compares market value to accounting value. P/B > 1 means market expects ROE > cost of equity. Intangibles often not on balance sheet
P/S Ratio Price / Revenue per share Useful when earnings are negative (e.g. early-stage). Focus on top-line growth. Ignores profitability entirely

⚠️

The multiples trap: If you benchmark to comparable firms and the whole sector is overvalued, you'll also conclude your stock is fairly priced. "You go down if the entire ship sinks." Always combine multiples with at least one absolute valuation method.
