Topic 1 · Options

Financial options give you the right without the obligation. Real options give your investment projects the same flexibility.

What is a Financial Option?

An option is a derivative contract — its value is derived from an underlying asset (usually a stock). The key word is right, not obligation: you can choose to use it or walk away.

💡
Everyday analogy Think of a cinema ticket refund policy. You buy a ticket (pay a premium). If the film is great you go (exercise the option). If reviews are terrible you can get a refund (let it expire). You never lose more than the ticket price.
CALL option

The right to BUY

You profit when the asset price rises above your agreed strike price.

Long call (buy): You believe the price will rise — hedge against rising costs.
Short call (sell/write): You believe the price will stay flat or fall.

PUT option

The right to SELL

You profit when the asset price falls below your agreed strike price.

Long put (buy): You believe the price will fall — hedge against falling revenues.
Short put (sell/write): You believe the price will stay flat or rise.

The 4 Positions at a Glance

✅ Long Call — buy a call

You pay a small premium now to lock in the right to buy later.
Maximum loss: the premium paid. Upside: unlimited.

⚠️ Short Call — sell a call

You collect the premium but take on the risk that the buyer will exercise.
Maximum gain: the premium. Downside: unlimited.

✅ Long Put — buy a put

You pay a premium to protect yourself against falling prices.
Like insurance for your assets.

⚠️ Short Put — sell a put

You earn the premium but must buy the asset at the strike price if the buyer exercises — risky if prices crash.

🔗
Combination Strategy: Long Straddle Buy a call AND a put on the same asset, same expiry. You profit if the price moves strongly in either direction. An import/export firm uses this to hedge currency risk — they don't know whether their home currency will strengthen or weaken, so they protect both ways. The counterpart (short straddle) bets on low volatility.

Valuing Options: the Black-Scholes Model

Developed by economists Fischer Black and Myron Scholes (Nobel Prize 1997), this formula tells us the fair price of a European call option on a non-dividend-paying stock.

C = S · N(d₁) − K · e−rT · N(d₂)

d₁ = [ ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2) · T ] / (σ · √T) d₂ = d₁ − σ · √T

Where: S = current stock price K = strike price T = time to expiration (in years) r = risk-free interest rate σ = volatility (standard deviation of stock returns) N(·) = cumulative normal distribution

You do not need to memorise this formula, but you must understand what drives option prices:

📈 Stock Price (S) ↑

Call option becomes more valuable (closer to or above strike price).

🎯 Strike Price (K) ↑

Call option becomes less valuable (harder to beat a higher target).

⏳ Time (T) ↑

More time = more chance something good happens = more valuable.

💰 Risk-free Rate (r) ↑

Higher rates reduce the present value of the strike price you'd pay, making calls more valuable.

🌪️ Volatility (σ) ↑

Both calls and puts become more valuable. Higher uncertainty = more upside potential.

⚠️ Limitation

Black-Scholes is for European options only. American options (exercisable any time) need more complex models.

🔑
Key insight — "Law of One Price" The model works by imagining a replicating portfolio: a combination of stock and risk-free borrowing that perfectly mimics the option's payoff. In an efficient market, these two things must have the same price — otherwise arbitrage profits would exist and be instantly traded away.

Real Options in Capital Budgeting

🛩️
The Boeing 737 MAX case When serious technical problems emerged, Boeing faced a three-way choice: stop manufacturing entirely, wait and resume later, or invest in fixing the problems and scale up. Traditional NPV gave a single yes/no answer. Real options analysis recognised these as valuable choices that management could make as more information came in.

Standard NPV is a "one-shot" decision — it assumes all information is known upfront and managers are passive. In reality, managers adapt. A real option is the ability to adjust an investment decision as new information arrives.

📋 Types of Real Options

  • Option to delay — wait for better market conditions
  • Option to expand — invest more if successful
  • Option to scale down — reduce exposure if struggling
  • Option to abandon — cut losses and exit
  • Option to switch — pivot to a different use

💎 Why They Add Value

Real options always add positive value to a project because you only exercise them when it is beneficial.

The key rule: the more uncertainty there is, the more valuable the real options become — the opposite of what standard NPV implies!

NPV with real options = Standard NPV + Option Value

Decision Tree Analysis — The Meghan Flea Market Example

A booth costs $500. Good weather (75% probability) yields $1,500 profit. Bad weather (25%) causes $100 loss. No time value of money.

❌ Naive Approach (no real option)

Meghan goes regardless of weather.

Expected value = 0.75×($1,500) + 0.25×(−$100) − $500
= $1,125 − $25 − $500 = $600

✅ Real Option Approach (option to wait)

Meghan only goes if the weather is good.

Expected value = 0.75×($1,500) − $500
= $1,125 − $500 = $625

Value of real option = $625 − $600 = +$25

Real Option: Wait for weather forecast first → only go if sunny → eliminate the bad-weather branch entirely.
🗝️ Manager's 3-Step Real Options Playbook
1

Identify the uncertainty

Ask: "What is the main variable we cannot control or know upfront?" (Meghan: weather. A pharma firm: clinical trial outcomes. An oil company: oil price.)

2

Design interim decision points

Structure the project so that management can react once the uncertainty is resolved — before full commitment. This is where the real option "lives".

3

Recalculate NPV including option value

Add the value of the real option to your standard NPV. Even a previously negative-NPV project can become worth pursuing once you include the option to abandon or scale up.

🏆
Big Idea — Options & Uncertainty Financial options and real options share the same surprising property: more risk = more value. Volatility is the enemy of a standard investor, but it is the friend of anyone holding an option. This is why startups and R&D projects — high uncertainty by nature — can be worth far more than their expected cash flows suggest.

Topic 2 · Market Efficiency

Can you consistently beat the stock market? The Efficient Market Hypothesis says it is much harder than you think.

The Random Walk — Price Patterns Don't Exist

A classic experiment: show analysts a chart of a stock's 5-year price history and a chart generated by repeated coin flips. They are indistinguishable. Why?

🎲
The logic of efficient markets If there were a recognisable pattern in past prices that predicted future prices, rational traders would immediately exploit it. Their trading would push prices to the level that removes the predictable profit — and the pattern would disappear. Therefore, in equilibrium, prices must already reflect all information, and future moves must be unpredictable (a "random walk").

The 3 Forms of Market Efficiency

Economist Eugene Fama (Nobel Prize 2013) proposed three levels of efficiency, each building on the previous one:

Weak Form
Prices reflect all historical trading data (past prices, volumes).
Implication: Technical analysis (chart patterns) cannot generate consistent excess returns. Fundamental analysis may still work.
Semi-Strong Form
Prices reflect all publicly available information (earnings reports, news, economic data).
Implication: Neither technical nor fundamental analysis can consistently beat the market. Only insider information could do so.
Strong Form
Prices reflect all information — public and private (insider).
Implication: Even insider trading cannot produce abnormal returns. This form is widely considered too extreme to hold in practice.
✅
What economists broadly agree on Most developed markets are at least weak-form efficient, and probably semi-strong efficient most of the time. Strong-form efficiency is almost certainly violated — that is why insider trading laws exist and why insiders are legally required to disclose trades.

But Wait — Anomalies Do Exist

Evidence shows that markets are not perfectly efficient. Known anomalies include:

📅 January Effect

Stocks (especially small caps) tend to rise in January, possibly due to tax-loss selling in December and re-buying in the new year.

🏠 Home Bias

Investors systematically overweight domestic stocks despite diversification benefits of holding international assets.

📦 Disposition Effect

Investors tend to sell winning stocks too early and hold losing stocks too long — irrational behaviour driven by psychology, not information.

🎮 GameStop Squeeze

In 2021, retail investors coordinated on Reddit to short-squeeze GameStop stock far beyond fundamental value — a clear price dislocation.

🖼️ NFT Mania (2021)

Digital assets with no cash flows traded for millions. A textbook speculative bubble driven by narrative and FOMO, not fundamentals.

🤖 AI Bubble?

As of 2024-26, some analysts argue AI-related stocks are priced beyond fundamentals — potentially another efficiency test in real time.

Active vs Passive Investing

🎯 Active Investing

  • Fund managers pick individual stocks and try to beat the benchmark (e.g. S&P 500)
  • Requires belief in market inefficiencies
  • Higher fees eat into returns
  • Most active funds underperform passive over the long run

📊 Passive Investing

  • Index funds simply replicate market benchmarks (e.g. S&P 500 ETF)
  • Assumes markets are sufficiently efficient
  • Very low fees
  • Majority of active managers fail to beat index funds over 10+ years
⚠️
A new concern: Is passive investing making markets LESS efficient? As trillions of dollars flow into passive index funds, fewer investors are doing fundamental analysis to set prices. Research from the LSE (2024) and others suggests that index-driven buying may weaken the price discovery mechanism and increase co-movement of stocks — potentially creating new inefficiencies.

Interesting Alternatives to the EMH

🧠 Behavioral Finance

Developed by Kahneman & Thaler (both Nobel laureates), behavioral finance documents that humans are not rational. Common biases include:

  • Overconfidence — we overestimate our own knowledge
  • Loss aversion — losses hurt ~2× more than equivalent gains feel good
  • Herding — we follow the crowd even against our own information
  • Anchoring — we fixate on irrelevant reference numbers

🦎 Adaptive Market Hypothesis (AMH)

Proposed by MIT Professor Andrew Lo, the AMH applies evolutionary biology to finance:

  • Investors are like organisms: they learn, adapt, and compete
  • Markets are neither always efficient nor always irrational — they evolve
  • In periods of "calm", markets approach EMH. In crises, biases dominate
  • Strategies that worked yesterday may stop working as competitors copy them — and then work again later
🏆
Big Idea — Degrees of Efficiency Markets are not perfectly efficient, but they are competitive enough that beating them consistently is very hard. The practical lesson: be skeptical of anyone claiming to have found a reliable way to predict stock prices — if it were truly reliable, traders would have already arbitraged it away.

Topic 3 · Debt Policy & Capital Structure

How much debt should a firm take on? The answer is surprisingly complex — and gets you straight to the heart of corporate finance.

Modigliani & Miller Proposition I (MM I) — Capital Structure is Irrelevant

⚖️
The Irrelevance Proposition (1958) In a perfect world (no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, no transaction costs, efficient markets), the total value of a firm does not depend on how it is financed. Whether you fund with 10% debt or 90% debt, firm value is the same: VU = VL.

Why? Shareholders can replicate any leverage decision the firm makes on their own account (using personal borrowing). The firm creates no value by doing something investors can do themselves for free. Any "arbitrage opportunity" from mispricing would be traded away instantly.

🔓
The real-world twist Remove even one MM assumption and the financing decision suddenly matters. Taxes? Bankruptcy? Information asymmetry? Each one means the way you finance the firm affects its value — and that's where the interesting theories begin.

Modigliani & Miller Proposition II (MM II) — Leverage Increases Shareholder Returns

r_e = r_a + (r_a − r_d) × (D / E)

Where: r_e = expected return on equity (what shareholders demand) r_a = return on firm's assets (unlevered cost of capital) r_d = cost of debt (interest rate) D/E = debt-to-equity ratio (leverage)

As debt increases, shareholders demand a higher return — because they bear more financial risk. This is perfectly rational: leverage amplifies both gains AND losses.

Scenario Financing Period 0 (invest) Period 1 (+25% ↑) Period 1 (−25% ↓) ROE ↑ ROE ↓
No Leverage 100% equity €100,000 €125,000 €75,000 +25% −25%
50% Leverage 50% debt + 50% equity €50,000 own €75,000 → pay debt → €25,000 €25,000 → pay debt → €25,000 loss +50% −50%
⚔️
Debt is a double-edged sword Leverage magnifies returns in good times but magnifies losses in bad times. This is why the 2008 financial crisis was so severe: banks and households were heavily leveraged. When house prices fell, losses were amplified across the entire financial system — subprime mortgage delinquencies triggered a global cascade.

Two Competing Capital Structure Theories

Once we accept that the real world has taxes and financial distress costs, two main theories explain how firms should choose their debt level:

⚖️ Trade-Off Theory

Find the optimal debt level by balancing two forces:

  • Tax shield benefit (+): Interest payments are tax-deductible. The more debt, the less tax you pay.
    Tax Shield = tc × D × rd
  • Financial distress cost (−): Too much debt raises the probability of bankruptcy — costly for everyone (legal fees, lost customers, fire-sale assets).
Optimal debt: the point where the marginal benefit of the next euro of debt (tax shield) equals its marginal cost (distress risk).

📋 Pecking Order Theory

Firms don't have an "optimal" target — they just follow a cost-of-capital hierarchy:

  1. Internal funds first (retained earnings)
    Cheapest: no new investor communication needed
  2. External debt second
    More expensive: lenders price in default risk
  3. External equity last
    Most expensive: signals to market that management thinks shares are overvalued
Driver: Information asymmetry — management knows more than outside investors, making external capital costly.

Trade-Off Theory — What You'd Observe

  • Firms in high-tax environments use more debt
  • Firms with stable, tangible assets (utilities, real estate) use more debt
  • Firms with volatile cash flows (tech startups) use less debt

Pecking Order — What You'd Observe

  • Profitable firms use less debt (self-fund via retained earnings)
  • Firms issue equity only as a last resort
  • No clearly defined "target" debt ratio across time

How Much Debt is Too Much?

⚠️
Macro-level warning — the 2008 Financial Crisis The Global Financial Crisis began with excessive debt at every level: individual households took on mortgages they couldn't afford; banks leveraged 30:1 on these mortgages; investors bought opaque derivatives on top of those. When US house prices fell just 5–10%, the entire leveraged system unravelled. The lesson: what seems individually manageable becomes systemically catastrophic when everyone is over-leveraged simultaneously.

📊 Debt Capacity Signals

  • Interest coverage ratio
  • Debt-to-EBITDA ratio
  • Credit rating
  • Free cash flow vs. debt service

🚨 Distress Warning Signs

  • Rising credit spreads
  • Covenant breaches
  • Inability to refinance maturing debt
  • Declining operating cash flows

✅ Safe Practices

  • Match debt maturity to asset life
  • Maintain liquidity buffers
  • Stress-test at higher interest rates
  • Keep refinancing risk low
🏆
Big Idea — Financing Does (and Doesn't) Matter MM proved that in a perfect world, how you finance a firm is irrelevant. The real world — with taxes, bankruptcy, and information gaps — breaks that irrelevance. Trade-off theory finds an optimal debt level; pecking order theory says firms follow a financing hierarchy driven by cost. Neither theory is complete on its own, and the right capital structure depends heavily on the firm's industry, profitability, and risk profile.

Chapter Summary — One-Page Cheat Sheet

The three big ideas and their core logic in a single view.

📊 Options

A right without obligation. Call = right to buy. Put = right to sell.

Black-Scholes: Option value driven by S, K, T, r, σ. Volatility always adds value.

Real options: Embed flexibility into capital projects. More uncertainty → more real option value. Standard NPV undervalues projects with real options.

📉 Market Efficiency

Prices reflect available information. Three forms: weak → semi-strong → strong.

Implication: Beating the market consistently is very hard. Most active managers fail to outperform index funds.

But: Anomalies exist. Behavioral finance and the Adaptive Market Hypothesis explain why markets are not perfectly rational.

🏦 Debt Policy

MM I: In a perfect world, capital structure is irrelevant. MM II: leverage amplifies both returns and risk.

Trade-off theory: Optimal debt balances tax shield vs. distress costs.

Pecking order: Firms prefer internal funds → debt → equity. Driven by information asymmetry costs.
