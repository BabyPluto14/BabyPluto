Part 1 — Why Does CAPM Exist?

The problem CAPM was built to solve.

❓ The Problem

You want to invest in a stock. You know it's risky. But how much return should you demand for that risk?

Before CAPM (1964), there was no agreed formula. Everyone just guessed. CAPM gave the world a precise, mathematical answer:

"The return you should demand on any investment = Risk-Free Rate + Compensation for the Risk You Cannot Avoid"
💡
The key insight: Not all risk deserves compensation. If you can eliminate a risk for free by diversifying, the market won't pay you for bearing it. CAPM identifies exactly which risk the market does pay you for — and prices it precisely.

🎯 The Two Types of Risk (recap)

Unique / Idiosyncratic Risk

Diversifiable

Risk specific to one company (bad CEO, product failure). You can make it disappear by holding many stocks. Market pays you ZERO for this.

Market / Systematic Risk

Non-Diversifiable

Economy-wide risk (recessions, rate hikes, wars). No matter how many stocks you hold, this stays. Market MUST pay you for this.

CAPM measures market risk using Beta (β) and tells you exactly how much return to demand for it.

Part 2 — Building Up to CAPM Step by Step

CAPM doesn't appear from nowhere. It's the result of a logical 3-step journey.

1
Start: You have N stocks → Efficient Frontier

By combining stocks in different weights, you get millions of possible portfolios. The efficient frontier is the upper-left edge — the best return for each level of risk. Every rational investor should be ON this frontier.

2
Add the Risk-Free Asset → Capital Market Line (CML)

You can now also invest in a risk-free asset (government bond, rate = Rƒ). This changes everything. You can mix the risk-free asset with one special portfolio M (the Market Portfolio) to beat the efficient frontier. This combination creates a straight line: the CML.

3
Apply to Individual Securities → Security Market Line (SML) = CAPM

The SML translates the logic of the CML to individual stocks. Instead of using total risk (σ), it uses Beta (β) — the only risk the market pays for. This gives us the CAPM formula.

🗺️
The journey in one line:
Many Stocks → Efficient Frontier → Add Risk-Free Asset → CML → Apply to Individual Stocks → SML (CAPM)

Part 3 — Beta (β): The Heart of CAPM

Before you can use CAPM, you must deeply understand Beta.

β What Beta Means — Plain English

Beta answers one question: "If the market moves by 1%, how much does this stock move?"

Beta Definition
β = Cov(Rᵢ, Rₘ) / Var(Rₘ)
Rᵢ = return on the stock
Rₘ = return on the market (e.g. S&P 500, MSCI World)
In practice: Beta = slope of regression line (stock returns vs market returns)
Market portfolio always has β = 1 by definition
β < 1
Defensive
Less volatile than market
e.g. utilities, pharma, food
β = 1
Moves with market
Same risk as market
e.g. index ETF
β > 1
Aggressive
More volatile than market
e.g. tech, biotech, crypto
β < 0
Counter-cyclical
Moves opposite market
e.g. gold, inverse ETFs

Key exam point: A high beta stock demands a higher required return. If you bear more market risk, you must be compensated with more expected return. This is the whole logic of CAPM.

🧮 How to Calculate Beta — Worked Example

Worked Example 1 — Computing Beta from Data
Monthly returns for Stock ABC and the market over 5 months:
MonthMarket Return (Rₘ)Stock ABC Return (Rᵢ)
Jan+2%+3%
Feb−1%−2%
Mar+4%+7%
Apr−2%−4%
May+3%+5%

Notice the pattern: when market goes up 1%, ABC goes up roughly 1.7–2%. That means beta is roughly around 1.7–2. Let's confirm:

In practice you'd run a regression. The slope of the line "ABC return = α + β × Market return" gives you beta. From this data, β ≈ 1.75 — meaning ABC is an aggressive stock, moving ~1.75× the market.

💡
Exam shortcut: If the question says "beta = X" — just use it directly in the CAPM formula. You don't always need to compute it from scratch.

📊 Beta of a Portfolio

Good news: portfolio beta IS just the weighted average of individual betas (unlike portfolio risk).

Portfolio Beta
βₚ = w₁·β₁ + w₂·β₂ + … + wₙ·βₙ
wᵢ = weight of stock i in portfolio | βᵢ = beta of stock i
Worked Example 2 — Portfolio Beta
You hold: 40% in Stock A (β = 0.6), 35% in Stock B (β = 1.2), 25% in Stock C (β = 1.8)
βₚ = 0.40 × 0.6 + 0.35 × 1.2 + 0.25 × 1.8
= 0.24 + 0.42 + 0.45
= 1.11

Your portfolio is slightly more aggressive than the market (β slightly above 1).

Part 4 — The Capital Market Line (CML)

For portfolios — the best possible risk/return combinations when you add a risk-free asset.

📈 What the CML Shows

When you can lend or borrow at the risk-free rate Rƒ, you can combine Rƒ with the Market Portfolio M in any proportion. This gives you a straight line that is BETTER than the efficient frontier alone.

Lending (Conservative)

Put SOME money in stocks (Portfolio M) and lend the rest at Rƒ. You're between Rƒ and M on the line.

Market Portfolio M

100% invested in the market portfolio. This is the tangency point — the highest Sharpe ratio possible.

Borrowing (Aggressive)

Borrow money at Rƒ and invest MORE than 100% in M. Amplifies both returns and risk — you're to the right of M.

CML Formula — for Portfolios
E(Rₚ) = Rƒ + [(E(Rₘ) − Rƒ) / σₘ] × σₚ
Rƒ = risk-free rate
E(Rₘ) = expected return of the market portfolio
σₘ = standard deviation of the market portfolio
σₚ = standard deviation of YOUR portfolio
(E(Rₘ)−Rƒ)/σₘ = Sharpe ratio of the market — this is the "price of risk"
CML: The straight line through Rƒ and M. Portfolio M has the highest possible Sharpe ratio.
🎯
CML is for portfolios only. It uses total risk (σ) on the x-axis. But when we want to price an individual stock (not a full portfolio), we need a different line — the SML. That is CAPM.

Part 5 — The Security Market Line (SML) = CAPM

This is the formula you use in exams, in practice, for pricing any individual asset.

⭐ The CAPM Formula — Memorise This

CAPM / Security Market Line (SML)
E(Rᵢ) = Rƒ + βᵢ × (E(Rₘ) − Rƒ)
E(Rᵢ) = required/expected return on security i ← THIS IS WHAT YOU SOLVE FOR
Rƒ = risk-free rate (10-year government bond yield)
βᵢ = beta of security i (its market risk sensitivity)
E(Rₘ) = expected return on the market portfolio
E(Rₘ) − Rƒ = market risk premium (typically 5–7% historically)
Required Return = What you get for waiting (Rƒ) + What you get for taking risk (β × Market Premium)

🧩 Breaking Down Each Piece

ComponentWhat It IsTypical ValueWhere to Get It
Rƒ
Risk-free rate		 Return with zero risk. The baseline everyone gets. 2–5% (varies) 10-year government bond yield (e.g. German Bund, US Treasury)
β
Beta		 How much market risk this specific stock carries. 0.4 to 2.0 for most stocks Calculated from regression, or looked up (Bloomberg, Yahoo Finance)
E(Rₘ)
Market return		 What the whole market is expected to return. 8–10% historically Historical average of market index (S&P 500, MSCI World)
E(Rₘ) − Rƒ
Market risk premium		 The extra return you get for holding stocks vs bonds. 5–7% Calculated as E(Rₘ) − Rƒ. Often just given in the exam.

📊 The SML Graph

The SML: x-axis = Beta, y-axis = Required Return.
Stocks ON the line are fairly priced. Above = underpriced (buy). Below = overpriced (sell/avoid).
🔑
CML vs SML — the key difference:

CML → x-axis = total risk (σ) → used for portfolios
SML → x-axis = beta (β) → used for individual securities

In exams, you almost always use the SML (CAPM formula).

Part 6 — Computing CAPM: Worked Examples

Step-by-step calculations from simple to complex.

1️⃣ Basic CAPM — Standard Calculation

Worked Example 3 — Basic CAPM (from your slides)
Risk-free rate (Rƒ): 3.5% Expected market return E(Rₘ): 10% Beta of the company (β): 1.2
1
Calculate the Market Risk Premium
Market Risk Premium = E(Rₘ) − Rƒ = 10% − 3.5% = 6.5%
2
Calculate the Security's Risk Premium
Security Risk Premium = β × Market Risk Premium = 1.2 × 6.5% = 7.8%
3
Add the Risk-Free Rate
E(R) = Rƒ + Security Risk Premium = 3.5% + 7.8% = 11.3%
✅
Answer: 11.3% — Shareholders require a minimum return of 11.3% for bearing this level of market risk.

2️⃣ Defensive Stock (Low Beta)

Worked Example 4 — Defensive Company (e.g. Water Utility)
Rƒ: 3%, E(Rₘ): 9%, β: 0.5
1
Market Risk Premium
9% − 3% = 6%
2
Security Risk Premium
0.5 × 6% = 3%
3
Required Return
E(R) = 3% + 3% = 6%

Low risk → low required return (6%). Makes sense for a stable utility company.

3️⃣ Aggressive Stock (High Beta)

Worked Example 5 — Tech / Growth Company
Rƒ: 3%, E(Rₘ): 9%, β: 2.0
1
Market Risk Premium
9% − 3% = 6%
2
Security Risk Premium
2.0 × 6% = 12%
3
Required Return
E(R) = 3% + 12% = 15%

High risk → high required return (15%). Same inputs as Example 4, but β=2 instead of 0.5. See how much beta matters!

4️⃣ Solving for Beta (Reverse CAPM)

Sometimes the exam gives you the required return and asks you to find beta. Just rearrange the formula.

Rearranged CAPM — solving for Beta
β = (E(Rᵢ) − Rƒ) / (E(Rₘ) − Rƒ)
Worked Example 6 — Find Beta
Rƒ: 4%, E(Rₘ): 10%, Required return E(R): 13%
1
Market Risk Premium
10% − 4% = 6%
2
Apply rearranged formula
β = (13% − 4%) / 6% = 9% / 6% = 1.5

5️⃣ Solving for Market Return (Another Reverse)

Worked Example 7 — Find E(Rₘ)
Rƒ: 2%, β: 1.4, Required return E(R): 12.8%
1
Rearrange CAPM for E(Rₘ)
E(R) = Rƒ + β(E(Rₘ) − Rƒ)
12.8% = 2% + 1.4 × (E(Rₘ) − 2%)
10.8% = 1.4 × (E(Rₘ) − 2%)
E(Rₘ) − 2% = 10.8% / 1.4 = 7.71%
E(Rₘ) = 9.71%

6️⃣ AB InBev — Full Real-World Example (from your slides)

Worked Example 8 — AB InBev CAPM
Rƒ: 3% (10-year government bond) E(Rₘ): 8% β: 1.32
1
Market Risk Premium
E(Rₘ) − Rƒ = 8% − 3% = 5%
2
Security Risk Premium
β × MRP = 1.32 × 5% = 6.6%
3
Cost of Equity (= CAPM result)
COE = 3% + 6.6% = 9.6%

AB InBev shareholders require a minimum return of 9.6%. If you're valuing AB InBev shares using a DDM model, you use 9.6% as your discount rate.

Part 7 — Reading the SML Graph in Exams

Understanding what the graph tells you about whether a stock is fairly priced.

🔍 Three Positions on the SML

Where the Stock FallsWhat It MeansWhat HappensAction
ON the SML Actual return = required return (CAPM) Stock is fairly priced — in equilibrium Hold
ABOVE the SML Actual return > required return (CAPM) Stock is underpriced — giving more than you should get for its risk. Investors will rush to buy it, pushing price up until return normalizes back to SML. BUY
BELOW the SML Actual return < required return (CAPM) Stock is overpriced — giving less than fair compensation for its risk. Investors sell it, pushing price down until return rises back to SML. SELL / AVOID
Worked Example 9 — Is This Stock Fairly Priced?
Rƒ: 4%, E(Rₘ): 10%, β: 1.5 Stock's actual current expected return: 14%
1
Calculate CAPM (fair) return
E(R) = 4% + 1.5 × (10% − 4%) = 4% + 9% = 13%
2
Compare actual vs fair return
Actual return: 14%
CAPM (fair) return: 13%
Actual > Fair → Stock is ABOVE the SML → UNDERPRICED → BUY
Worked Example 10 — Another Pricing Check
Rƒ: 3%, E(Rₘ): 8%, β: 0.8 Stock's actual expected return: 6%
1
Calculate CAPM (fair) return
E(R) = 3% + 0.8 × (8% − 3%) = 3% + 4% = 7%
2
Compare actual vs fair return
Actual return: 6%
CAPM (fair) return: 7%
Actual < Fair → Stock is BELOW the SML → OVERPRICED → SELL / AVOID

Part 8 — CAPM → Cost of Equity (COE)

The CAPM result IS the Cost of Equity. Here is how it's used.

💰 COE = CAPM Output

The CAPM formula gives you the minimum return shareholders require for investing in the company's stock. This is called the Cost of Equity (COE).

Cost of Equity
COE = rₑ = Rƒ + β × (E(Rₘ) − Rƒ)
This is used as the discount rate when valuing equity cash flows (e.g. dividends in the DDM model).
It's the opportunity cost for shareholders — what they could earn on an equally risky investment.
📌
With Fama-French Size Premium:
If the company is small, add a size premium (e.g. 1%) on top:

COE = Rƒ + β × (E(Rₘ) − Rƒ) + Size Premium
= 3.5% + 7.8% + 1.0% = 12.3% (from slide example)

Part 9 — From COE to WACC

Once you have COE from CAPM, you can calculate the company's overall cost of capital.

🏗️ Why WACC?

A company is funded by two groups: equity holders (shareholders) and debt holders (banks, bondholders). Each requires a different return. WACC blends them into one company-wide hurdle rate.

WACC Formula
WACC = (E/V) × rₑ + (D/V) × r_d × (1 − t)
E = market value of equity
D = market value of debt
V = E + D (total firm value)
rₑ = Cost of Equity (= CAPM result)
r_d = Cost of Debt (bond yield or interest rate)
t = corporate tax rate
(1−t) = tax shield — interest is tax-deductible, so real cost of debt is lower
Worked Example 11 — Full CAPM to WACC (AB InBev)
Rƒ: 3% | β: 1.32 | E(Rₘ): 8% D/(D+E): 42% | E/(D+E): 58% Cost of Debt (r_d): 4.65% | Tax rate: 25%
1
Compute COE using CAPM
COE = 3% + 1.32 × (8% − 3%)
= 3% + 1.32 × 5%
= 3% + 6.6% = 9.6%
2
Compute after-tax cost of debt
r_d × (1 − t) = 4.65% × (1 − 0.25)
= 4.65% × 0.75 = 3.4875%
3
Compute WACC
WACC = 0.58 × 9.6% + 0.42 × 3.4875%
= 5.568% + 1.465%
= 7.03%
🎯
Use 9.6% when valuing AB InBev shares (equity investor perspective — COE).
Use 7.03% when evaluating AB InBev investment projects (company perspective — WACC).

✏️ Practice Questions

Try These — Answers Below

Q1. Rƒ = 5%, E(Rₘ) = 12%, β = 0.75. What is the required return?

Q2. Rƒ = 2%, E(Rₘ) = 9%, β = 1.6. What is the required return?

Q3. Rƒ = 4%, E(Rₘ) = 10%, required return = 15.8%. What is beta?

Q4. Portfolio: 50% in β=0.8 stock, 30% in β=1.5 stock, 20% in β=2.0 stock. What is portfolio beta?

Q5. COE = 11%, r_d = 5%, tax = 30%, D/V = 40%, E/V = 60%. What is WACC?

Q6. CAPM says a stock should return 9%. Its actual expected return is 7%. Is it underpriced or overpriced? Buy or sell?

Answers
Q1: E(R) = 5% + 0.75 × (12%−5%) = 5% + 5.25% = 10.25%

Q2: E(R) = 2% + 1.6 × (9%−2%) = 2% + 11.2% = 13.2%

Q3: β = (15.8%−4%) / (10%−4%) = 11.8% / 6% = 1.97

Q4: βₚ = 0.5×0.8 + 0.3×1.5 + 0.2×2.0 = 0.40 + 0.45 + 0.40 = 1.25

Q5: WACC = 0.60×11% + 0.40×5%×0.70 = 6.6% + 1.4% = 8.0%

Q6: Actual (7%) < CAPM fair (9%) → BELOW SML → Overpriced → Sell / Avoid
