Chapter 1 — Institutions of the European Union
This chapter establishes what kind of entity the EU is, traces the history of European
integration from the post-war period to the Treaty of Lisbon and Brexit, explains the
rules and process for EU membership and withdrawal, and describes the
composition, powers, and decision-making rules of each of the four political
institutions plus the Court of Justice. It forms the constitutional bedrock for
everything else in the course: competences, policy-making procedures, the single
market, the EMU, and EU external relations all presuppose a working knowledge of
the institutional architecture analysed here.

1. What Kind of Entity Is the EU?
1.1 International Organisations — Basic Concepts
The EU is an international organisation. International organisations share four
defining features: they are established by states through a founding treaty; they
pursue common goals set out in that treaty; they operate through common organs
(secretariat, council, assembly, etc.); and they possess international legal
personality, meaning they hold rights and duties under international law and their
headquarters are inviolable.
Common examples include the UN, WTO, WHO, NATO, IMF, World Bank, ASEAN,
and Mercosur. All are established by states. The EU belongs to this family but is
exceptional within it because it combines supranational and intergovernmental
elements simultaneously.

1.2 Supranational vs Intergovernmental
This distinction is the single most important conceptual tool in the course. It runs
through every chapter and recurs in almost every exam question.

📌 Key distinction: Supranational organisation vs Intergovernmental
organisation
In a supranational organisation, member states transfer sovereignty — decisionmaking powers — to the organisation for specific policy areas. Decisions can be
taken by majority vote and are legally binding on all members, including those that
voted against. An independent organ (judges, commissioners) can also bind
member states without their individual consent. In an intergovernmental
organisation, no sovereignty is transferred. Legally binding decisions require
unanimity; a state cannot be bound against its will. Decisions taken by majority
are only binding on states that voted in favour.
The EU is unique because it contains both features. Most economic policy areas
(customs union, single market, competition, trade, monetary policy for the eurozone)
are supranational: QMV in the Council can bind a dissenting member state. The

Common Foreign and Security Policy remains purely intergovernmental: all
decisions require unanimity and no legislative acts can be adopted. No other
international organisation combines these two dimensions at the EU's level of depth
and legal integration.

⚠ Exam warning: Almost all major international organisations outside the EU are
predominantly intergovernmental (UN, NATO, WTO, WHO, IMF). The EU is
exceptional precisely because it is substantially supranational. Be ready to
explain the exact consequences: majority voting, legally binding decisions, and
the ability to bind a dissenting member state.

2. Historical Overview of European Integration
The EU emerged from two urgent post-war challenges: rebuilding destroyed
European economies and preventing future large-scale conflict. Economic
integration was used deliberately as an instrument of peace — embedding former
enemies in a web of mutual dependency that would make war materially impossible.

2.1 Post-War Foundations (late 1940s) — All Intergovernmental
Three organisations were created in rapid succession after 1945. All were purely
intergovernmental.
•

Organisation for European Economic Cooperation (OEEC, 1948): Established
to coordinate economic recovery and distribute Marshall Plan aid from the
United States. Later evolved into the OECD (Organisation for Economic
Cooperation and Development), still based in Paris.

•

Council of Europe (1949): Founded on Churchill's initiative to promote
democracy, rule of law, and human rights. Its main instrument is the
European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR), enforced by the European
Court of Human Rights in Strasbourg. Currently 46 member states —
significantly larger than the EU. Has nothing to do with EU institutions or EU
law.

•

NATO (1949): Military collective-defence alliance. Article 5 makes an armed
attack against one member an attack against all, entitling a collective armed
response. Includes the USA and Canada; purely intergovernmental; decisions
by consensus.

⚠ Exam warning — Council of Europe ≠ European Council ≠ Council of the EU.
The Council of Europe is a separate international organisation (46 members,
founded 1949, no link to EU). The European Council and the Council of the EU are
both EU institutions, but with entirely different compositions and powers. This
distinction is tested every year.

2.2 The 1950s — First Supranational Steps
In 1950, French Foreign Minister Robert Schuman proposed placing the FrancoGerman coal and steel industry under a supranational authority. The strategic logic
was explicit: coal and steel are the raw materials of military production; pooling
them would make war between France and Germany materially impossible.
Economic integration as an instrument for peace — a theme that runs through the
entire history of the EU.
The six founding states — France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands,
and Luxembourg — agreed to the Treaty of Paris (1951), establishing the European
Coal and Steel Community (ECSC). This was the first genuinely supranational
organisation in history: the High Authority could take legally binding decisions over
the coal and steel sector without requiring unanimous agreement.
In 1952, the same six states attempted a European Defence Community (EDC) — a
supranational military organisation to manage West German rearmament in the
context of Cold War tensions. France refused to ratify, fearing a supranational
command over its armed forces just seven years after the war. A purely
intergovernmental alternative, the Western European Union (WEU), was established
instead.
The Treaties of Rome (1957, in force 1 January 1958) created the European Economic
Community (EEC) — with a common market covering goods, services, capital, and
labour, plus common agricultural, competition, and trade policies — and EURATOM
for civilian nuclear energy. The six founding states had committed to the most
ambitious economic integration project the world had seen.

2.3 The 1960s–1980s: Enlargements, Crises, and the SEA
The 1960s and 1970s were marked by political friction (de Gaulle's 'empty chair'
policy paralysing Council decisions) and steady enlargement. The UK, Denmark, and
Ireland joined in 1973 (first enlargement); Greece in 1981 (second); Portugal and
Spain in 1986 (third). Austria, Finland, and Sweden joined in 1995 (fourth).
The decisive renewal came in the 1980s. Commission President Delors published the
1985 White Paper 'Completing the Internal Market', identifying three categories of
barriers — physical (border checks), technical (divergent product standards), and
fiscal (different VAT and excise duties) — and proposing a package of roughly 300
legislative measures to be adopted by 31 December 1992. To make this possible, the
Single European Act (1986) introduced qualified majority voting (QMV) in the Council
for single-market measures (removing the unanimity blockage), the concept of
mutual recognition, and a strengthened role for the European Parliament.

2.4 The 1990s: The Maastricht Treaty and the Three-Pillar EU
The fall of the Berlin Wall (1989), German reunification (1990), and the collapse of
the Soviet Union (1991) transformed European politics. The Treaty on European
Union, signed at Maastricht in 1992 and in force November 1993, created the
European Union and introduced a three-pillar structure.

2.5 Nice (2001), the Constitutional Treaty (2004), and the Treaty of Lisbon
(2009)
The fifth enlargement (2004) brought in ten new member states — Czech Republic,
Cyprus, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Slovakia, and Slovenia
— followed by Bulgaria and Romania in 2007 (sixth enlargement) and Croatia in 2013
(seventh enlargement). Institutional reforms to prepare for this expansion were
made at Nice (2001).
The Treaty Establishing a Constitution for Europe (2004) failed after rejection by
French and Dutch voters in 2005. The Treaty of Lisbon (signed 2007, in force 1
December 2009) preserved its key institutional innovations while abandoning the
constitutional framing. It replaced the three-pillar structure with two equal treaties
— the Treaty on European Union (TEU) and the Treaty on the Functioning of the
European Union (TFEU) — and introduced several major democratic and
institutional changes.

Key Lisbon Treaty innovations
Permanent European Council President: Full-time, appointed for 2.5 years
(renewable once) by QMV, replacing the six-monthly rotating presidency.
High Representative for Foreign Affairs: Merged post of HR for CFSP and
Commissioner for External Relations; also VP of the Commission; chairs the
Foreign Affairs Council.
European External Action Service (EEAS): EU diplomatic service, operational
from 2012.
European Citizens' Initiative: One million signatures from at least seven
member states can invite the Commission to propose legislation.

3. Enlargement and Withdrawal
3.1 Eligibility: Copenhagen Criteria
Article 49 TEU: any European state that respects EU values (Article 2 TEU: human
dignity, freedom, democracy, equality, the rule of law, human rights) may apply. The
Copenhagen criteria (1993) operationalise this requirement:
•

Political: stable institutions guaranteeing democracy, rule of law, human
rights, and protection of minorities.

•

Economic: a functioning market economy able to cope with competitive
pressure within the EU.

•

Administrative: the capacity to take on EU membership obligations — full
implementation of the acquis communautaire.

3.2 Membership Procedure (Three Steps)
1. Candidacy: The state applies to the Council. The Commission assesses
eligibility. The Council decides (unanimity of all member states) whether to
grant candidate status and open negotiations.
2. Membership negotiations: The Commission negotiates the terms — primarily
the time frames for implementing each chapter of the acquis. The Council
must approve each completed chapter. The process is monitored through
regular Commission progress reports.
3. Accession: Once complete, the Commission gives its opinion; an accession
treaty is drawn up; the European Commission, European Council, and
European Parliament must all approve; all existing member states and the
candidate state ratify.
Current candidate countries (2026): Montenegro, Serbia, Turkey, North Macedonia,
Albania, Ukraine, Moldova, Georgia, and Bosnia and Herzegovina. The acceleration
of candidacies for Ukraine, Moldova, and Georgia reflects the geopolitical impact of
Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.

3.5 Withdrawal — Article 50 TEU
Any member state may withdraw in accordance with its own constitutional
requirements. It notifies the European Council, which provides guidelines. The
Union negotiates and concludes a withdrawal agreement with the state — concluded
by the Council by QMV after obtaining Parliament consent. If no agreement is
reached within two years (extendable by unanimity), the state leaves without a deal.
Brexit completed this process on 1 February 2020.

4. The Institutional Framework of the EU
Article 13 TEU lists seven EU institutions. Four are political institutions involved in
policy direction and law-making: the European Parliament, the European Council,
the Council of the EU, and the European Commission. Three others have specialised
mandates: the Court of Justice of the EU, the European Central Bank, and the
European Court of Auditors. Beyond these, nine EU bodies (e.g. the EEAS, Economic
and Social Committee) and around 30 decentralised agencies (e.g. Europol, Eurojust)
complete the architecture. Bodies are part of the EU; agencies have separate legal
personality.

4.1 The European Council
Institution: European Council
Main role

Provides the EU with political direction and defines general
guidelines and priorities. Takes strategic decisions on
enlargement, budget framework, and EU foreign policy.

Composition

Heads of state or government of all 27 member states + President
of the European Commission. Members may be accompanied by
a minister; Commission President by a Commissioner.

Key powers

Defines general political direction. Cannot adopt legislative acts.
Appoints Commission President (QMV), its own President, and
the High Representative. Decisions on enlargement candidacies
require unanimity.

Democratic link

Represents member states at the highest political level.
Indirectly democratically legitimate through national electoral
processes.

Exam relevance

Distinguish from Council of EU (ministers, legislation) and
Council of Europe (separate organisation). Cannot adopt
legislation — only the Commission/Parliament/Council
legislative trio can. Current President: António Costa (since 1
December 2024).

The Permanent Presidency was created by Lisbon. Previous holders: Herman Van
Rompuy (2009–2014), Donald Tusk (2014–2019), Charles Michel (2019–2024). The
President chairs summits, ensures preparation and continuity, and reports to the
European Parliament after each meeting. Decisions in the European Council are
taken by consensus — not formal voting but negotiation until no one objects — except
where the treaties specifically provide for QMV (e.g. appointment of its President).
The European Council's outputs are called 'Conclusions of the Presidency'. These
have major political weight but are not legally binding legislative acts. They must be
translated into legislation through the ordinary legislative procedure involving the
Commission, Parliament, and Council of the EU. Parliamentary control over the
European Council is very limited — the Parliament cannot censure it, which is one
of the EU's persistent democratic deficits.

4.2 The European Parliament
Institution: European Parliament
Main role

Represents EU citizens. Co-legislates with the Council under the
ordinary legislative procedure. Approves the EU budget.
Supervises the European Commission.

Composition

720 MEPs directly elected by EU citizens every five years in
proportional elections. Grouped by political affinity into 8
political groups + non-attached members. President: currently
Roberta Metsola (Malta).

Key powers

Co-decision on legislation (OLP). Budgetary authority — final
word on expenditures. Consent to international trade
agreements. Approves Commission President and full College of
Commissioners. Motion of censure can force Commission
resignation. Individual Commissioner responsibility since
Lisbon.

Democratic link

Directly elected — the only EU institution with a direct
democratic mandate from citizens. Groups by political ideology,
not nationality.

Exam relevance

The only directly elected EU institution — this uniqueness is a
standard exam point. Three sources of uniqueness:
parliamentary body in an IO; directly elected; genuine legislative
powers.

EP elections take place every five years (most recently 2024: 720 seats). Seats are
allocated among member states by degressive proportionality — smaller states are
somewhat over-represented. Belgium has 22 MEPs; Germany has 96. Elections use
proportional representation and are held simultaneously across all member states.
The standard voting age is 18.
The eight current political groups range from far-left to far-right. The two largest —
the European People's Party (EPP, centre-right, ~188 seats) and the Socialists and
Democrats (S&D, centre-left, ~136 seats) — have historically formed the legislative
majority needed for most decisions. Since 2024, the Patriots for Europe group (rightwing nationalist, ~84 seats) is the third largest, including Rassemblement National
(France) and Vlaams Belang (Belgium). The Renew group (liberal) and ECR
(conservative) complete the major groups. Decisions in plenary require an absolute
majority of votes cast.
The Parliament meets in plenary at least 12 times per year in Strasbourg — a political
compromise insisted upon by France — while committee work (22 committees) takes
place in Brussels. MEPs are notably more independent of executive discipline than
members of most national parliaments, giving the EP genuinely greater legislative
influence in policy-making relative to many national parliaments.

The Council presidency rotates among member states every six months. Currently:
Cyprus (January–June 2026). The rotating state chairs all configurations except the
Foreign Affairs Council, which is chaired permanently by the High Representative.
Preparation occurs through COREPER (the Committee of Permanent
Representatives) — ambassadors from each member state who resolve most
technical issues before ministerial meetings.

Decision-making in the Council — Three Voting Rules
Unanimity: Every member state must vote in favour. Any state can block (veto).
Still applies to: CFSP/defence, indirect taxation, social security, EU membership,
and constitutional-level decisions. Reflects intergovernmentalism.
Qualified Majority Voting (QMV): Double majority: at least 55% of member states
(≥15 out of 27) AND representing at least 65% of the EU population must vote in
favour. Applies to most legislative decisions. A minority of states can be bound
against their will — a supranational feature. Larger states (Germany, France, Italy,
Spain, Poland) carry greater weight through the population criterion.
Simple majority: 50%+1 of member states. Used only for procedural matters.

QMV is the key supranational feature of the Council: it allows the EU to bind a
dissenting member state. This is why, for example, Hungary can block sanctions
decisions in the CFSP (unanimity), but cannot block single-market legislation (QMV).
The distinction between policy areas where unanimity applies vs. QMV applies is one
of the most important structural features of EU decision-making.

4.4 The European Commission
Institution: European Commission
Main role

The EU's supranational executive: proposes legislation,
implements policy, manages the budget, enforces EU law, and
represents the EU externally in trade matters.

Composition

College of 27 Commissioners (one per member state, though
Lisbon originally allowed 2/3 — European Council confirmed
one-per-state in 2009) + administrative arm of ~32,000 officials
in Directorates-General (DGs). Current President: Ursula von
der Leyen (since 1 December 2024, second term to 31 October
2029).

Key powers

Exclusive right of legislative initiative (with limited CFSP
exceptions). Guardian of the treaties — can bring infringement
proceedings before the CJEU. Manages the EU budget. Enforces
competition law. Concludes trade negotiations.

Democratic link

Independent of member state governments — Commissioners
must act in the general EU interest. Accountable to the European
Parliament (approval and censure).

4.5 The Court of Justice of the European Union
Institution: Court of Justice of the EU (CJEU)
Main role

The EU's judicial authority. Ensures uniform interpretation and
application of EU law. Enforces EU obligations on member states
and institutions.

Composition

Two courts since 1989: the Court of Justice (27 judges, one per
member state, 6-year renewable terms) and the General Court
(54 judges, two per member state). Judges appointed by
common accord of national governments; predominantly civilservice or academic backgrounds. Based in Luxembourg.
Current CJ President: Koen Lenaerts (Belgium, also a KU Leuven
professor).

Key powers

Compulsory jurisdiction — all member states accept it
automatically on accession. Direct actions (annulment, failure to
act, infringement). Preliminary rulings on interpretation of EU
law for national courts.

Democratic link

Judges are independent; not representing national interests.
Indirect democratic link through treaty framework established
by member states.

Exam relevance

Understand the difference between direct actions and
preliminary rulings. The preliminary ruling procedure is the key
mechanism for uniform application of EU law across all 27
member states and the main indirect access route for
individuals.

Preliminary Ruling Procedure (Article 267 TFEU)
What it is: A national court handling a case involving EU law is uncertain about
the correct interpretation of an EU rule.
Step 1: The national court submits a reference question to the CJEU. Courts of
final instance (no further appeal) must refer; lower courts may refer.
Step 2: The CJEU gives an authoritative interpretation (a 'preliminary ruling').
Step 3: The national court applies that interpretation to decide the case.

Why it matters: Promotes uniform interpretation and application of EU law
across all 27 member states. Provides indirect access for individuals who cannot
normally bring cases directly before the CJEU.
Exam link: Contrast with direct actions (brought by institutions or member
states). The preliminary ruling is the mechanism through which EU law
penetrates and shapes national legal systems every day.
Direct actions include: actions for annulment (challenging legality of EU acts),

treaty obligations (the Commission's main enforcement tool against non-compliant
member states). Lengthy and costly procedures are the main practical weaknesses
of the system.

4.6 The European Central Bank and European Court of Auditors
The European Central Bank (ECB, Article 282 TFEU) is responsible for the monetary
policy of the eurozone — currently 21 member states. It is independent of EU
institutions and member state governments. Its primary objective is price stability
(inflation target: close to but below 2%). It manages the euro, operates the
Eurosystem, and since the Banking Union (2014) also supervises major eurozone
banks under the Single Supervisory Mechanism. The ECB is a supranational
institution with genuine exclusive power over monetary policy in the eurozone.
The European Court of Auditors (Article 285 TFEU) examines whether EU funds have
been collected and spent legally and in accordance with sound financial
management. It produces annual reports (including the statement of assurance on
the EU accounts) and special reports on specific spending programmes. It has no
judicial powers — it does not impose penalties — but its findings are politically
significant and can trigger Commission or OLAF investigations.

Chapter 2 — Policy-Making in the EU: Competences and
Budget
This chapter covers how EU law is made (legislative procedures), the types of legal
acts that result, the distribution of competences between the EU and member states,
the subsidiarity principle, and the structure of the EU budget. Together these explain
the constitutional logic of EU action: who can act, through which procedure,
producing what type of binding act, funded by what revenues.

1. Legislative Procedures
1.1 The Ordinary Legislative Procedure (Co-Decision)
The ordinary legislative procedure (OLP) — renamed from 'co-decision' by the Lisbon
Treaty — is the standard legislative procedure for most EU policy areas. It
establishes the Commission, Parliament, and Council as the three-institution
legislative triangle, with Parliament and Council as co-equal legislators.

Ordinary Legislative Procedure
Actors: European Commission (sole right of initiative), European Parliament (colegislator), Council of the EU (co-legislator).
Step 1 — Commission proposal: Commission proposes a legislative act.
Parliament and Council cannot initiate legislation themselves.
Step 2 — First reading: Parliament adopts its position (possibly amending).
Council either accepts Parliament's position (act adopted) or adopts its own firstreading position.
Step 3 — Second reading: Parliament approves the Council's position (act
adopted), rejects it (act fails), or proposes amendments. If amended, goes to
Council for second reading.
Step 4 — Conciliation (if needed): A Conciliation Committee of equal EP and
Council representatives negotiates a joint text within six weeks.
Step 5 — Third reading: Both EP and Council must adopt the joint text without
amendment. If either rejects, the act fails.
Decision rule: Parliament by absolute majority; Council by QMV in most cases.
Why it matters: Has become the dominant procedure since Lisbon, covering
most EU policy areas. It is democratic (EP approval required), supranational
(QMV in Council can bind dissenting states), and produces legally binding acts.
Exam link: Know that the Commission is always the initiator. The Council cannot
adopt an act the Parliament has not approved. The procedure is stipulated by the
treaties for each legal basis — the institutions cannot choose which procedure to
use.

1.2 Special Legislative Procedures
1.2.1 Consultation Procedure
Origin: the original procedure of the EEC, still the most widely used before the SEA.
Now limited to specific areas: some internal market exemptions, competition law.
Process: Commission proposes; Council seeks EP's opinion (compulsory, but nonbinding); Council votes. The Parliament is consulted but its opinion does not bind the
Council.
1.2.2 Consent Procedure (formerly Assent)
Introduced by the SEA. The EP must approve without the ability to propose
amendments — a de facto right of veto. Used for: new EU membership (accession);
withdrawal arrangements; international agreements in areas covered by OLP; cases
of serious breach of fundamental rights (Article 7 TEU); and legislation against
discrimination. Parliament votes yes or no.

1.3 The European Citizens' Initiative (ECI)
Introduced by Lisbon as a direct democracy instrument. Allows citizens to invite the
Commission to propose legislation in any area where the Commission has
competence. Conditions: 7 citizens from at least 7 different member states who are
old enough to vote must organise the initiative; once registered, must collect 1
million statements of support with minimum thresholds in at least 7 member states.
Result: the Commission must consider the initiative and respond publicly. It is not
obliged to propose legislation.

⚠ Exam warning: The ECI gives citizens the right to invite (petition) the
Commission to propose legislation — not the right to make formal legislative
proposals themselves. The Commission retains its exclusive right of initiative. So
far 127 initiatives have been registered; 11 have successfully reached the
signature threshold.

📌 Key distinction: Regulation vs Directive
A regulation is binding in its entirety and directly applicable: no national
implementing act is needed and it automatically becomes part of national law. A
directive is binding only as to the result to be achieved — member states choose
the form and methods. The statement 'a directive is binding in its entirety' is
FALSE (it describes a regulation). The statement 'a directive is binding as to the
result' is TRUE.

4. CFSP Decision-Making
The CFSP is the major exception to the supranational legislative pattern. Because it
is purely intergovernmental: the Commission has no right of initiative (member
states and the High Representative do); the Parliament is only consulted optionally;
no legislative acts can be adopted; decisions are non-legislative acts taken by the
European Council or Council, essentially by unanimity. Any single member state can
block. This is why Hungary has been able to delay or veto EU sanctions packages
against Russia — unanimity applies and no QMV override exists.

5.1 Subsidiarity Principle
For all shared competences, the subsidiarity principle (Article 5(3) TEU, inserted at
the UK's request in 1992) requires that the EU act only if the objective cannot be
sufficiently achieved by member states acting alone and can be better achieved at EU
level by reason of its scale or effects. It prevents centralisation of competences that
member states can handle adequately. National parliaments police subsidiarity
through the yellow card mechanism: one-third of national parliaments can formally
object within eight weeks; the Commission must review and explain its decision.

6. The EU Budget
6.1 The Multiannual Financial Framework
The EU budget operates within the Multiannual Financial Framework (MFF) — a
seven-year spending plan setting ceilings for each expenditure category. The current
MFF covers 2021–2027. The budget must balance — the EU cannot run a deficit.

The two largest expenditure categories are cohesion policy (structural and

budget. Smaller but growing categories include research and innovation (Horizon

2. The Customs Union
The Treaty of Rome (1958) set 1970 as the deadline to achieve a customs union. In
fact it was completed on 1 July 1968 — 18 months ahead of schedule — with the
abolition of all internal customs duties among the (then) six member states and the
introduction of the Common Customs Tariff (CCT) for all goods entering from outside
the EU. The CCT is the same for all member states: the simple average is 6.5%; the
trade-weighted average is 1.1%, reflecting the many zero-rate items.

2.1 Economic Impact: Trade Creation and Trade Diversion
The economic analysis of customs unions uses two concepts that explain welfare
effects of moving from separate national tariff regimes to a common external tariff.
📌 Key distinction: Trade creation vs Trade diversion
Trade creation occurs when membership of the customs union allows a more
expensive domestic product to be replaced by a cheaper product from a partner
country. This is welfare-improving: consumers access cheaper goods. Trade
diversion occurs when cheaper imports from a third country (non-member) are
replaced by more expensive imports from a partner country inside the customs
union, because the common external tariff now makes the third country's goods
more expensive. This is welfare-reducing. For the EU, trade creation substantially
exceeded trade diversion overall (~USD 10bn creation vs ~USD 1bn diversion at
the time of formation). The main area of trade diversion has been agricultural
products — heavily protected under the Common Agricultural Policy.
Beyond trade creation/diversion, intra-EU trade among the original six member
states increased by approximately 25% following formation of the customs union.
Trade with third countries also increased, as the common external tariff brought
greater predictability.

3. From Customs Union to Single Market: The 1985 White Paper
Achieving a customs union was only the first step. The Treaty of Rome's ultimate goal
was a common market covering goods, services, persons, and capital. Progress
toward this goal was painfully slow for two decades, and the European Commission
concluded by the early 1980s that without fundamental changes in approach, the
single market would never be achieved.

3.2 The Methodological Problem
The method used before 1985 was the root of the problem. Two obstacles combined.
First: decision-making required unanimity in the Council. With 10 member states in
the early 1980s, any one state could block a proposal it disliked. Progress was
glacially slow. Second: the approach to harmonisation was total harmonisation —
the EEC tried to adopt extremely detailed technical specifications for every product
category in every member state. This required vast quantities of technical legislation,
each item subject to unanimity, producing near-paralysis.

3.3 The 1992 Programme — New Approach
The 1985 White Paper 'Completing the Internal Market' proposed around 300
legislative measures to be adopted by 31 December 1992. Three methodological
breakthroughs enabled this.
•

QMV for single-market measures: The Single European Act (1986) introduced
qualified majority voting for single-market legislation, removing the unanimity
blockage. One dissenting member state can no longer veto.

•

Mutual recognition (Cassis de Dijon principle): If a product is lawfully
produced and marketed in one member state, it must in principle be permitted
to be sold in all others. Member states cannot impose their own standards as
a barrier.

•

New approach to harmonisation: EU legislation now sets only essential health
and safety requirements. Detailed technical specifications are left to
European standardisation bodies. This drastically reduced the volume of EU
legislation needed.

4. The Four Freedoms of the Single Market
4.1 Free Movement of Goods
Free movement of goods goes well beyond the customs union (which covered only
tariffs). It also requires the removal of quantitative restrictions (quotas) and all
measures having equivalent effect — a broad concept defined by the Court of Justice.
Key CJEU Cases — Free Movement of Goods
Dassonville (Case 8/74, 1974): The Court defined 'measures having equivalent
effect to quantitative restrictions' as: all trading rules enacted by member states
which are capable of hindering, directly or indirectly, actually or potentially,
intra-Community trade. This captures non-tariff barriers including national
standards and certification requirements.
Cassis de Dijon (Case 120/79, 1979): A German rule requiring fruit liqueurs to
have at least 25% alcohol content was applied to Cassis de Dijon (a French
blackcurrant liqueur with ~15-20% alcohol). The Court rejected this as an
unjustified non-tariff barrier and established mutual recognition: if a product is
lawfully produced and sold in one member state, it must be accepted in all others.
This became the foundation of the new approach to harmonisation.

Chapter 4 — The Economic and Monetary Union
The EMU represents the final stage of the EU's economic integration process. This
chapter explains the theoretical advantages and disadvantages of monetary union,
the debate between Monetarists and Economists over sequencing, the Delors
roadmap from the Hague Summit to the Treaty of Maastricht, the convergence
criteria, the actual introduction of the euro, and the governance mechanisms put in
place to manage the EMU — including the Stability and Growth Pact, Banking Union,
and responses to the 2008 financial crisis and COVID-19.

1. The Final Stage: Theory of an EMU
In theory, an Economic and Monetary Union means one common monetary policy
and one common economic policy — a single central bank, a single currency, and
coordinated fiscal and structural policies. In practice, the EU's EMU is asymmetric:
the monetary dimension is fully supranational (the ECB, an exclusive EU
competence for eurozone members), while the economic dimension remains largely
a system of coordination among sovereign states.

1.2 The Monetarists vs Economists Debate
📌 Key distinction: Monetarists (France, Belgium) vs Economists
(Germany, Netherlands)
The Monetarists argued: first fix exchange rates, and economic convergence will
follow naturally as states are forced to align their policies under the constraint of
a fixed rate. The Economists argued the opposite: first converge economic
policies (especially fiscal policies), and only then fix exchange rates and introduce
a single currency. Without prior convergence, a monetary union among
economically divergent states will generate permanent structural tensions. The
Treaty of Maastricht outcome was a compromise incorporating elements of both
views — a convergence stage (Stage 2 with the convergence criteria) followed by
a monetary union, but with escape clauses that allowed less-than-perfect
convergence. This compromise is one reason the EMU has proven structurally
fragile in crisis situations.

2. From the Hague to Maastricht: The Road to the Euro
The objective of an EMU was not in the original Treaty of Rome (1957). It first
appeared formally at the Hague Summit (1969), where member states agreed to
explore a path to EMU. An initial attempt (Werner Plan, 1970) was derailed by the
collapse of the Bretton Woods monetary system and the 1973 oil crisis.
In 1979, the European Monetary System (EMS) was introduced as a partial substitute:
a system of semi-fixed exchange rates with agreed bands of fluctuation (±2.25%)
among participating EEC members. It was not yet a monetary union, but it created
monetary discipline and closer central bank cooperation.
The decisive push came with the Delors Report (1989), presented by Commission
President Jacques Delors. The report proposed a three-stage roadmap that was
integrated into the Treaty of Maastricht (1992), giving the EMU project a legal
framework and a timetable with a target of approximately 10–12 years.
The Delors Roadmap — Three Stages to the Euro
Stage 1 (1990–1993): Free movement of capital (single market requirement).
Closer coordination of economic policies. Closer cooperation among national
central banks.
Stage 2 (1994–1998): Convergence of economic and monetary policies measured
against the five Maastricht convergence criteria. Establishment of the European
Monetary Institute (EMI) — precursor to the ECB. Monitoring and assessment of
which states meet the criteria.
Stage 3 (1999–2002): Establishment of the ECB. Irrevocable fixing of exchange
rates between participating currencies (1 January 1999). Introduction of the euro
as a physical currency — banknotes and coins replacing national currencies (1
January 2002 for the initial 12 participants).

5. Managing the EMU: Governance Mechanisms
5.1 The Stability and Growth Pact (1997)
The SGP was put in place to ensure that eurozone members maintained fiscal
discipline after adopting the euro. It operates through a preventive arm (multilateral
surveillance: member states submit annual stability programmes; the Commission
monitors compliance) and a corrective arm (Excessive Deficit Procedure: if a
member state's deficit exceeds 3% of GDP, the Council declares an excessive deficit
and requires correction; financial sanctions — deposits that become fines — can be
levied). In practice, the SGP was undermined in 2003 when France and Germany

exceeded the 3% limit and the Council refused to impose sanctions. The Pact was
revised to allow more flexibility, then strengthened again after the 2008 crisis.

5.4 COVID-19 Response (2020)
The COVID-19 pandemic triggered the activation of the 'general escape clause' of the

needed. The EU also established the Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRF) — a €750

issuance). This was unprecedented: the EU borrowing jointly on capital markets to
fund national recovery plans. The RRF requires member states to submit National
Recovery and Resilience Plans committing to green and digital investments and
structural reforms.

•

Coordination of economic policy between member states (not a single
economic policy — coordination remains flexible).

•

Fiscal limits through the SGP and Fiscal Compact (coordination of fiscal

•

An independent monetary policy run by the ECB (supranational, exclusive
competence for eurozone members).

•

Single rules and supervision of financial institutions — the Banking Union.

•

A single currency and the euro area.

Chapter 5 — Competition Policy of the EU
EU competition policy protects the functioning of the single market by preventing
companies and governments from distorting competition. It is an exclusive EU
competence (as necessary for the internal market). It covers three areas: (1)
prohibiting restrictive business practices (cartels); (2) prohibiting abuse of a
dominant position; (3) controlling mergers. State aid by governments is a fourth
component addressed under Articles 107–108 TFEU.

1. Overview and Legal Basis
Competition policy has become what the professor calls the 'flagship of the EU'. Its
major aim is to safeguard the correct functioning of the single market. Without it,
dominant firms, cartels, and state subsidies would fragment the single market just
as effectively as tariff barriers. The Commission — not national courts — is the
primary enforcer at EU level. National competition authorities exist alongside EU
competition law but cover practices that do not significantly affect trade between
member states.

effect the prevention, restriction, or distortion of competition within the EU are
prohibited. This covers price-fixing cartels, market-sharing, production quotas, bidrigging, and certain exclusive distribution arrangements.

Chapter 6 — External Relations of the European Union
The EU's external relations have two fundamentally different tracks: external
economic relations (trade policy, development cooperation — predominantly
supranational, exclusive competence) and external political relations (CFSP,
defence — purely intergovernmental). This structural distinction governs who acts,
how decisions are made, and what the EU can actually achieve in each domain.

1. The Two Tracks of EU External Action
📌 Key distinction: External economic relations vs External political
relations (CFSP)
External economic relations (trade policy, development cooperation) are
supranational: the EU has exclusive or strong shared competence, the
Commission negotiates on behalf of all member states, and decisions are taken
under the ordinary legislative procedure or by QMV. External political relations
(CFSP, defence and security policy) are purely intergovernmental: no transfer of
sovereignty, unanimity required, no legislative acts possible, and the Commission
and Parliament have only marginal roles. CFSP was the second pillar under
Maastricht; though the pillar structure was abolished by Lisbon, the CFSP's
intergovernmental character was preserved.

3. Common Foreign and Security Policy (CFSP)
3.1 Origins and Development
The CFSP was established by the Treaty of Maastricht (1992) as the second pillar of
the EU, in response to the geopolitical upheavals of 1989–1991. Goals: strengthen EU
security; promote peace and international security; promote international
cooperation, democracy, rule of law, and respect for human rights. From the start,
the CFSP was purely intergovernmental: unanimity in Council; no legislative acts;
Commission and Parliament with minimal roles.
The Treaty of Amsterdam (1997) added the Policy Planning and Early Warning Unit
within the Council Secretariat and created the post of High Representative for the
CFSP (HR-CFSP) — giving the CFSP a public face.

3.2 Lisbon Treaty Changes to the CFSP
Key CFSP changes under the Treaty of Lisbon
CFSP still intergovernmental: Despite abolishing the pillar structure, Lisbon
preserved the CFSP's intergovernmental character. Unanimity and no legislative
acts remain the rule.
Permanent European Council President: António Costa (since December 2024)
chairs European Council meetings that define CFSP strategic guidelines.
Double-hatted High Representative: The HR for Foreign Affairs and Security
Policy now simultaneously holds: (a) the HR-CFSP post (chairs Foreign Affairs
Council) and (b) Vice-President of the European Commission for External
Relations. Current HR: Kaja Kallas (Estonia). Purpose: ensure coherence between
the Council and Commission tracks of EU external action.

European External Action Service (EEAS): EU diplomatic service established by
Lisbon and operational since 2012. Hybrid body supporting the HR. Runs ~140 EU
delegations worldwide.
Mutual assistance clause (Art. 42(7) TEU): If a member state is the victim of
armed aggression on its territory, other member states have an obligation of aid
and assistance by all means in their power. Weaker than NATO Art. 5 in practice.
Solidarity clause (Art. 222 TFEU): Member states act jointly if one is struck by a

Chapter 7 — Check Your Progress: Answer Guide
This chapter is a study tool, not a summary. For each true/false statement, the
correct answer is given, the reasoning is explained, the relevant chapter is identified,
and an exam-style takeaway is provided. The professor's exam format:
approximately 10 true/false statements (answer + explanation required — no points
for a bare true/false); one essay question.

True/False Statements
Statement 1
"All EU member states are expected to adopt the euro once they meet a number of
convergence criteria."
Answer and Explanation
Answer: Partly TRUE — but with an important exception.
Broadly true: For most EU member states (those with 'derogations'), the
obligation to eventually adopt the euro is real. All states that joined the EU from
2004 onward are legally obligated to adopt the euro once they meet the five
convergence criteria and align their legislation — there is no permanent opt-out
for them.
The key exception: Denmark has a formal opt-out negotiated at Maastricht
(1992). Denmark is NOT legally obligated to adopt the euro, even if it meets all the
convergence criteria. The UK also had an opt-out before Brexit. These are
fundamentally different from derogations.
Sweden nuance: Sweden has a derogation (no opt-out) and is technically
obligated to join when it meets the criteria — but it deliberately avoids meeting
the exchange rate criterion by staying outside the ERM.
Relevant chapter: Chapter 4 — EMU: convergence criteria, opt-outs vs
derogations.
Exam takeaway: Always mention the Denmark opt-out as the exception.
Understand the distinction: opt-out = never obligated; derogation = obligated but
not yet ready.

Statement 2
"The Council of Europe defines the general political direction of the EU."
Answer and Explanation
Answer: FALSE — and it contains a common fatal exam error.
Why false: The Council of Europe is a completely separate international
organisation (46 members, founded 1949, focuses on human rights and
democracy, operates the European Court of Human Rights). It has no role in EU
policy-making and is not an EU institution.
What is correct: The European Council — the EU institution composed of heads
of state or government — defines the general political direction of the EU (Article
15(1) TEU: 'The European Council shall provide the Union with the necessary
impetus for its development and shall define the general directions and priorities
thereof').
Relevant chapter: Chapter 1 — Institutions: European Council vs Council of the
EU vs Council of Europe.
Exam takeaway: This is perhaps the most-tested conceptual confusion in the
course. A complete answer must identify that (a) the Council of Europe is a
separate organisation unrelated to the EU, and (b) it is the European Council
(heads of government) that defines political direction — and the European Council
cannot adopt legislative acts.

Statement 3
"Within the EMU there is one single institution responsible for economic policy."
Answer and Explanation
Answer: FALSE.
What is true about monetary policy: There IS one single supranational
institution responsible for monetary policy in the eurozone: the European Central
Bank (ECB). The ECB independently sets interest rates, manages the money
supply, and pursues price stability. Monetary policy is an exclusive EU
competence for eurozone members.
Why it is false about economic policy: There is no single supranational
institution responsible for economic policy. Economic policy — fiscal policy, tax
policy, structural reforms, labour market policy — remains primarily a national
responsibility. The EU's role is coordination (European Semester, SGP, Excessive
Deficit Procedure, country-specific recommendations). There is no 'EU Economic
Ministry' equivalent to the ECB.
The asymmetry: This is the defining structural feature of the EMU: fully
supranational monetary policy but only coordinated economic policy. One
interest rate for 21 diverse economies, with no equivalent coordinating authority
for fiscal policy.

Relevant chapter: Chapter 4 — EMU: ECB, SGP, asymmetric EMU.
Exam takeaway: The word 'economic' makes this statement false. If the
statement said 'monetary policy', it would be true. Be precise about the
monetary/economic distinction — it is the central architectural feature of the
EMU.

Statement 4
"Customs duties on goods imported from outside the EU are the biggest source of
revenue for the EU budget."
Answer and Explanation
Answer: FALSE.
What the biggest source actually is: The GNI-based contribution (each member
state pays approximately 0.7% of its gross national income) is by far the largest
source of EU revenue, representing approximately 65% of the total. This is
essentially a proportional membership fee.
Why customs duties are not the biggest: Customs duties and agricultural levies
(traditional own resources) represent only approximately 14% of EU revenues.
VAT-based contributions (~16%) are also larger than customs duties.
All four revenue sources in size order: (1) GNI-based contributions ~65%; (2)
VAT-based contributions ~16%; (3) Customs duties and agricultural levies ~14%;
(4) Plastic own resource ~4.8%; (5) Other (fines, etc.).
Relevant chapter: Chapter 2 — EU Budget: revenue sources.
Exam takeaway: Customs duties do contribute to the EU budget, but they are not
dominant. Knowing the approximate shares of the four main revenue sources is
exam material.

Statement 5
"A planned merger between two companies based in the EU must be notified in
advance to the European Commission."
Answer and Explanation
Answer: Partly TRUE — but requires important qualification.
What is true: There is a compulsory pre-merger notification system: all mergers
and acquisitions with a 'Community dimension' must be notified to the
Commission before completion.
The critical qualification: The obligation to notify does not depend on where the
companies are based. It depends on the Community dimension thresholds: (1)
combined worldwide turnover of all parties > €5 billion; AND (2) at least two
companies each have EU-wide turnover > €250 million. A small merger between

two EU companies below these thresholds does NOT go to the Commission — it
may go to national competition authorities.
Additional nuance: Even non-EU companies (e.g. two American companies
merging) must notify if they meet the EU thresholds. Location of headquarters is
irrelevant to the notification trigger.
Relevant chapter: Chapter 5 — Competition Policy: merger control.
Exam takeaway: The notification obligation depends on size (Community
dimension thresholds), not on EU location. Only large mergers go to the
Commission. Smaller ones go to national authorities.

Statement 6
"The European Citizens' Initiative is a European Union mechanism aimed at
increasing direct democracy by giving EU citizens the right to make formal proposals
for EU legislation."
Answer and Explanation
Answer: Mostly TRUE — but one word is technically incorrect: 'proposals'.
What is correct: The ECI is indeed a direct democracy mechanism introduced by
the Treaty of Lisbon. It is aimed at giving EU citizens a direct channel to EU policymaking. The conditions: 7 citizens from at least 7 member states; 1 million
signatures from at least 7 member states with national thresholds. The result: the
Commission must consider the initiative and respond publicly.
The technical inaccuracy: Citizens do not have the right to 'make formal
proposals for EU legislation'. The right of legislative initiative belongs exclusively
to the European Commission. The ECI gives citizens the right to invite (petition)
the Commission to consider making a proposal. The Commission must respond
but is not obligated to propose legislation.
In practice: 127 initiatives registered; 11 have met the signature threshold. None
has directly forced new legislation.
Relevant chapter: Chapter 2 — Policy-making: ECI and Commission's exclusive
right of initiative.
Exam takeaway: The distinction 'right to propose' vs 'right to invite the
Commission to propose' is the key precision. The Commission retains its
exclusive initiative.

Statement 7
"A directive is binding in its entirety."
Answer and Explanation
Answer: FALSE. This statement describes a regulation, not a directive.
A regulation: Is binding in its entirety AND directly applicable. It becomes part of
national law automatically on the date it enters into force, without any national
implementing legislation. It overrides conflicting national rules.
A directive: Is binding only as to the result to be achieved — member states must
achieve the stated objective but choose the form and methods. They have a
deadline (usually 2 years) to transpose the directive into national law. The same
directive can look different in different member states, as long as the required
result is achieved.
A decision: Is binding in its entirety — but only on those specifically addressed (a
member state, a company, or an individual).
Relevant chapter: Chapter 2 — Types of EU legal acts: Article 288 TFEU.
Exam takeaway: Know all three binding acts. The key distinction: regulation
(binding entirely, directly applicable, general); directive (binding as to result,
national implementation required, general); decision (binding entirely, specific
addressee).

Essay Question: Has the Lisbon Treaty Strengthened Democracy in
the EU?
This question requires a structured, balanced argument. The professor signals that
this is a genuine analytical question — not simply a list of Lisbon innovations.
Democracy in the EU context means: to what extent are citizens involved in — and
can they influence — EU decision-making and policy-making? The answer should
have a clear thesis, develop arguments for and against, and reach a reasoned
conclusion.
Model Answer Structure
Thesis (1–2 sentences): The Lisbon Treaty strengthened democracy in the EU in
real and measurable ways — particularly through the extension of the ordinary
legislative procedure, the European Citizens' Initiative, and the legally binding
Charter of Fundamental Rights — but it did not resolve all democratic deficits,
especially the limited accountability of the European Council and the Parliament's
marginal role in the CFSP.
Argument 1 — OLP extension: Lisbon made the ordinary legislative procedure
the standard for almost all EU policy areas. Since the OLP requires the directly
elected Parliament to co-approve all legislation, no EU law can be adopted
without the consent of citizens' elected representatives. Before Lisbon, major

Multinationals and European Institutions – Compact Exam Handbook

areas (agriculture, immigration, JHA) excluded the Parliament. This is the most
concrete democratic gain.
Argument 2 — ECI: For the first time, citizens can formally invite the Commission
to propose legislation by gathering one million signatures across at least seven
member states. This is a form of direct democracy — citizens have a direct
channel to EU policy-making, not mediated solely through national governments
or MEPs.
Argument 3 — Fundamental Rights Charter: The Charter of Fundamental
Rights has the same legal value as the treaties. Citizens' rights against EU
institutions and against national authorities when implementing EU law are now
legally enforceable before the CJEU and national courts. This is a democratic
achievement: subjecting public power to enforceable rights.
Argument 4 — National parliaments: The yellow card mechanism gives national
parliaments a formal role in EU law-making through subsidiarity review. This
adds a second democratic layer beyond the European Parliament — linking EU
legislation to nationally elected legislatures.
Counterargument 1 — European Council: The most powerful political decisions
are taken in the European Council, but this institution is the least democratically
accountable: the Parliament cannot censure it, cannot amend its Conclusions,
and has only a reporting relationship with its President. The biggest decisions
(budget framework, institutional appointments, strategic direction, CFSP) are
largely beyond parliamentary control.
Counterargument 2 — CFSP remains intergovernmental: Foreign policy — an
increasingly important domain given Ukraine, China, Middle East — remains
purely intergovernmental. The Parliament has almost no role; unanimity gives
each member state a veto. Citizens have no direct democratic input into CFSP
decisions. This is a structural gap in EU democratic governance.
Counterargument 3 — ECI in practice: The ECI gives citizens the right to petition
the Commission, not to make legislative proposals. The Commission can decline
to act without legal consequence. In practice, no ECI has directly produced
legislation. The democratic impact has been limited.
Conclusion: Overall: yes, the Lisbon Treaty strengthened EU democracy — the
OLP extension alone is a substantial and durable democratic gain. But the
democratic deficit persists in the European Council (no meaningful parliamentary
oversight) and the CFSP (intergovernmental; no Parliament role). The EU's
democracy is increasingly robust at the legislative level but remains weak at the
strategic political level.

## Glossary of Essential Terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Acquis | The full body of EU law and obligations that candidate states must adopt and implement. |
| Common Commercial Policy | EU trade policy, an exclusive EU competence. |
| COREPER | Committee of Permanent Representatives preparing Council decisions. |
| Direct applicability | An EU act applies without needing national transposition, typical of regulations. |
| European Semester | Annual cycle of economic and fiscal policy coordination. |
| Exclusive competence | Only the EU may legislate and adopt binding acts. |
| Intergovernmental | Decision-making dominated by Member States and national governments. |
| Mutual recognition | A product lawfully marketed in one Member State should generally be accepted in another. |
| Ordinary legislative procedure | Main EU law-making procedure requiring agreement between Parliament and Council. |
| Qualified majority voting | Council voting rule requiring 55% of Member States representing 65% of the EU population. |
| Shared competence | Both EU and Member States can act, but Member States act where the EU has not acted. |
| Subsidiarity | EU action in shared areas only when objectives cannot be better achieved by Member States. |
| Supranational | Decision-making where powers are pooled or transferred to common EU institutions. |
| Treaty of Lisbon | Reform treaty creating current TEU/TFEU framework and abolishing the formal pillar structure. |
