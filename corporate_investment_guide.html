🧠 Psychological Biases — The Hidden Enemy

Your textbook notes that bad investments are often not the result of bad data — they are the result of human psychology overriding rational analysis. Gu & Lev (2011) found that managers of overvalued firms tend to make ill-advised acquisitions to "justify" the high share price.

Overconfidence / Hubris
"Our team can make any deal work." CEOs systematically overestimate their ability to create value.
Anchoring
Fixating on an early number (e.g., the asking price) even when new data says it's too high.
Mental Accounting
Treating money differently based on its source — e.g., spending "windfall" profits recklessly.
Loss Aversion
Holding onto failing projects too long to avoid "booking" a loss (sunk-cost fallacy).
Confirmation Bias
Seeking information that confirms the deal is good; ignoring red flags.
Herd Behavior
"Everyone in our industry is doing acquisitions, so we should too." Groupthink in the boardroom.
Hindsight Bias
After a failure: "We should have known." This distorts learning for future decisions.
Gambler's Fallacy
"Our last three investments failed — this one is due to succeed." Past outcomes don't change probability.
✅
The solution: Use rigorous, objective decision tools — particularly NPV — to anchor decisions in numbers rather than intuition.

📏 Rules of Thumb — Simple but Flawed

Before exploring the gold-standard NPV, it helps to understand the simpler tools that many companies still use — and why each one falls short.

Rule 1 — Book Rate of Return (BRR)

This measures a project's annual profit as a percentage of the book (accounting) value of the asset.

Formula
Book Rate of Return = Annual Profit from Project ÷ Book Value of Asset
Simple Example

A machine costs $100,000 (book value). It generates $12,000 net profit per year.

BRR = $12,000 / $100,000 = 12%

Compare to a "hurdle rate" — if 12% > hurdle, accept. But the hurdle is arbitrary!

❌
Problem: Book value is distorted by whichever depreciation method is chosen. The same physical asset can show very different book values depending on accounting policy — meaning the BRR number is not reliable.

Rule 2 — Break-Even Analysis

This tells you how many units you must sell (or how much revenue you must generate) before you cover all your costs — i.e., before you stop losing money.

Formulas
Unit Contribution Margin = Price per Unit − Variable Cost per Unit

Break-Even Units = Fixed Costs ÷ Unit Contribution Margin
Step-by-step Example

A company sells a product at €50/unit. Variable costs = €30/unit. Fixed costs = €200,000/year.

  • Contribution margin = €50 − €30 = €20 per unit
  • Break-even = €200,000 ÷ €20 = 10,000 units
  • If you can realistically sell >10,000 units, proceed. If not, rethink the project.
⚠️
Problem: Break-even only tells you when you stop making a loss — it says nothing about how much value you create. A project that just breaks even creates zero value for shareholders. It also ignores the time value of money.

Rule 3 & 4 — Payback Period & Discounted Payback Period

The payback period counts how many years it takes for cumulative cash inflows to equal the initial investment.

Concept
Keep adding up cash flows year by year until they equal the initial outlay → that's the payback period
ProjectC₀C₁C₂C₃Payback Period

*Project B has a huge payout in year 3 that simple payback essentially ignores!

The discounted payback period is the same idea but uses present values of each cash flow (at a given discount rate, e.g. 5%).

❌
Problems with both:
  • Neither focuses on value creation — a fast payback project can still destroy value.
  • Regular payback ignores time value of money entirely.
  • Both ignore cash flows that occur after payback (Project B's $5,000 in year 3!).
  • The "desired payback period" cutoff is arbitrary — where does it come from?

💎 NPV — The Value-Creating Rule

NPV stands for Net Present Value. It is the single most important concept in corporate finance. It directly measures how much financial value a project creates for the firm.

The Core Idea in Plain English

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow — because you can invest today's dollar and earn a return. NPV takes all future cash flows of a project, adjusts each one for how far in the future it is (by discounting at the company's cost of capital), and adds them all up. If the total is positive, the project creates value. If negative, it destroys value.

The NPV Formula
NPV = −C₀ + C₁/(1+r)¹ + C₂/(1+r)² + C₃/(1+r)³ + … + Cₙ/(1+r)ⁿ

Where: C₀ = upfront investment (negative), Cₜ = cash flow in year t, r = cost of capital (discount rate), n = project lifespan

Three Ingredients You Need

  • Forecast all future cash flows — revenues minus costs, year by year (incremental flows only)
  • Choose the right discount rate — the company's weighted average cost of capital (WACC), reflecting investor expectations
  • Calculate and compare to zero — NPV > 0 → accept; NPV < 0 → reject

NPV Example from the Textbook

ProjectC₀C₁C₂C₃NPV @ 10%Decision
A–2,0001,0001,0001,000 +487✅ Accept
B–2,0005005005,000 +2,847✅ Accept
C–2,0005001,8000 –200❌ Reject
D–2,0001,8005000 –115❌ Reject

Notice: Project B has the longest payback period but by far the highest NPV. This is why payback rules miss the big picture.

✅
NPV Rule: Accept all projects with NPV > 0 (if projects are independent). If projects are mutually exclusive, choose the one with the highest NPV.

📈 IRR — Internal Rate of Return

The IRR is the discount rate that makes a project's NPV exactly equal to zero. In other words, it's the rate of return the project itself earns. The rule: accept if IRR > cost of capital (r).

IRR Definition
Find IRR such that: 0 = −C₀ + C₁/(1+IRR)¹ + C₂/(1+IRR)² + … + Cₙ/(1+IRR)ⁿ

Textbook Example

Turbo Machine Tool — IRR Calculation

Purchase price: $4,000. Cash flows: Year 1 = $2,000, Year 2 = $4,000.

Solve for IRR in:  0 = −4,000 + 2,000/(1+IRR) + 4,000/(1+IRR)²

IRR = 28.08%

If the company's cost of capital is, say, 10% → IRR (28%) > r (10%) → Accept the project.

When IRR and NPV Agree

For a standard project (one upfront outflow, then positive inflows), IRR and NPV always agree:

NPV > 0
⟺
IRR > r

When IRR Breaks Down ⚠️

IRR can give misleading or impossible results in these situations:

SituationProjectC₀C₁IRRNPV@10%Problem
Lending vs. Borrowing A (lending)–1,000+1,500 50%+364 Both have IRR = 50% but A is great (you earn 50%), B is terrible (you pay 50%!) — NPV correctly distinguishes them.
Lending vs. Borrowing B (borrowing)+1,000–1,500 50%–364
No / Multiple IRRs C–1,000+3,000 None+339 Cash flow reversal can produce 0 or 2+ IRRs — the rule becomes meaningless.
Different Life-spans F vs GProjects with unequal lives 33% vs 20% $3,592 vs $9,000 Higher IRR ≠ more value. G creates far more total value despite lower IRR.
⚠️
Takeaway: IRR is intuitive and easy to communicate ("our project returns 28%"), but use NPV to make the final call whenever cash flows are irregular, projects have different lifespans, or when choosing between mutually exclusive projects.

🏦 Capital Rationing & WAPI

In a perfect world, a company would fund every project with a positive NPV. In practice, capital is often limited — you have a fixed budget and must choose which combination of projects to fund.

The Problem with Simply Ranking by NPV

If your budget is $300,000 and Project A needs $200,000 (NPV $230k) while Project B needs $125,000 (NPV $141k), taking A and B together costs $325,000 — over budget. You need a smarter allocation rule.

Step 1 — Profitability Index (PI)

Profitability Index
PI = NPV ÷ Investment Required

PI measures how much value you get per dollar invested. Higher is better when capital is scarce.

Step 2 — WAPI (Weighted Average Profitability Index)

WAPI Formula
WAPI = Σ [ PIᵢ × (Investmentᵢ ÷ Total Budget) ]

Choose the combination of projects that maximises the WAPI — this gives you the most total NPV per dollar of budget used.

Worked Example (from textbook)

Budget = $300,000. Available projects:

ProjectInvestmentNPVPI = NPV/Inv
A$200,000$230,0001.15
B$125,000$141,2501.13
C$175,000$194,2501.11
D$150,000$162,0001.08
Which combination wins?

Option 1: A + B → Cost = $325k ❌ (over budget)

Option 2: A + D → Cost = $350k ❌ (over budget)

Option 3: B + C → Cost = $300k ✅ → Total NPV = $141,250 + $194,250 = $335,500

Option 4: B + D → Cost = $275k ✅ → Total NPV = $141,250 + $162,000 = $303,250

Option 5: C + D → Cost = $325k ❌ (over budget)

WAPI for B+C = 1.13×(125/300) + 1.11×(175/300) = 0.471 + 0.648 = 1.119

Best choice: B + C — highest WAPI and fits within budget.

🔄 Equivalent Annual Cash Flow (EAC)

How do you compare two projects (or machines) that have different lifespans? A 2-year machine vs. a 3-year machine can't be compared directly by NPV — the 3-year machine has more time to generate value.

The Solution: Convert NPV into an Annual Annuity

The EAC converts a project's total NPV into an equivalent fixed annual amount. This puts projects with different lifespans on the same footing.

EAC Formula
EAC = NPV ÷ Annuity Factor(t, r)

where Annuity Factor(t, r) = [1 − (1+r)⁻ᵗ] ÷ r

Once you have the EAC for each project, choose the project with the higher EAC (for revenue projects) or lower EAC cost (for cost-minimization projects like choosing between machines).

Textbook Machine Example (cost of capital = 6%)

MachineYear 0Year 1Year 2Year 3NPV @6%Annuity FactorEAC
A –15–5–5–5 –28.372.673–10.62/yr
B –10–6–6— –21.001.833–11.46/yr
✅
Decision: Machine A costs –$10.62/year in equivalent annual terms vs. Machine B's –$11.46/year. Choose Machine A — it is cheaper on an annualised basis despite a higher upfront cost.
💡
Why not just compare NPVs? Machine A's NPV looks worse (–28.37 vs –21.00) but it lasts 3 years vs 2 years for B. Direct NPV comparison would unfairly penalise A. EAC corrects this.
CH6 — Corporate Investments in Practice

📊 Building a Project's Free Cash Flows

NPV only works if you feed it the right cash flows. In practice this means constructing a bottom-up model from a projected Profit & Loss statement and adjusting carefully.

The Master Formula

Project Free Cash Flow (FCF)
FCF = Operational Cash Flow − ΔNWC − CAPEX

Where ΔNWC = change in net working capital, CAPEX = capital expenditure

How to Get Operational Cash Flow from a P&L

+Sales Revenue
−Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
−Other Operating Costs
−Taxes on Operating Profit
=Operational Cash Flow (OCF)
−Change in Net Working Capital (ΔNWC)
−Capital Investment (CAPEX)
+Salvage Value (end of project)
= FREE CASH FLOW

Note: Depreciation does NOT appear here. It's a non-cash item. Its impact is felt only through the tax shield.

What to Include — and What to Exclude

ItemInclude?Why
Incremental revenues & costs✅ YesThese are directly caused by the project
Working capital changes (ΔNWC)✅ YesCash tied up in inventory, receivables etc. is a real outflow
Opportunity costs✅ YesE.g. a building you could rent out but instead use for the project
Salvage / terminal value✅ YesCash recovered when the project ends and assets are sold
Sunk costs❌ NoAlready spent; they don't change based on your decision today
Allocated overhead❌ NoCentral costs that exist regardless of this project
Depreciation (direct)❌ NoNon-cash; only its tax impact (tax shield) matters
Interest payments❌ NoCaptured in the discount rate (WACC) — don't double count

The Depreciation Tax Shield — Why Your Depreciation Schedule Matters

Even though depreciation is non-cash, higher depreciation → lower taxable profit → lower taxes → more cash left over. This is the "depreciation tax shield."

Depreciation Tax Shield
Tax Shield = Tax Rate × Annual Depreciation Charge

The US tax system (and many others) allows accelerated depreciation — you write off more in early years. Front-loading depreciation pushes the tax savings earlier, and since earlier cash flows are worth more (TVM), the NPV improves.

💡
MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System) in the US allows 5-year, 7-year, etc. schedules with higher depreciation percentages in year 1-2. Choosing the fastest allowed schedule legally increases a project's NPV.

🐄 Case Study 2 — UPP Cattle Food Project

UPP wants to launch a new line of cattle food. This case is a rich exercise in identifying all incremental cash flows — including opportunity costs and working capital dynamics.

Project Parameters

ItemDetail
Building (opportunity cost)Currently rented out; rent = $100k/yr growing at 4%/yr inflation → must be included as an opportunity cost
CAPEX$1,200,000 production line, straight-line depreciation over 10 years
Project lifespan8 years (terminated before asset is fully depreciated)
Terminal / Salvage valueInfrastructure resold at $400,000 at end of year 8
Initial Working Capital$350,000 upfront injection
Ongoing Working Capital10% of annual sales (varies each year)
Year 1 Sales$4,200,000, growing 5%/year
Direct Production Costs90% of sales
Tax Rate25%
Cost of Capital12%

Key Learning Points from This Case

  • Opportunity cost is real: The foregone rent ($100k growing at 4%) is NOT in the accounting records but must be in your FCF model. If you use the building, you give up real cash.
  • Working capital is dynamic: It starts at $350k but then tracks 10% of sales — so as sales grow, so does WC. Each year-on-year change in WC is a cash flow. At year 8, all WC is recovered.
  • Mid-project asset disposal: The depreciation schedule runs 10 years but the project ends at 8. The book value at year 8 = $1,200k − 8×$120k = $240k. If sold for $400k, the gain = $160k taxable at 25% → after-tax salvage = $400k − 25%×$160k = $360k.
  • Tax payments are based on accounting profit (including depreciation), not on cash profit.
💡
General Approach: Build a P&L → compute tax → reconstruct OCF by adding back depreciation (non-cash) → subtract ΔNWC and CAPEX → discount at 12% → if NPV > 0, invest.

⚙️ Case Study 3 — Make-or-Buy Decision

A machine manufacturer currently buys 200,000 supply parts at $2/piece from an external supplier. They're considering producing them in-house at $1.5/piece plus a $150k capital investment.

The Incremental Analysis Framework

A make-or-buy decision is analysed by looking at the incremental cash flows of switching from buy to make. What changes?

ItemCurrent (Buy)Alternative (Make)Incremental Change
Annual supply cost200k × $2 = $400k200k × $1.5 = $300kSave $100k/yr
Capital Investment$0$150,000–$150,000 upfront
Working Capital$0+$30,000–$30,000 (recovered at end)
Tax impact of depreciation$0Depends on schemeTax shield on depreciation

How Depreciation Scheme Changes the Answer

The problem asks you to compare three depreciation treatments for the $150k investment:

Immediate Full Write-off
BEST NPV

Entire $150k written off in Year 0 → tax refund immediately → maximum front-loading of tax shield.

Linear (10-yr)
MEDIUM NPV

$15k/yr depreciation → steady small tax shield across 10 years.

7-Year MACRS
MEDIUM NPV

Front-loaded but less extreme than immediate expensing. NPV lies between the two above.

Worked NPV Sketch (Immediate Write-off, r = 15%, tax = 21%)

  • Year 0 cash: CAPEX –$150k + WC –$30k + Tax refund from immediate depreciation: +21% × $150k = +$31.5k → Net Year 0 = –$148.5k
  • Annual savings: $100k/yr. Tax on savings: 21% × $100k = $21k. After-tax savings = $79k/yr for 10 years.
  • Year 10 recovery: +$30k WC returned. After-tax = +$30k (no tax on WC recovery).
  • NPV = –$148.5k + $79k × AF(10, 15%) + $30k/(1.15)¹⁰ ≈ –$148.5k + $79k×5.019 + $7.4k ≈ +$255.4k → MAKE!
✅
Under all depreciation schemes, making in-house has a positive NPV — the company should MAKE the supplies itself. The immediate write-off scheme maximises the NPV due to the upfront tax shield.

The FCF Construction Checklist

✅ Always Include

  • Incremental revenues & costs
  • Taxes on operating profit
  • Change in working capital (ΔNWC)
  • Capital expenditure (CAPEX)
  • Salvage / terminal value
  • Opportunity costs (foregone income)

❌ Never Include

  • Sunk costs (already spent)
  • Allocated overhead (not incremental)
  • Depreciation (non-cash — only tax shield matters)
  • Interest payments (in WACC already)
