What this chapter covers

Chapter 1 introduced exchange as one of three ways to coordinate an economy (alongside reciprocity and redistribution). Exchange is the backbone of the market — and when it grows into the dominant coordination mechanism of a whole economic system (a “market society”), some call the result capitalism. This chapter asks what capitalism actually is, how it arose, and whether there is one capitalism or several.

The one-sentence version

Capitalism is not a moral verdict or “greed,” but coordination through profit-oriented market exchange in a system that is endogenous yet constructed (states built the markets). It arose in a particular time and place — the debate is how uniquely Western — it tends to expand into ever more of social life (Habermas's “colonization of the life world”), and it comes in varieties (Weber's four types; today's liberal vs. coordinated economies).

Orientation

0.From exchange to “market society”

This chapter builds directly on the last one. Recall the three ideal-typical coordination mechanisms: exchange, reciprocity, redistribution. Exchange — transactions in a market — is the essential backbone of the market.

The move this chapter makes

When market exchange stops being just one mechanism among three and becomes the dominant coordination mechanism of an entire economic system — a market society — some observers call that capitalism. The rest of the chapter is about pinning down what that word should (and shouldn't) mean, where the system came from, and how many kinds of it there are.

Section One

1.What capitalism is (and is not)

“Capitalism” carries heavy emotional baggage in everyday life that differs from a careful sociological definition. That baggage comes in two connected forms — a pejorative tone and an equation with greed — and clearing both away is the first job.

What capitalism is not

1 · A term of abuse (the pejorative connotation)

In daily speech “capitalism” is often a negative verdict — the word of choice for Marxists and the anti-globalisation movement, set against a supposedly neutral “free market.” But this is unstable: neoliberals happily use “capitalism” as a positive badge too. Because the same word swings between insult and compliment, social science must treat it as value-free as possible — a category of analysis, not a cheer or a boo.

2 · Greed

A second popular image: capitalism as a system where greed dominates all social relations. Oliver Stone's Wall Street films made stockbroking and corporate raiding into icons of capitalist greed — and total submission to it. Some defenders of capitalism even argue for the ethical value of greed, reinforcing the impression.

“Greed is good.” (1987)  →  “Now it seems it's legal.” (2010)— Gordon Gekko, Wall Street & Wall Street: Money Never Sleeps
Why “greed” fails as a definition

Greed is far too common to pick capitalism out: it appears in plenty of pre-, non- and quasi-capitalist systems (classical Rome, the Middle Ages). What is distinctively capitalist is not greed itself but its own regulation of the legitimacy of greed — which wealth gets honoured.

  • Under capitalism: more prestige for wealth won through market transactions (profit); less for merely inherited wealth.
  • Pre-capitalist societies: the inverse status ordering — inherited, landed wealth ranks highest, “trade” is faintly vulgar. Think of the landed gentry of 19th-century England (Jane Austen's world), where living off rents outranks earning a profit.

What capitalism is

So if it's neither a swear-word nor greed, what is it? The sociological definition has two distinguishing features:

Definition
Capitalism is 1 an endogenous but constructed system of 2 coordination through exchange on the market with durable profit acquisition as the central motivator.

We take the two elements in reverse order — the second (profit-oriented exchange) first, because it is the more concrete.

Element 2 · Market exchange oriented toward profit

This is Polanyi's exchange type (as opposed to reciprocity and redistribution) — but now specifically harnessed to the durable pursuit of profit. Two things travel with it as logical (and probably necessary) conditions:

i
Protected property rights
Property is protected by an authority — the state — whose enforcement makes using your own means of force redundant. You don't need private muscle to hold what's yours; law and the state do it. This is what makes calm, large-scale market exchange possible.
ii
Profit-seeking firms
Companies that systematically pursue profit. Note the careful wording: the goal is profit — but that goal is not necessarily achieved. Capitalism is defined by the orientation, not by guaranteed success.

Element 1 · An endogenous but constructed system

The subtler feature — and a genuine paradox, captured by the image of the Sorcerer's Apprentice (a force conjured up by people that then runs beyond their control). Capitalism is both at once:

Endogenous — its own runaway dynamicConstructed — made and maintained by actors
A complex system with a dynamic of its own that exceeds the understanding of its actors (cf. Marx). No one is fully “driving”; the system has emergent momentum.Yet its emergence and reproduction depend on deliberate intervention by actors — especially political and intellectual elites.
The key corrective

Capitalism did not arise from simply “leaving markets alone.” It depended on the intentional intervention of (early) states in the construction of markets — coining money, enforcing contracts, chartering companies, standardising weights. Laissez-faire is best understood not as capitalism's natural condition but as its ideological base — a story the system tells about itself. Markets were made, not found.

Section Two

2.Genesis & dynamics of capitalism

Two propositions describe how capitalist economic organisation expands — one across space, one within society:

  1. The thesis of unicity — capitalism developed in the West and spread outward geographically to the rest of the world (§2.1).
  2. The thesis of the colonization of the life world — the logic of capitalism reaches into ever more areas of social life than just the economy (§2.2).

2.1  The West and the rest?

The classical thesis of unicity holds that capitalism developed in the West (Europe) — and only there. Yet this sits awkwardly with an inconvenient fact: technologically, the West actually lagged behind India, the Middle East and China until roughly the 16th–17th century. Taking China as the case, the debate becomes: did China also develop (elements of) capitalism?

Two positions
PositionClaim
Thesis of unicityNo — only the West developed capitalism.
RevisionismYes — the emergence was multipolar; China had capitalist elements too.
Consensus · China's head start (until the Ming, late 13th c.)

Both sides agree China led for a long time. Elements of that lead:

  • Printing — 9th century (the West: only the 15th!)
  • Gunpowder
  • Compass and mechanical clocks
  • Admiral Zheng He's naval expeditions (1405–33): seven voyages, large-scale, far-reaching and effective — hundreds of ships and crews running into the tens of thousands.

The unicity reading: from then on China stood still, for essentially cultural reasons (a self-satisfied “triumphalism,” a turn inward), and the West took over — using gunpowder, via further innovation, to build the military and hence trade supremacy that carried capitalism. Revisionism attacks this on two fronts:

Revisionist argument 1 · The gap is much more recent

The divergence only really opened after 1800. Until Western industrialisation, China kept pace with the West in marketisation and monetisation. What China lacked came late and specific: mechanisation and central workplaces — the factory, and the industrialisation it drove.

Revisionist argument 2 · Ecological, not cultural, causes — the “high-level equilibrium trap”

The relative standstill wasn't about culture but about a disadvantageous population-to-land ratio. The causal chain:

Fast population growth (demographic factors)
→
Over-supply of cheap labour  +  comparatively expensive capital
→
Labour-saving innovation less likely & more costly → relative standstill

When labour is dirt cheap and capital dear, there is little incentive to invent machines that save labour. China was, in effect, trapped at a high level of pre-industrial development — efficient, but with the wrong price signals to industrialise. Not a cultural failing; an ecological-economic bind.

2.2  Colonization of the life world

“Expansion” in §2.1 meant a geographical variable — how much territory and how many people are drawn in. But expansion can also happen within a single society, as the market logic reaches into domains that used to lie outside it. Two underlying ideas:

  1. Incorporating ever more areas of society into capitalist logic — e.g. the commercialisation of reproduction (commercial surrogacy; the case of Gujarat, India).
  2. Markets emerging out of markets — e.g. financial markets in stocks and derivatives (markets built on top of other markets).
Case · commercial surrogacy in Anand, India

A clinic in Anand lets poor rural women “rent” themselves as surrogate mothers. Middle-class prospective parents (Indian and foreign) effectively “order” a baby; usually the biological father is the male partner, sometimes both partners contribute (via IVF). The surrogates remain in the clinic for the whole pregnancy, and the baby is handed over immediately at birth. It is an extreme — and probably growing — case of the commercialisation of intimate life.

Jürgen Habermas names this kind of expansion the colonization of the life world. His framework rests on a distinction between two types of social action, and two corresponding spheres:

Communicative action
The Life World
  • Action aimed at mutual understanding, not an external goal
  • Private life and the community as civitas (politics in the pure sense)
  • Produces identity, belonging, community — the symbolic existence of humans
  • Governed by truth & truthfulness
Strategic (instrumental-rational) action
The System
  • Action aimed at an external goal (convince, sell)
  • Material existence — including the economy
  • Governed by efficiency & effectiveness, not truth
  • Efficiency typically expressed through monetised currency
COLONIZATION → the SYSTEM's money/efficiency criteria invade and re-value the LIFE WORLD

The problem arises when relationships and values that used to belong to the life world get valued according to system criteria (money, efficiency). That intrusion is what Habermas calls colonization — the system colonising the life world.

Is the image valid? Two caveats
  1. It's not a one-way, generalised march. Some things that were commodities before capitalism are strongly tabooed in capitalist societies — slaves, acquiring partners through dowries, buying government positions. In these areas the market actually retreated.
  2. The life world has always expressed value in economic terms too — the engagement ring, gifts. Money and intimacy were never hermetically sealed. So the boundary shifts both ways, and was never pure.
Section Three

3.Capitalism or capitalisms?

Is capitalism one thing, or should we distinguish several types? This has always been debated. We look at two typologies — a classical one (Max Weber) and a recent one (“Varieties of Capitalism”).

3.1  Weber's typology — four types

Weber distinguished four types, with a certain evolutionary logic running toward the fourth as the most mature form — a historical approach.

Weber's four types of capitalism (1 → 4: increasing maturity)
TypeWhat it is
1 · TraditionalConcentrated on trade and a limited amount of banking (e.g. credit). Think of pockets of capitalist life in medieval towns — islands of market functioning in a sea of self-sufficient (autarkic) agricultural communities.
2 · PoliticalPolitical events and processes open access to large, often one-shot profit opportunities. Cases: colonialism, monopolies granted by the king.
3 · AdventurerSwift, random, exceptional profit opportunities. Type 2 is a subset of this. Pure forms: plundering/predatory economies — extortion, piracy.
4 · RationalThe type set out in “what capitalism is” — the most mature form (durable, calculated profit through market exchange). In practice it often still mixes with elements of types 1–3.

3.2  Varieties of capitalism — liberal vs. coordinated

A more recent theory (the “Varieties of Capitalism” school, c. 2001) sets out to compare existing contemporary systems across countries — a comparative rather than historical approach. It rests on three starting points:

  1. Firms and their coordination problems are central — chiefly: how do companies handle problems of asymmetric information and control? Other actors (employees, financiers) face the same. There are two solution styles → see the table.
  2. Institutional complementarities — choosing a solution in one domain makes it more profitable (lower learning/coordination cost) to use the same style in another domain. So the pieces line up into coherent regimes.
  3. Comparative institutional advantages — both regimes survive because each has its own strengths (especially different effects on innovation), preserved over time.
The two ideal-typical regimes
DimensionLiberal market economyCoordinated market economy
Coordination styleOutsourcing to the market, flexiblyMutual long-term agreements & cooperation
Skills & schooling (example)General, portable skills; hire & fire on the marketFirm/industry-specific skills built through stable relationships
Labour & capital marketsBoth market-oriented & flexible (complementary)Both stable & long-term (complementary)
Typical countriesMainly Anglo-SaxonScandinavian, continental-European, Japan
Comparative advantage in innovationRadical innovation (e.g. the PC, the internet) — via flexible use of human capital & financeIncremental innovation — via stable, long-term relationships in human capital & finance
The pay-off

There isn't a single “best” capitalism converging on one model. The two regimes persist side by side because their institutions are internally consistent (complementarity) and each is genuinely better at something (comparative advantage) — the liberal at radical breakthroughs, the coordinated at steady refinement. “Capitalism” is therefore better written in the plural: capitalisms.

Five things to remember
  1. Capitalism ≠ a swear-word or greed. It's profit-oriented market exchange become the dominant coordination mechanism.
  2. Markets were made, not found: capitalism is endogenous (runaway dynamic) and constructed (states built the markets; laissez-faire is ideology).
  3. Expansion runs two ways: outward geographically (the West-and-the-rest debate) and inward into social life (colonization of the life world).
  4. The West's lead is recent & contingent — China led for centuries; the post-1800 gap owes more to ecology (the high-level equilibrium trap) than culture.
  5. There are capitalisms, plural: Weber's four historical types, and today's liberal (radical innovation) vs. coordinated (incremental innovation) economies.
